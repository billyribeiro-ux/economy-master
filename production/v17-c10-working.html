A decision memo earns its brevity by making the reasoning inspectable. Removing equations from the first paragraph is helpful; removing the assumptions that could reverse the recommendation is not.

Capability and prerequisites. Translate technical findings into a decision for a specified policymaker or board without overstating certainty. Build on research responsibility and independence, distinguishing an identified quantity from a policy target, and the library's present-value and decision-under-uncertainty foundations. Both packages below are fictional instructional cases with stipulated models. They are not current empirical observations, advice to an actual institution, or evidence that any board approved a proposal.

1. Write the decision contract before the summary

An executive memo states a decision, the feasible alternatives, the evidence and assumptions that distinguish them, the recommendation and the next action. A technical appendix lets another analyst reconstruct that reasoning. A trigger is a specified observation, approval or condition that requires review or changes an action. A statement such as “monitor risk” is not a trigger until it says which risk, what threshold, who checks it and what follows.

Ask who can decide what, by when, subject to which constraints. A public committee may care about service access, budget, distribution and accountability. A company board may need incremental value, cash availability, financing commitments and execution responsibility. The analyst should not silently choose the institution's welfare weights or risk tolerance. State the decision rule: maximizing expected value, protecting a minimum cash balance and minimizing worst-case regret are different choices. A table cannot make that normative choice on the reader's behalf.

Lauren Brodsky's Harvard Kennedy School Communications Program guide, How to Write a Policy Memo, recommends a specific problem, attention to the decision maker, comparison of practical options, an early recommendation and revision for clarity. We use those bounded writing principles, not its illustrative policy example or a claim that this format causes better decisions. The first-paragraph test is practical: can a reader identify the decision and its important condition without reading the appendix?

Separate four kinds of statements. An observation records something actually measured. A model implication follows from explicit primitives. A scenario asks what happens if an input takes a stated value. A recommendation combines those inputs with a criterion and constraints. “Under a 75% favorable-state assumption, expected value is positive” is not “there is a 75% chance our estimate is correct.” A parameter range need not be a probability distribution, a forecast interval, a confidence interval or an identified set. Name which it is and why.

Use layered communication: an executive conclusion with the main uncertainty, an option display with units and comparison basis, then an appendix with derivation and limitations. The layers must retain the same target, sign, timing and evidence status. Do not change “modeled benefits” into “cash savings,” “conditional recommendation” into “approval,” or “scenario probability” into “measured likelihood” to make the shorter version more persuasive. If a senior reader asks for a more definite claim than the analysis supports, offer the missing assumption or evidence needed rather than inventing certainty.

2. Displays are part of the argument

A chart title should tell the reader what is compared and under what uncertainty. Label units, time horizon, denominator and whether values are gross or net. Use a common scale for comparable quantities; an axis choice can amplify or compress apparent differences. The accepted ONS axes guidance supports careful baselines and comparable scales. Its uncertainty-display guidance supports clearly annotated ranges. These are bounded design references, not permission to call every shaded band a confidence interval.

For bars that encode magnitude by length, a zero baseline preserves that encoding. A line graph may use a narrower labeled range for changes, but disclose it and avoid using the visual height as an unexplained ratio. Preserve negative values and the decision threshold. Distinguish lines with labels or patterns, not color alone, and provide a text/table equivalent. When the same uncertain parameter drives two options, compare their values at the same parameter value. Subtracting unrelated ends of two separate ranges may exaggerate the uncertainty in their difference.

3. Complete policy communication package

Case 1 — A municipal service expansion with uncertain take-up

Technical brief and decision conditions. A fictional committee chooses one indivisible six-month service option: full expansion A, smaller expansion B, or status quo O. No splitting, policy lottery or adaptive mid-period switch is in this decision menu. Available spending is100 thousand currency units. All costs and benefits below are incremental to O over the same six months; costs are paid from that budget. Benefits value users' time and access in the same monetary units; they are not cash receipts. Equal monetary weights across the two neighborhoods are stipulated, and unmeasured effects are excluded from this small model. The committee must separately decide whether those weights and exclusions are acceptable.

The uncertain take-up index θ lies in the stipulated range (0,1). There is no estimated probability law for θ. Cost/benefit coefficients (cost, benefit intercept, benefit slope) are ((0,0,0),(100,80,40),(70,65,15)) for O,A,B. Thus net values are VO=0, VA=−20+40θ and VB=−5+15θ. Both spending options fit the cash budget, but affordability does not imply positive net benefit. All coefficients and the θ range are assumptions supplied for this exercise, not estimates from a real service trial.

Decision rule and technical appendix. Regret for option j at θ is maxkVk(θ)−Vj(θ): the shortfall relative to the best permitted option if θ were known. The committee's proposed rule is to choose the deterministic option with the smallest maximum regret. For a fixed j, every Vk−Vj is affine. Its maximum on[0,1] is at an endpoint; taking the maximum over the finite k therefore also needs only those endpoints. This proves the endpoint calculation over the entire continuum, not just over the three displayed scenarios.

At θ=0 the best value is0, yielding regrets(O,A,B)=(0,20,5); at θ=1 the best is20, yielding(20,0,10). Hence the worst regrets are (20,20,10), uniquely selecting B in this menu. The lowest net values are (0,-20,-5). A different rule maximizing that lowest net chooses O. Neither criterion is dictated by the data; they express different attitudes toward uncertain opportunity losses and downside. B's maximum regret10 is not its possible monetary loss: its lowest net is−5.

With known θ, B first beats O when θ>1/3, A first beats O when θ>1/2, and A beats B when θ>3/5 because VA−VB=−15+25θ. The optimal-choice transitions are O below1/3, a tie O/B at1/3, B between1/3 and3/5, a tie A/B at3/5, and A above3/5. The three break-even/comparison thresholds are (1/3,1/2,3/5). A's own break-even1/2 is not the switch from B to A. An assumed mean Eθ=1/2 would give expected nets (0,0,5/2), but the midpoint alone does not supply a probability model or a chance of loss.

Full executive memo to the fictional service committee

To: Service Committee. From: Analysis team, instructional specimen. Decision: Select one six-month option at the next budget meeting. Recommendation: Choose the70-unit smaller expansion B if the committee adopts the stated smallest-worst-regret rule and accepts the model's benefit valuations. Its largest modeled shortfall from the best option is10, compared with20 for full expansion and status quo. It can still produce a net loss of5 at the bottom of the stipulated take-up range; it is not a guaranteed saving.

Both expansion options fit the100-unit budget. Full expansion spends100 and has net values from−20to20; B spends70 and ranges from−5to10. These ranges reflect the supplied take-up model, not confidence intervals. B also directs20 more gross benefit units to the peripheral neighborhood than A at every modeled take-up value, though we lack neighborhood cost incidence and excluded-effect estimates. The committee must confirm whether the aggregate valuation and distribution trade-off match its objective.

If the committee instead requires the highest worst-case net outcome, keep the status quo. If credible new evidence restricts every feasible θ to above3/5, reopen the comparison: full expansion then beats both alternatives throughout that narrower range. The planning analyst owns a pre-commitment review of the benefit assumptions, feasible θ range and omitted effects; the finance officer confirms cost70 and budget availability before contracting. Any unsupported narrowing of the range or cost increase goes back to the committee with a revised display. This memo requests a conditional choice; it does not record approval or authorize spending. The appendix gives the exact thresholds and regret proof.

Audience translation with unchanged meaning. A resident-facing paragraph could say: “The smaller option costs70 of the100 available budget units. Under the committee's proposed rule, it limits how far the outcome could fall short of the best choice after demand becomes known. It may still cost more than its valued benefits. Its modeled access benefits are more favorable to the peripheral neighborhood, but not every local cost or impact has been measured. The committee will decide which trade-off to accept.” This removes technical notation while preserving conditionality, units and the possibility of loss. It does not replace a public process or assert that residents prefer the option.

Case 2 — Positive expected value with a cash constraint

Technical brief. A fictional firm has110 cash now, a pre-existing payment of50 at the end of year1, and a required cash floor20 after each modeled date's receipts and payments. The existing payment is the same under every option: include it in the cash ledger, but not again in the incremental project NPV. Choose no investment O, a small project S, or a large project L. No taxes, other cash flows, liquidation, refinancing or changing state probabilities are assumed. All amounts are currency units, not thousands; the different scale from Case1 is deliberately labeled.

There are two states, favorable G and adverse B, with stipulated probability P(G)=3/4. This is a planning assumption, not an estimated forecast. The risk-neutral objective is expected incremental NPV at the chosen common planning discount rate r=1/4, subject to meeting the cash floor in both states. This is a defined decision model, not a claim that discounting expected risky cash flows at an arbitrary rate gives a market price. Raw rows(cost now,year1G,year1B,year2 either state) for L and S are ((90,90,30,50),(60,50,35,35)). The liquidity inputs(opening cash,year1 existing payment,cash floor) are (110,50,20).

A stipulated committed loan offers20 now with repayment125/4=31.25 at year2, no intermediate payment or fees: (20,125/4). It can be used with L; whether such a commitment could actually be obtained is unobserved and must be confirmed before action. The model assumes it remains available in both states. Borrowing is a financing alternative, not extra operating revenue.

Full executive memo to the fictional board

To: Board Investment Committee. From: Finance analysis team, instructional specimen. Decision requested: Conditional authorization of L only after verification of the20-unit committed facility and the stated cash exposures. Under the supplied75% favorable-state assumption and25% planning discount rate, financed L has expected incrementalNPV2 and meets the20 cash floor in both modeled states. Without that facility, L fails the floor in the adverse first year despite the same positive expectedNPV. If the facility cannot be confirmed, retain O under this model; the smaller feasible project has expectedNPV−0.6.

The main downside is explicit: L's adverse-stateNPV is−34, and even with financing first-year adverse cash is exactly20. Our positive mean does not imply a positive outcome in each state. The two-state schedule omits tax, execution and other cash shocks, so approval should not rest on the expected value alone. The year1 pre-existing50 payment is already included in the cash display and is not deducted twice from incremental project value.

The treasurer must verify a binding20 receipt at date0 and31.25 repayment at year2, with no unmodeled fees, early calls or state-contingent withdrawal. The operating lead must review whether30 is a defensible adverse year1 receipt and whether the year2 receipt50 and common obligation50 are complete. The finance lead returns an updated model before spending if favorable probability is no longer defensibly above17/24, if adverse receipt falls below30, if a required cash floor rises, or if financing terms change. These are review conditions, not claims that the probability or floor has been empirically certified.

Authorization remains a board decision, and the proposed conditions are not satisfied merely because this memo lists them. If the board accepts the stated risk-neutral objective, statewise cash protection and input assumptions, financed L ranks above the other supplied feasible options. The appendix makes both the value and liquidity arguments reproducible; the board should record any different risk objective or additional scenario rather than asking for a stronger headline from unchanged evidence.

Unchanged message for an operating audience. “The large project is the preferred modeled choice only with the committed funding and the stated cash conditions. At the adverse first-year receipt30, cash after the existing payment is exactly the minimum20, so any additional outflow or lower receipt needs immediate review. Track receipts and obligations separately; project value includes later receipts that cannot pay an earlier bill. Report deviations to the treasurer before funds are committed or obligations become irreversible.” This operational note preserves the same floor and trigger without presenting NPV as spendable cash.

5. Audit the short version against the appendix

Read the memo sentence by sentence and ask which primitive, calculation or judgment supports it. For Case1, “B limits regret” relies on the declared deterministic menu, common θ range and committee rule; it does not imply a nonnegative outcome. For Case2, “financed L is feasible” relies on the committed facility, modeled dates, both stated states and complete cash ledger; it is not demonstrated by expectedNPV. A model can be internally correct and externally inadequate. Put the most decision-relevant inadequacy next to the recommendation, not only in a distant footnote.

Record provenance compactly: inputs are observed, estimated, externally sourced or stipulated; calculations identify code/version or hand derivation; uncertainty labels say what the range means; the decision rule names its owner. An unpublished model output is not independent empirical validation, and a source describing how to write a memo does not support the memo's economic numbers. If a reader disputes a number, trace it through the appendix. If a reader disputes a value judgment, identify the criterion and compare the resulting choice. If a new observation invalidates an input, issue a versioned correction to every recipient of the earlier analysis under the actual communication process.

Practice rubric. For a twenty-point memo task, award decision/audience/authority3; correct quantitative comparison5; honest uncertainty and source status4; feasible alternatives/trade-offs4; recommendation with owner/trigger and faithful appendix4. An unsupported probability, cash commitment or causal finding requires correction even if prose is clear. A complete response supplies the revised memo or display, not merely a list of desirable headings. Test communication with a reader's reconstruction of the decision and its condition; confidence ratings alone do not establish comprehension.
