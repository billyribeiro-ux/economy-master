Choose the allocation rule, the payment rights and the network

An allocation office can assign placements by reported preferences, sell owned resources at prices, or support collaboration among people who affect one another’s returns. These are different economic problems. A stable placement need not maximize a sum of monetary values; an efficient assignment needs an actual payment rule; and a welfare-improving network may never form through individually attractive steps. The design question begins by specifying what each participant can choose and which changes require someone else’s consent.

Capability. Prove applicant deferred acceptance is individually strategy-proof in a finite strict one-to-one market, including incomplete lists and outside options; certify efficient monetary assignments; and solve network games and link-formation problems with explicit global, boundary and financing arguments. Six original cases and twelve solved exercises distinguish these claims.

Prerequisites. The introductory matching chapter established deferred-acceptance stability and proposer optimality while deferring the full incentive proof. Sections 1–3 below close that proof. Static games supplies Nash equilibrium and best replies; Mechanism design and auctions supplies the distinction between a type, an admissible report and a payment rule. Basic finite linear algebra and quadratic completion are used explicitly. All examples here are synthetic models, not measured market outcomes.

1. Strict matching with an outside option

Let A be a finite set of applicants and H a finite set of institutions with one place each. Their sizes need not agree; either set may be empty. Each agent has a strict preference order over members of the opposite side and the outside outcome ∅, meaning unmatched. A partner is acceptable exactly when ranked above ∅. We encode a report as an ordered subset of acceptable names. All omitted names are below outside; their relative order is irrelevant to the algorithm. A false report may reorder names, omit a truly acceptable name, or declare a truly unacceptable name acceptable.

A matching μ is a partial bijection: μ(a)=h if and only if μ(h)=a; otherwise μ(i)=∅. There are no payments, matching externalities or couples. An agent compares matchings solely by its own assigned partner. Individual rationality means nobody is matched to an unacceptable partner. An unmatched applicant and an empty institution are permitted outcomes. A pair (a,h) blocks μ if both strictly prefer each other to their assigned partner or outside. A matching is stable when it is individually rational and has no blocking pair. Stability tests permitted bilateral deviations; it is neither a statement about every coalition nor a numerical welfare sum.

In applicant-proposing deferred acceptance, abbreviated APDA, select any unmatched applicant with an untried acceptable name. It proposes to its highest remaining name. That institution retains its favorite acceptable proposal among its existing hold and the new offer, rejecting the rest. An unacceptable proposal is rejected immediately. Continue until no eligible proposal remains; make the holds final. These proposals are steps in a computation on reports, not binding offers that a proposer can strategically withdraw while the algorithm runs. Institution-proposing DA, IPDA, interchanges the two sides.

Termination and stability. Every ordered proposer–recipient pair is tried at most once, so at most |A||H| proposals occur. A recipient’s held partner only improves in its strict order; after accepting someone acceptable it never returns to unmatched. Final individual rationality follows from the proposal and retention rules. If (a,h) blocked the final outcome, a would have proposed to h before its final, less-preferred partner, or before exhausting its list. Institution h rejected a immediately or later and thereafter held someone strictly better. Its final partner is therefore better than a, contradicting blocking. The proof applies to either proposing side and includes empty lists.

The first rejected achievable pair. Call (a,h) achievable if it occurs in some stable matching for this fixed profile. Suppose some achievable pair is rejected, and take the first such rejection. Let ν be stable with ν(a)=h. Rejection cannot be for unacceptability, because ν is individually rational. Institution h instead holds b≠a, whom it strictly prefers to a. To have proposed to h, b has already been rejected by every institution it ranks above h. If ν(b) were above h, that earlier rejection would itself concern an achievable pair, contrary to our choice of the first one. It cannot equal h, since ν(a)=h. If ν(b)=∅, b still prefers h because it proposed only to acceptable names. Thus b strictly prefers h to ν(b), and h prefers b to ν(h)=a. Pair (b,h) blocks ν: a contradiction.

No proposer is ever rejected by an achievable partner. Consequently APDA gives each applicant a partner weakly preferred to its partner in any stable matching. This is proposer optimality. It also proves schedule independence: each legal proposal schedule yields a stable proposer-optimal outcome; strict preferences imply that any two such outcomes assign the same partner to every proposer and hence are the same matching. This establishes an outcome independent of how we queue eligible proposals.

The definitions and rejection argument can be compared with Manea’s matching slides, dated 27 June 2016. Proposer optimality concerns stable matchings for one fixed reported profile. A lie changes that profile. Therefore this result alone is not a proof that truth-telling is optimal.

2. A complete proof of proposer strategy-proofness

We fix every applicant’s report except applicant i’s and fix all institutions’ strict priority and acceptability lists. The other reports need not be truthful. We will construct one set Si, independent of i’s report, and prove that APDA always assigns i its reported-best element of that set. Truth then selects its true best element. The construction requires a finite unmatched-set lemma, not an assumption about incentives.

The same agents are unmatched in every stable matching

Suppose μ and ν are stable for the same profile, μ(m0)=∅, and ν(m0)=h0. By individual rationality m0 prefers h0 to outside. Stability of μ forces h0 to have a partner m1 whom it prefers to m0; otherwise that pair blocks μ. Since ν(h0)=m0, stability of ν forces m1 to have a partner h1 whom it prefers to h0. The partner must exist, because h0 is acceptable to m1. Stability of μ now forces h1 to have a partner m2 whom it prefers to m1. Continue alternately:

hk=ν(mk),   mk+1=μ(hk),
hk≻mₖμ(mk),   mk+1≻hₖν(hk).

Every next partner exists by the preceding strict preference and stability. Nor can the path close into a cycle. At a first repeated applicant mk+1=mj, j=0 would give m0 a μ-partner, contradicting μ(m0)=∅. If j>0, injectivity of μ gives hk=hj−1; injectivity of ν then gives mk=mj−1, an earlier repeated applicant. This contradicts the definition of the first repeat. Finiteness makes an indefinitely extending path of distinct applicants impossible. Thus no applicant is unmatched only in μ. Swap μ and ν for the reverse inclusion, and interchange applicants and institutions for the same conclusion on the other side. Every stable matching has exactly the same unmatched agents. This is the finite one-to-one result just proved; no hospital-capacity extension is being invoked.

Construct a menu without asking i for a report

Run IPDA with i rejecting every proposal. Define Si to contain outside and every institution that proposes to i during this reject-all run. Its computation never uses i’s preferences. If we delete the futile proposals to i, the remaining steps form a legal IPDA run with i removed. By schedule independence its final matching is the unique IPDA outcome ν−i of that smaller market. Institutions propose in descending acceptable order, so

Si={∅} ∪ {h∈H : i≻hν−i(h)}.

If h is unmatched in the smaller market, the comparison is with outside. An institution that finds i unacceptable cannot be in the second set. An institution that proposes to i in the reject-all run subsequently moves only downward on its list, so i ranks above its final partner. Conversely, if i ranks above that final partner, or above outside when h exhausts its list, h must have tried i. This proves the displayed equivalence, including empty institutions. The menu may contain institutions that i truly finds unacceptable; outside will protect i against them under a truthful report.

Every actual APDA partner is in this menu

For any ordered-subset report Pi, let h*=APDAi(Pi,P−i). If h*=∅, it is already in Si. Otherwise take the full APDA matching and replace i’s report by the singleton list [h*]. That matching is still individually rational and stable: i keeps its only acceptable partner, deleting other acceptable names cannot create an i-blocking pair, and everyone else’s assignments and comparisons are unchanged. By the unmatched-set lemma, IPDA on the singleton problem must also match i, necessarily to h*.

Couple that singleton IPDA run with the reject-all run: choose the same free institution for the next proposal as long as the histories agree. Before the first offer from h*, i rejects every offer in both runs, so all responses agree. The singleton run must eventually receive h*’s offer to finish matched. Therefore the reject-all run receives the same offer. Hence h*∈Si. This argument used stability and the proved unmatched-set lemma, not strategy-proofness.

No menu member is reported better than the actual partner

First suppose h* is an institution. Truncate Pi to the names strictly above h*, deleting h* and every lower name. Call the resulting list Ti. Take a legal full APDA execution and reproduce its prefix up to just before i’s first proposal to its final partner h*. Every earlier proposal by i is to a member of Ti and has been rejected; all those proposals and responses remain legal and identical in the truncated problem. At that point i is unmatched and has exhausted its entire truncated list. It makes no further proposals and remains unmatched while the other applicants finish. This constructs a stable truncated-problem outcome with i unmatched. If h*=∅, take Ti to be the original entire acceptable list; the original final outcome already leaves i unmatched.

The unmatched-set lemma implies that IPDA on the Ti problem leaves i unmatched as well. Now i is a receiver. Once it accepts an acceptable offer, it always holds one: it can only upgrade, and a held proposer does not withdraw. Thus the truncated IPDA run never receives an offer belonging to Ti. Couple it with reject-all up to a first such offer. All responses before that event would agree, so the event would occur in both or neither. It occurs in neither. Therefore Si contains no institution strictly Pi-preferred to h*.

The incentive theorem. The two arguments show, for every report Pi, that APDA selects the Pi-best element of the fixed set Si, where outside is included. A truthful report therefore selects the true-preference maximum over all outcomes any unilateral report can induce. No lie can strictly improve the applicant’s true outcome. This is individual strategy-proofness, or weak dominance of truthful reporting, in the full ordered-subset report domain. It covers arbitrary reordering, truncation, an empty list and falsely acceptable names. It does not assert that every lie hurts, that recipients are strategy-proof, or that coalitions cannot manipulate.

This route is developed from the noncircular Appendix B of Gonczarowski, Heffetz and Thomas, version 4 dated 9 September 2026. Their main-text menu proof assumes the known incentive theorem; it would be circular for our purpose. Here the finite unmatched-set lemma and the singleton and truncation couplings have been supplied explicitly. The theorem remains confined to strict one-to-one matching without externalities; ties, capacities, couples and contracts require their own domains and proofs.

3. An outside option can be the best attainable outcome

Worked case 1: four applicants, three places

Read each list from best to worst. Omitted names are unacceptable; outside lies below every listed name. There is one seat at each institution.

APDA immediately gives A–X and B–Y. C and D both seek Z, which retains C. The result is μ=(A–X,B–Y,C–Z,D–∅). Under IPDA, X first proposes to B, Y to A and Z to C, giving ν=(A–Y,B–X,C–Z,D–∅). Both are stable. To show the list is complete, observe that any stable matching must fill Z with C: if Z is empty or holds D, C and Z block. Both A and B must be matched within {X,Y}, because a vacant institution and an unmatched mutually acceptable applicant would block. The only two bijections of that submarket are the displayed assignments. Thus there are exactly 2 stable matchings, not merely two examples of stable matchings.

The fixed menu of A is {∅,X,Y}, and that of B is {∅,X,Y}. For example, remove A and run IPDA: X proposes to B, while Y is rejected by B in favor of X and ends empty. Both X and Y would prefer A to their resulting partner or outside. C’s menu is {∅,Z}. D’s is {∅}: when D is removed, Z already obtains C, whom it prefers to D. Truth therefore gives D its best attainable outcome, although that outcome is unmatched. This is not a guarantee that every applicant obtains a place.

A complete finite check enumerates 21 individually rational partial matchings: seven partial matchings in the A,B–X,Y submarket times three choices for Z (empty, C or D). Testing every mutually acceptable pair leaves exactly μ and ν. Over three institutions there are 1+3+6+6=16 ordered-subset reports, so 64 unilateral reports across the four applicants. Each reproduces its report-best fixed-menu choice. The general incentive proof does not depend on this finite verification.

Changed institution. If C is removed, D gets Z. If C remains but Z’s priority is reversed to D above C, D again gets Z and C becomes unmatched. Neither change is a preference lie by D with the rest of the environment held fixed. As a separate boundary example, one applicant who truly finds the only institution unacceptable receives outside when truthful; falsely listing that institution can produce a worse match if it accepts the applicant. Including outside in the proof matters.

4. Which messages and whose incentives?

Worked case 2: a recipient’s lie and an applicant’s short list

This is a different market. All four partners on each list are acceptable; outside comes last.

Truthful APDA gives (A–X,B–Y,C–Z,D–W): X holds A rather than D, and D goes to W. IPDA gives (A–Y,B–Z,C–X,D–W), because every institution obtains its first choice. These are the only stable matchings. Completeness can be seen without guessing: D must receive W, since any other partner for D leaves W willing to take D and D prefers W to Y or Z, while X cannot stably hold D when either A or C would then block. More directly and without relying on that abbreviated chain, enumerate all 4!=24 full bijections and test all sixteen pairs; precisely the two displayed bijections have no blocking pair. An incomplete matching cannot be stable in this complete balanced market: an unmatched applicant and empty institution would block. The finite checker also tests all 209 partial matchings, including those immediately excluded by this observation.

Allow only X to report C,D,A,B instead of its true C,A,D,B. When D proposes, X now holds D and rejects A. A moves to Y, displacing B; B moves to Z, displacing C; C moves to X, displacing D; D goes to W. The resulting matching is the second one above. X has improved in its true order from A to C. The applicant theorem says nothing about this recipient deviation. We have exhibited one manipulable recipient, not proved a universal impossibility statement about every matching mechanism.

With truthful institution lists fixed, the institution portions of the applicants’ menus are A:{X,Y,W}, B:{Y,Z}, C:{X,Z}, D:{W}; add outside to each. Every one of the 1+4+12+24+24=65 possible ordered lists can be checked, totaling 260 unilateral reports. Menus are possible outcomes across changed reports, not merely partners occurring in stable matchings for the true profile. A’s menu includes W, even though A gets neither W in the two true-profile stable outcomes.

Now impose a one-name report cap. A,B,C,D sincerely list X,Y,Z,X, respectively. The result leaves D unmatched. D can instead submit the permitted list [W] and obtain W. “Report your true first choice” is therefore not a dominant instruction in this capped domain. The full true ordering was not an admissible message. If the cap is two and everyone lists its true first two, D can list X,W and the original APDA outcome returns. A cap change, recipient manipulation and applicant misreporting in the unrestricted domain are three distinct experiments.

5. Assignments with prices and owned resources

Consider a separate finite resource market. Each buyer can obtain at most one object; each object can have at most one buyer. Buyer i values object j at vij in quasilinear utility units and can decline for incremental utility zero. Each object belongs to its own seller, whose retention value is zero. If the buyer pays pj, its utility increment is vij−pj, and the seller receives that payment. There may be a declared set of forbidden buyer–object pairs. Monetary values and transfers are meaningful here; adding or comparing ordinal placement ranks from the previous sections would not produce this model.

A global price certificate. Choose nonnegative numbers ui,pj satisfying ui+pj≥vij on every allowed edge. For any feasible partial assignment M,

Σ(i,j)∈M vij ≤ Σi matchedui+Σj soldpj ≤ Σiui+Σjpj.

The first inequality uses the edge constraints and the second uses nonnegativity and the one-unit resource limits. If a candidate assignment has equality on each assigned edge, ui=0 for every unmatched buyer and pj=0 for every unsold object, it attains the bound. It therefore maximizes total value over all partial assignments. Every assigned buyer obtains its best allowed value minus price and weakly prefers buying to outside. Every unmatched buyer has no positive-surplus allowed object. Nonnegative prices and zero retention values make sellers willing to sell, and an unsold object is priced at zero. These statements form a competitive supporting-price certificate.

This proof is just a sum of inequalities. A finite set of assignments always has a value maximizer; we have not shown that every possible assignment problem has supporting prices, nor invoked general linear-programming integrality. The welfare sum includes sellers: each payment subtracts from one party and adds to another. This model and that cancellation are discussed in Pass’s matching-markets chapter, Sections 8.1–8.3.

Choose prices p=(5,2,0) and buyer utilities u=(7,5,7). The nine values ui+pj are rows (12,9,7), (10,7,5), (12,9,7), each weakly above its valuation row. Assignment A–X, B–Z, C–Y has equality on every chosen edge. Its value is 12+5+9=26, exactly Σu+Σp=19+7. Thus it is globally optimal, including against partial allocations. The six full-assignment values, in order XYZ, XZY, YXZ, YZX, ZXY, ZYX for A,B,C, are 25,26,25,17,21,12. Every value is positive, so a partial assignment can be extended to a full one with higher total value. The optimum is unique.

The payment ledger matters. A pays seller X 5, B pays seller Z 0 and C pays seller Y 2. Buyer cash closes at (15,20,18); seller cash receipts, in X,Y,Z order, are (5,2,0). Total cash remains 60. Buyer utility increments total 19 and seller receipts total 7, giving social value 26. Seller Z is indifferent about selling for zero; that tie is allowed. Although B also demands X and C also demands Z at these prices, the displayed chosen allocation clears the market.

Change one resource constraint. Forbid B–Z. The optimal value falls to 25, attained by exactly A–X/B–Y/C–Z and A–Y/B–X/C–Z. Prices (6,2,0) and utilities (6,4,7) give rows (12,8,6), (10,6,4), (13,9,7). All allowed-edge inequalities hold and the chosen edges attain equality in both allocations. The B–Z inequality fails, 4<5, but it is no longer an allowed allocation. The total bound is 17+8=25. All nine original values are positive and the direct certificate covers the 27 allowed partial assignments; no extension argument through a forbidden edge is needed. At zero prices both A and B would most want X. An efficient allocation without supporting prices is not automatically a decentralized clearing outcome.
