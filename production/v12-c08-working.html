Regression discontinuity and synthetic controls

A threshold can supply a local comparison, and a weighted collection of untreated units can supply a comparative trajectory. Neither device makes the missing counterfactual observable without assumptions. Regression discontinuity asks whether potential-outcome means continue smoothly through a cutoff. Synthetic control asks whether preperiod information and donor support constrain the untreated postperiod trajectory. This chapter derives restricted results strong enough to use, and counterexamples that show their limits.

Capability. Define a cutoff target correctly, separate continuity from local randomization, derive local-linear bias and variance, evaluate fuzzy compliance assumptions, diagnose donor support and factor rank, and interpret placebo ranks under their actual probability law. Six original cases and twelve complete solutions use finite calculations. No case is an empirical effect estimate.

Prerequisites. Chapter 1 supplies limits and normal calculations; Chapter 2 supplies weighted least squares; Chapter 3 supplies exclusion, monotonicity and instrument rank; Chapter 6 supplies finite assignment inference; Chapter 7 supplies counterfactual trend and support distinctions. Fixed-score independent Gaussian errors, continuous-score populations, finite noiseless factor tables and randomized policy labels are separate laws below.

1. A cutoff effect is defined by conditional limits

Let R be a continuously distributed forcing score with positive density on both sides of a known, ex-ante cutoff c=0. The sharp rule assigns treatment D=1{R≥0}. Assume consistency Y=Y(D), no interference, and finite relevant conditional means. For d=0,1 assume the conditional mean E[Y(d)|R=r] admits a version m_d(r) continuous on a neighborhood of zero. Because the score has positive support there, two such continuous versions that agree almost everywhere also agree everywhere: a nonzero difference at a point would remain nonzero on an open set of positive probability. The cutoff target τ_RD=m₁(0)−m₀(0) is therefore defined by unique continuous limits, not by an arbitrary value of a conditional expectation on the probability-zero event R=0.

For r<0 the observed conditional mean is m₀(r), and for r>0 it is m₁(r). Taking one-sided limits proves

τ_RD=limr↓0E[Y|R=r]−limr↑0E[Y|R=r].

This is a local mean effect under the stated continuity restrictions. It does not identify the effect for every score or make treatment randomly assigned inside a finite window. Score sorting, another intervention at the same threshold, or discontinuous potential-outcome composition can invalidate the argument. A density or balance diagnostic can reveal some failures, but not establish the unobserved continuity restriction. The inspected Cattaneo–Titiunik review distinguishes this continuity design from a separately assumed local-randomization experiment.

Worked example 1: window averages and a sorting counterexample

Let R be uniform on [−1,1], Y(0)=2+R+R² and Y(1)=Y(0)+3+R. The sharp cutoff effect is 3. In a symmetric window of radius h≤1, the right mean is 5+h+h²/3 and the left mean is 2−h/2+h²/3. Their difference is 3+3h/2: it is 9/2 on the full support and 33/10 when h=1/5. Shrinking the window removes this discrepancy in the limit; a small finite window has not become a randomized experiment.

The observed law also admits a zero-effect model if both potential outcomes are set equal to the observed piecewise curve: 2+r+r² for r<0 and 5+2r+r² for r≥0. This model has the same (R,D,Y), but discontinuous potential-outcome means. Continuity is doing identification work that the observed data alone cannot supply.

For a separate sorting law, let U be a fair 0/1 type, let V be independent uniform [−1,1], set R=(2U−1)|V| and Y(0)=Y(1)=U. Because |V| is uniform [0,1], R still has density 1/2 on both sides of zero. Yet U=0 to the left and U=1 to the right, so the observed mean jumps by 1 while the treatment effect is zero. A perfectly continuous score density does not establish potential-outcome continuity or rule out consequential sorting.

2. Boundary regression weights, bias and uncertainty

Condition on one side's fixed score values x_j relative to zero and nonnegative kernel weights K_j. Fit a+bx by minimizing ∑K_j(Y_j−a−bx_j)². Write S_ℓ=∑K_jx_j^ℓ and Δ=S₀S₂−S₁². At least two distinct positively weighted scores imply Δ>0 by weighted variance. Solving the two normal equations gives the cutoff intercept

â=∑ⱼwⱼYⱼ, wⱼ=Kⱼ(S₂−S₁xⱼ)/Δ,
∑ⱼwⱼ=1, ∑ⱼwⱼxⱼ=0.

These identities reproduce constants and linear functions. Positive K_j do not imply positive outcome weights w_j: estimating an intercept at the edge extrapolates from nearby scores. Fit the two sides separately and subtract their intercepts. For fixed weights and independent Gaussian errors with known variances σ_j², the resulting estimate is exactly normal conditional on the scores. Its mean is the weighted mean-function contrast; its variance is the sum of squared outcome weights times their variances across both sides. This exact finite law is an assumption, not a claim that general RD estimates are Gaussian with known variance.

Worked example 2: estimating a correction changes the variance

Use three distances r=(1/10,1/5,3/10), scores −r on the left and +r on the right, and equal kernel weights. Left means are 1+2x+x², giving (81/100,16/25,49/100); right means are 3+4x+5x², giving (69/20,4,93/20). Add independent normal errors with known standard deviation σ=1/10 to all six observations. The true cutoff jump is 2.

Each local-linear intercept has weights w=(4/3,1/3,−2/3). They sum to 1, annihilate the side's linear score and have ∑w_jr_j²=−1/30. The fitted mean jump is 2+(5−1)(−1/30)=28/15, with variance (14/3)σ². An exact quadratic interpolating intercept instead has weights v=(3,−3,1), annihilating both score and squared score, and has mean jump 2.

Estimate each side's quadratic coefficient from the same observations by q̂=(Y₁−2Y₂+Y₃)/(2·(1/10)²). With δq=q_+−q_−, the linear jump bias is −δq/30. Correcting the jump by adding δq̂/30 changes its weights to w+(5/3)(1,−2,1)=v. The general variance identity includes both the correction variance and its covariance with the original estimator. Here the correction variance is (100/3)σ² and the covariance is exactly zero because w·(1,−2,1)=0. Thus

Var(τ̂_corrected)=[14/3+100/3+2·0]σ²=38σ².

The zero covariance is a calculated feature of this particular design, not permission to ignore covariance in general. If the corrected unbiased estimate were paired with the old standard error σ√(14/3), its nominal 95% interval would cover with probability 2Φ(z₀.₉₇₅√[(14/3)/38])−1≈0.507820530046. Using the correct known variance restores the exact Gaussian reference under the stipulated quadratic means. This calculation illustrates the principle of accounting for an estimated bias correction; it is not a general robust-bias-correction theorem.

A conservative bound without exact quadratic means. Suppose instead the side means are twice continuously differentiable between zero and the observed scores, with |m_+″|≤10 and |m_-″|≤2. Taylor's theorem and the reproduction identities bound the linear jump's absolute bias by B=(10+2)∑|w_j|r_j²/2=13/25. This triangle-inequality bound need not be sharp. With the same known independent Gaussian noise, the interval centered on the linear estimate with radius B+z₀.₉₇₅σ√(14/3) has coverage at least 95% over that bounded-curvature class: on the 95% event that noise is within z standard errors, total error is at most noise plus B. The radius is approximately 0.943400612067. It is a bias-aware interval, not the old variance attached to an estimated correction.

The inspected 2019 Cattaneo–Idrobo–Titiunik foundations manuscript separates smoothing bias from variance and discusses bias correction. The original calculations here state their finite Gaussian and smoothness conditions explicitly.

3. Undersmoothing requires a rate, not just a smaller number

Use a separate fixed-grid sequence. There are n available observations on each side of unit score support, at ±j/n for j=1,…,n, with mutually independent Gaussian errors of known variance σ²>0. Select the nearest k observations per side, 2≤k≤n, and define h=k/n. Widths are fixed before observing the outcome noise. Equal-weight local-linear regression has

wⱼ=2(2k+1−3j)/[k(k−1)], j=1,…,k,
∑wⱼ=1, ∑jwⱼ=0,
∑j²wⱼ=−(k+1)(k+2)/6, ∑wⱼ²=2(2k+1)/[k(k−1)].

For derivation, use S₀=k, ∑j=k(k+1)/2 and ∑j²=k(k+1)(2k+1)/6 in the intercept formula; factors of n cancel in w_j. Substituting that affine function of j into the four sums, using ∑j³=k²(k+1)²/4, gives the displayed identities. For exact side quadratics with coefficients q_+,q_− on x² and δq=q_+−q_−, the finite bias and jump variance are therefore

b_n=−δq(k+1)(k+2)/(6n²),
V_n=4σ²(2k+1)/[k(k−1)].

When k→∞ and h→0, leading mean squared error is δq²h⁴/36+8σ²/(nh). For δq≠0, differentiating this expression in h gives h_opt⁵=72σ²/(δq²n). This is the optimum of the leading expansion with known curvature, not an automatically available bandwidth. If k=floor(nh_opt), then k/(nh_opt)→1 because nh_opt grows as n⁴ᐟ⁵; the exact grid formulas have the same leading limits. The formula is not used at δq=0.

At that width, |b_n|/√V_n→1/2. A conventional interval that ignores bias consequently has limiting coverage Φ(z₀.₉₇₅−1/2)−Φ(−z₀.₉₇₅−1/2)≈0.920902465839, despite an exact normal error distribution at every n. Using h=r h_opt for a fixed r>0 changes the limiting absolute standardized bias to r⁵ᐟ²/2, not zero. A smaller fixed fraction improves this particular approximation but is not rate undersmoothing.

Proper undersmoothing here requires h/h_opt→0 and nh→∞. For example h=n⁻ᵃ with 1/5<a<1 and k=floor(nh) gives k→∞ and √(nh)h²→0; the standardized quadratic bias vanishes. This establishes nominal limiting known-variance Gaussian coverage for this sequence. It does not justify searching many bandwidths for a favorable result, estimating an arbitrary covariance, or carrying the conclusion to a discrete score with no observations approaching zero.

Mean-square consistency under bounded curvature. If |m_+″|≤L_+ and |m_-″|≤L_- near zero, the absolute bias is at most (L_++L_-)h²∑|w_j|/2. Cauchy–Schwarz and the exact squared-weight sum imply ∑|w_j|≤√(k∑w_j²)≤√10 for k≥2. Also V_n≤20σ²/k. Thus mean squared error is bounded by [(L_++L_-)√10 h²/2]²+20σ²/k, which tends to zero whenever h→0 and k→∞. For known Gaussian noise, using this deterministic bias bound plus z√V_n gives the same finite coverage argument as example 2. A bound alone does not tell us that the observed density, the causal continuity assumption or an outcome-selected width is valid.

4. Fuzzy receipt identifies a cutoff complier effect under extra assumptions

Let Z=1{R≥0} be assignment or eligibility, and let actual receipt be D=D(Z). Adopt the following sufficient local model. Each unit has a stable latent type S=(D(0),D(1)); in a neighborhood of zero the type probabilities p_s are constant. Within every positive-probability type, conditional means μ_sd(r)=E[Y(d)|S=s,R=r] have continuous versions through zero. Hence both type probabilities and composite means E[1{S=s}Y(d)|R=r]=p_sμ_sd(r) have common cutoff limits. These restrictions define a common cutoff type population, instead of comparing differently composed limits. Assume consistency, no interference and exclusion: eligibility affects the outcome only through receipt, apart from score relationships already represented by the continuous conditional means.

Monotonicity D(1)≥D(0) leaves always-takers A=(1,1), compliers C=(0,1) and never-takers N=(0,0). By conditioning on types, the receipt jump is p_C and the outcome jump is p_C[μ_C1(0)−μ_C0(0)]; other types receive the same treatment on either side, and their continuous composite means cancel. If p_C>0, their ratio is the cutoff complier mean effect. This is distinct from the assignment outcome effect and from the average treatment effect across all cutoff types. A small or zero first stage is a rank/inference concern; no weak-first-stage normal theorem is supplied here.

Worked example 3: three distinct effects and two distinct failures

Types A,C,N have probabilities 1/4,1/2,1/4, constant near the cutoff. Their untreated outcome levels are 2,1,0 and treatment effects are 6,4,0, also constant in score. Receipt means are 1/4 below and 3/4 above. Outcome means are (1/4)8+(1/2)1=5/2 below and (1/4)8+(1/2)5=9/2 above. The assignment outcome jump is 2, receipt jump 1/2 and local Wald ratio 4. The cutoff average treatment effect is (1/4)6+(1/2)4=7/2.

If the observed outcome additionally contains a direct term Z/2, the outcome jump becomes 5/2 and the Wald ratio becomes 5, while the receipt-mediated complier effect remains 4. This violates exclusion. Separately introduce defiers F=(1,0): let masses of A,C,F,N be 1/4,3/8,1/8,1/4, with untreated levels 2,1,1,0 and effects 6,4,8,0. Receipt limits are 3/8 and 5/8; outcome limits are 7/2 and 4. The first stage is 1/4 and outcome jump 1/2, giving ratio 2. Algebraically it is [(3/8)4−(1/8)8]/[(3/8)−(1/8)]. It lies outside the two switching-type effects 4 and 8 because the defier contribution has negative weight. This violates monotonicity rather than exclusion; the two failures should not be conflated.

5. What preperiod fit can prove about a synthetic control

Let x₁ be the treated unit's vector of pre-treatment predictors and let X₀ collect the corresponding donor columns. Choose weights on the compact simplex W={w:w_j≥0,∑w_j=1} to minimize L(w)=(x₁−X₀w)′V(x₁−X₀w), where V is a prespecified positive-definite predictor-weight matrix. Predictors, their scaling, donor eligibility and the training/holdout split are chosen using preperiod information. Continuity of L and compactness of W guarantee an attained optimum.

The fitted predictor X₀w is unique: two different optimal fits would have a midpoint with strictly smaller quadratic loss under V>0. The weights themselves need not be unique. If w and w+v are feasible, 1′v=0 and X₀v=0, they give the same fit and loss. Conversely, different minimizers must have such a zero-sum flat direction. Thus uniqueness at an optimum means no nonzero feasible flat direction there; full column rank of the augmented matrix (1′;X₀) is a stronger sufficient condition. To make the finite algorithm reproducible when there is a tie, this chapter chooses the lexicographically smallest optimal weight vector in a donor order fixed in advance. Successively minimizing each coordinate over compact optimal sets establishes that this rule exists. A deterministic tie rule does not identify the missing counterfactual.

A finite noiseless factor result, proved. Suppose untreated outcomes satisfy Y_it(0)=f_t′λ_i with the same r-dimensional factor vector f_t for all units and no measurement or idiosyncratic noise. Let F_pre stack the factor rows at the training dates, and Λ₀ collect donor loadings. Exact prefit gives F_pre(λ₁−Λ₀w)=0. If F_pre has full column rank, its left inverse L=(F_pre′F_pre)⁻¹F_pre′ implies λ₁=Λ₀w, so the weighted donors exactly reproduce the untreated treated-unit outcome at every future date governed by these same factors.

More generally, if a particular future factor row lies in the row span of F_pre, write f_t′=a_t′F_pre; exact prefit then suffices for that date even without full column rank. With full column rank and approximate fit error e=x₁−X₀w, the loading error is Le, so the future counterfactual discrepancy obeys |f_t′Le|≤‖f_t′L‖‖e‖. The bound requires the maintained noiseless factor structure and a restriction on the future factor row; a small training loss alone supplies neither. If a new factor is absent from the entire preperiod, or noise enters the fit equation, this proof cannot simply be reused.

The inspected Abadie review discusses donor support, preperiod validation and common-factor reasoning. The result here is finite and noiseless; it is not a general stochastic synthetic-control consistency theorem.

Worked example 4: one supported counterfactual and a donor-removal transfer

Use factors f_t=(1,s_t) with s=(0,1,2,3,4). Donor loadings are A=(0,1), B=(2,0), C=(1,2); the treated loading is (5/4,3/4). Train on s=0,1, hold out the untreated date s=2, and evaluate the two post dates s=3,4. V=I. Donor training columns are (0,1),(2,2),(1,3), and the treated training vector is (5/4,2).

The unique exact-fit weights are (1/4,1/2,1/4). They sum to one and match both loadings; the augmented three-column design is nonsingular. The held-out treated value 11/4 is also matched. Post counterfactuals are 7/2 and 17/4. With observed treated outcomes 11/2 and 29/4, gaps are 2 and 3 under the maintained factor/no-contamination model.

Now exclude C and refit. With weight a on A and 1−a on B, the training loss is (−3/4+2a)²+a². Its derivative is −3+10a, so the constrained minimum is a=3/10, with B weight 7/10. Fitted training values are (7/5,17/10), residuals (−3/20,3/10), and squared-error loss 9/80. The holdout prediction is 2, leaving gap 3/4. Post forecasts are 23/10 and 13/5, so reported gaps become 16/5 and 93/20. These differ from the stipulated effects because removing a donor destroyed exact loading support.

For the maintained two-factor model, F_pre=((1,0),(1,1)) has left inverse L=((1,0),(−1,1)). Future rows f′L are (−2,3) and (−3,4). Applied to the prefit error, they yield counterfactual discrepancies 6/5 and 33/20; adding the true effects 2 and 3 reproduces the reported gaps. This is a model-specific diagnosis of the changed fit, not evidence that every small holdout error validates future factor stability.

6. Forecast support, causal support and nonunique weights

Worked example 5: the same prefit supports different future stories

Keep all observed data in example 4 unchanged. Add a third factor that is zero at every preperiod, takes values (1,2) in the postperiod, has loading 1 for the treated unit and zero for all donors. The treated untreated post path is now (9/2,25/4); with the same observed outcomes, treatment effects are (1,1), not (2,3). Every training and holdout observation is identical in the two stories. The augmented pre-factor matrix has a zero column and lacks full rank. Perfect prefit did not rule out this new post-only factor.

For a separate changed-data intervention, let donor B receive a spillover that raises each of its post outcomes by 2. Under the original weights, the synthetic forecast rises by (1/2)2=1, and gaps fall from (2,3) to (1,2). Donors must represent unaffected comparison outcomes for the causal interpretation. Excluding a potentially affected donor requires refitting and reassessing support; it does not establish that remaining donors are unaffected.

Now consider a distinct two-donor panel: both donors and the treated unit have preperiod vector (1,1); post donor outcomes are 2 and 8, and treated observed outcome is 7. Every convex weight fits perfectly. If w is the first donor's weight, forecasts are 2w+8(1−w)=8−6w, spanning [2,8]; gaps are −1+6w, spanning [−1,5]. The prespecified lexicographic rule picks weights (0,1), forecast 8 and gap −1, but other optimal weights give different forecasts.

That entire gap range is a sharp identification interval only if one maintains the extra restriction that the untreated treated-unit post outcome is generated by some fixed convex mixture of these same donors. Each w then reproduces the preperiod and an admissible post counterfactual; every gap in the interval is attainable. Without that restriction, this is only an algorithmic forecast range: a new treated-only post factor can place the counterfactual outside it. Optimization and causal identification are different claims.

7. Placebo ranks inherit an assignment law, not an automatic p-value

Specify an entire algorithm before ranking: donor pool, training predictors, weights, tie rule, holdout or exclusion rules, and the post statistic. For every candidate treated label, refit that complete algorithm treating the other eligible units as donors. Define RMSPE as the square root of the average squared gap over the stated period, and score T as post-RMSPE divided by pre-RMSPE. Prespecify zero handling: if pre-RMSPE is zero and post-RMSPE is positive, use the largest ordered score +∞; if both are zero, use score 0. Infinity here is an ordering convention, not a finite JSON number. All such ties remain included.

Finite rank validity. Under a sharp null of no policy effect on any unit/date and no interference, the outcome panel is fixed under every candidate assignment. Suppose the actual known probability of label j is q_j>0, summing to one. The fully refitted statistic T_j is then a fixed number (possibly the declared largest score). Define p_j=∑_ℓq_ℓ1{T_ℓ≥T_j}. For any α, the labels with p_j≤α form an upper score tail, with every tied label included together. If this set is nonempty, take its lowest score: the probability mass of the entire rejection set is its inclusive tail probability, at most α. If empty it is zero. Thus P(p_J≤α)≤α under that actual assignment law.

This proof does not need a correct factor forecast, but it does need the sharp null, known assignment law and full algorithm replay. It tests the sharp null, not a general zero average effect. A uniform rank can be a useful descriptive comparison for an observational intervention; without the required assignment law it does not acquire this exact calibration. Outcome-guided exclusions or filters applied only to the actual treated label do not replay the stated statistic; any filtering rule must be specified and applied for every assignment.

Worked example 6: refitting and using the actual label probabilities

Four units have preperiod profiles A=(0,0), B=(2,0), C=(0,2), D=(2,2) and one post outcome (4,0,1,2). For each label, minimize the two-date squared prefit error over the other three units' simplex, using the same algorithm. The refits are:

AB=1/2,C=1/2(1,1)17/27/2

Each fitted point is the closest point on the opposite triangle's boundary; the squared-error training loss is 2 and pre-MSE is 2/2=1. Under a genuinely uniform sharp-null label assignment, inclusive p-values are (1/4,1/2,3/4,1). Rejection at α=1/4 occurs only for label A and has probability 1/4. No 5% rejection is possible; the smallest attainable p-value is 1/4.

If the actual assignment probabilities are instead (7/10,1/10,1/10,1/10), using those uniform ranks would reject with probability 7/10 at nominal 1/4. Correct weighted tails are (7/10,4/5,9/10,1), so rejection probability at 1/4 is zero. The outcome table, fitted gaps and ordering are unchanged; the probability statement changes because the assignment law changed.

Complete solutions and evaluation criteria

  1. Consistency gives observed conditional means m₀(r) on r<0 and m₁(r) on r>0. Continuous versions and two-sided positive support make their limits unique, so their jump is m₁(0)−m₀(0). Two continuous versions agreeing almost everywhere cannot differ at zero, because continuity would create an open positive-probability region of disagreement. P(R=0)=0 permits arbitrary values of an unrestricted conditional expectation at that event, so only the stipulated continuous version defines the target. Setting both potential outcomes equal to the observed piecewise curve gives zero effects and the same observations, but each potential mean then jumps at zero. It violates the continuity premise.

  2. On the right, E[R]=h/2 and E[R²]=h²/3, giving mean 5+h+h²/3. On the left these are −h/2 and h²/3, giving 2−h/2+h²/3. The contrast is 3+3h/2, hence 9/2 and 33/10 at the requested widths. For sorting, |V| is uniform [0,1], while the independent fair U chooses the sign. Thus the score density is 1/2 on both sides. Conditional U is 0 left and 1 right, producing jump 1; Y(1)−Y(0)=0 for every unit. A continuous density or nonrejection of a density test does not establish continuous potential-outcome means or a randomized finite-window law.

  3. Invert the two normal equations: â=(S₂∑KY−S₁∑KxY)/(S₀S₂−S₁²). The coefficient of Y_j is the stated w_j; summing it gives 1 and summing w_jx_j gives zero. At the three equally spaced distances the weights are (4/3,1/3,−2/3), with squared sum 7/3 and squared-score moment −1/30. The mean jump is 2−4/30=28/15; two independent sides give variance (14/3)σ². Solving reproduction for degrees 0,1,2 gives quadratic weights (3,−3,1), squared sum 19, mean jump 2 and variance 38σ². Negative outcome weights perform boundary extrapolation; nonnegative kernel weights do not prohibit them.

  4. Each q̂=50(Y₁−2Y₂+Y₃); adding the right-minus-left q̂ difference divided by 30 changes each intercept's weight vector by (5/3)(1,−2,1). The correction's jump variance is 2σ²(25/9)(1+4+1)=(100/3)σ². Its covariance with the original jump is 2σ²(5/3)[4/3−2/3−2/3]=0. Total variance is 38σ². Pairing this unbiased corrected normal estimate with the old standard error gives coverage 2Φ(z₀.₉₇₅√[(14/3)/38])−1≈0.507820530046. For the separate curvature-bound result, ∑|w|r²=13/150, so B=(10+2)(13/150)/2=13/25. Add B to z₀.₉₇₅(1/10)√(14/3), obtaining radius about 0.943400612067. Noise within its central 95% interval implies total error within this radius; no generic unknown-variance or bandwidth-selection guarantee follows.

  5. Insert ∑j=k(k+1)/2 and ∑j²=k(k+1)(2k+1)/6 into the intercept formula to get w_j=2(2k+1−3j)/[k(k−1)].   12. Six objections, one point each. (1) A fixed fraction of h_opt leaves nonzero standardized leading bias; valid undersmoothing is a rate condition in a specified model. (2) A density nonrejection neither proves potential-outcome continuity nor establishes random assignment in a finite window. (3) Estimated bias correction changes variance and covariance; the old standard error has no general justification. (4) Selecting donors using treated post outcomes compromises the preperiod-only counterfactual procedure and is not the stated prespecified placebo algorithm. (5) Perfect prefit does not establish future-factor support, no spillovers or unique causal weights. (6) A uniform observational rank has no exact assignment calibration without the corresponding known law and sharp-null replay.

    Two repairs, two points each. An RD repair should declare the cutoff target and continuity story, inspect density/balance as diagnostics, and use an inference procedure whose bias, bandwidth and variance assumptions are actually supported. The finite known-Gaussian calculation here is an example, not a ready-made theorem for arbitrary data. A synthetic-control repair should fix preperiod training/holdout and donor eligibility rules, inspect support and sensitivity to donors, and report the actual evidential status of placebo ranks. Exact randomization language additionally requires a defensible known assignment law and full algorithm refitting. Each repair earns full credit only with its remaining identification/inference limitation.

    Conclusion, two points. The current estimates may be useful local or comparative descriptions, but the report has not established its causal or exact-coverage claims. A revised analysis separates the counterfactual assumptions, the estimation procedure and the probability law that calibrates uncertainty. The rubric rewards justified reasoning, not a particular effect sign or estimator brand.
