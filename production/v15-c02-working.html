Capability and prerequisites

Evaluate wage, employment and education mechanisms using explicit economic choices and credible comparisons. Distinguish a private investment return, a causal earnings effect and a social return; solve an employer's hiring decision; interpret discrimination and mobility evidence without silently changing the target. Prerequisite: public economics and taxation, including the accepted econometrics and graduate microeconomics sequence. Discounting, potential outcomes, Bayes' rule, concavity and equilibrium are used below. Every numerical population is synthetic. No example describes current labor law or a real employer.

Whose return, which worker, and what counterfactual?

A ministry sees higher wages among graduates and proposes a tuition grant. An employer says a wage floor must reduce jobs. A personnel report attributes an unexplained wage gap to discrimination, while a regional report treats migrants' higher pay as the benefit of moving. Each claim could be correct under particular assumptions. None follows from the reported difference alone. Investment changes skills and credentials; workers select into education, employers and locations; wages depend on bargaining and market structure; policy changes who participates and what the rest of the market does.

Labor economics studies the allocation of time and human capabilities through these institutions. Human capital is productive knowledge, skill or health embodied in people. A credential can also transmit information without itself raising productivity. General skills are useful at several employers; firm-specific skills create value mainly inside one match, making separation and bargaining relevant to training finance. A measured wage combines compensation for productivity, unpleasant conditions, scarce skills and bargaining positions. It is neither a sufficient statistic for personal worth nor automatically the worker's social marginal product.

Keep units explicit. Earnings and tuition below are goods per person at specified dates; hours or employment L are labor units; a wage is goods per labor unit. A log-wage coefficient is a proportional association, not a level of goods. Participation is a decision whether to work; the intensive margin is how much to work conditional on participation. Reservation wages summarize the value of an alternative in a specified environment. They are not fixed biological attributes: care obligations, commuting, benefits, job amenities and legal access can change them.

1. Investment is a dated feasible choice

For schooling alternatives s, let earnings y_t(s), tuition and other real expenses k_t(s), and a common riskless discount factor R=1+r>0 be specified. A risk-neutral person with unrestricted borrowing chooses the largest present value Σ_t[y_t(s)−k_t(s)]/R^t, adding nonpecuniary benefits if they matter. The difference between alternatives must include earnings lost while studying. Tuition is a cash bill; foregone earnings are an opportunity cost. Combining them into a present-value cost is correct, but pretending both must be paid in cash before enrollment can create a false liquidity constraint.

If earnings are risky, an expected-value ranking requires the corresponding risk preferences and financing assumptions. Under concave utility, the timing and covariance of earnings with other resources matter. The internal rate of return is a root of the net present-value equation; it need not exist or be unique for arbitrary cash-flow signs. With one initial cost and strictly positive later gains, the present value is strictly decreasing in R>0, so any root is unique. Comparing that root with a borrowing rate is justified only when that borrowing opportunity is actually available.

A cross-sectional regression log w=α+βs+X′γ+u does not by itself estimate this investment return. β can include selection into schooling, credential effects, occupation changes and omitted ability or family resources. Nor does a first-order condition at a person's optimum imply the entire earnings schedule is exponential in schooling. The investment and selection distinctions motivate Angrist's schooling lecture; we use a fully specified finite choice instead.

Worked case 1 — positive present value, blocked enrollment

There are dates 0, 1 and 2. Working immediately pays 20 at date 0 and 30 at each later date. Studying pays no date-0 earnings, costs tuition 10 immediately, and pays 50 at each later date. The safe gross discount rate is 11/10. Thus (initial opportunity cost,later gain,R)=(30,20,11/10) and the gain from studying is

Δ=−30+20/R+20/R²=570/121 ≈4.711 goods at date 0.

The complete two-choice comparison proves schooling is privately preferred under unrestricted finance. The unique break-even rate solves 3R²−2R−2=0: R*=(1+√7)/3, so r*≈21.525%. The negative quadratic root is outside the positive-gross-rate domain. This is a synthetic private earnings comparison, not evidence that schooling caused a population's earnings difference.

Now impose initial liquid wealth 12, a date-0 consumption minimum of 5, and no borrowing or sale of future earnings. Schooling leaves 12−10=2, so it is infeasible despite its higher present value. The cash shortfall is 3, not the thirty-unit opportunity cost. A loan of 3 at rate 10% restores date-0 consumption to 5 and requires 33/10 at date 1. That payment is feasible from schooling earnings 50; its present value is 3, so fair financing does not change the original net present value. Alternatively a grant of 3 must be paid for by someone else. It changes private liquidity and distribution; the grant itself is not an additional three goods of educational output. A social comparison must establish causal output gains, actual resource costs, spillovers and the financing burden.

2. A causal return has an assignment and a population

Let D(z)∈{0,1} denote training participation under encouragement z and Y(d) earnings under participation d. Assume consistency, no interference, random encouragement independent of all these potential quantities, exclusion of any direct z effect on earnings, both encouragement states occurring, monotonicity D(1)≥D(0), and positive probability of compliers C={D(1)=1,D(0)=0}. Always-takers train either way; never-takers never train. Then randomization and exclusion imply

E[Y|Z=1]−E[Y|Z=0]=E[(Y(1)−Y(0))(D(1)−D(0))]=P(C)E[Y(1)−Y(0)|C].

The first stage is E[D|Z=1]−E[D|Z=0]=P(C). Dividing gives the local average treatment effect among compliers, not the effect for every worker. This is the binary identification logic developed in Imbens and Angrist. The equation identifies a population quantity under assumptions; finite-sample inference still needs a design, uncertainty procedure and a sufficiently informative first stage. Exclusion is not a consequence of randomization. An encouragement that also changes job search may affect earnings without training.

Worked case 2 — three different returns in one population

A unit population has always-takers A, compliers C and never-takers N, with (P(A),P(C),P(N))=(1/5,2/5,2/5). Their untreated earnings are (Y_A(0),Y_C(0),Y_N(0))=(30,20,10) and their treatment effects are (τ_A,τ_C,τ_N)=(5,10,20). Randomize Z with probability one half independently of type. These are exact population quantities, not estimated cell means. Under Z=0 only A train; under Z=1 both A and C train. Consequently (E[D|0],E[D|1],E[Y|0],E[Y|1])=(1/5,3/5,19,23).

The Wald ratio is (23−19)/(3/5−1/5)=10. The average treatment effect over everyone is (1/5)5+(2/5)10+(2/5)20=13. Pooling both encouragement arms, trainees have mean earnings [(1/5)35+(1/5)30]/(2/5)=65/2. Nontrainees have mean [(1/5)20+(2/5)10]/(3/5)=40/3. The unadjusted training difference is 115/6. Thus (LATE,ATE,raw difference)=(10,13,115/6). Selection creates a large raw difference even though the instrument correctly identifies the smaller complier effect.

For a funded encouragement expansion from Z=0 to Z=1, suppose each training place consumes 6 real goods, outcomes are causal real-output gains in the same dated numeraire, and there are no other behavioral, administrative or financing costs. Training rises by 2/5, so additional cost is 12/5 and output rises by 4: net resources rise by 8/5. Lump-sum taxes finance the additional 12/5; subtract the training resources once, not again as an extra tax loss. The comparison concerns this expansion and its compliers. A program reaching never-takers, a wage change that redistributes employer profits, or a different training technology needs new evidence. If encouragement itself adds 2 earnings to everyone, the first stage stays 2/5 but the reduced form becomes 6; the Wald ratio becomes 15 and no longer isolates training.

3. Labor supply, demand and employer power

For a person with time endowment T, hours l∈[0,T], nonlabor income a and utility u(c,T−l), the budget is c=wl+a. At an interior differentiable optimum, w u_c=u_leisure. A higher wage raises the opportunity cost of leisure but also changes purchasing power. The compensated substitution effect favors work; the income effect can oppose it if leisure is normal. Aggregate labor supply additionally includes participation and population changes. A convenient quasilinear benchmark c−l²/2 has l=min{w,T} for w≥0; adding a fixed work cost can produce a discrete participation threshold. This benchmark deliberately removes income effects.

A price-taking employer hires until the value of marginal product equals the wage, subject to corners and global profitability. With product-market power, marginal revenue product replaces value at an unchanged product price. With an upward-sloping firm-specific labor supply w(L), a uniform-wage employer pays w(L)L and faces marginal expenditure w(L)+Lw′(L). Hiring one more worker raises the pay of existing workers too. Under smooth positive supply, this equals w(1+1/ε), where ε=(dL/dw)w/L. It is a wedge between marginal revenue product and wage, not proof that any particular observed market has this structure. See the bounded market-structure context in Angrist's lecture.

Worked case 3 — solve the entire wage-floor problem

A single employer has revenue R(L)=20L−L²/2 for L≥0 and faces inverse labor supply w(L)=2+L. Worker reservation costs are distributed so total opportunity cost of the lowest-cost L workers is ∫₀ᴸ(2+x)dx. Primitives are (marginal-revenue intercept,supply intercept,supply slope)=(20,2,1). With no wage floor, profit is 18L−3L²/2, strictly concave and maximized at L=6, w=8. Under competition the allocation solves 20−L=2+L, giving L=9, w=11. At this output total surplus 18L−L² is 81; the employer-power allocation produces 72.

Let an enforced minimum wage m≥0 apply to every hire. The least uniform wage capable of attracting L workers is max{m,2+L}. For m≥2, the wage bill is mL up to L=m−2, and (2+L)L above that kink. Profit is continuous and strictly concave on each side; its derivative jumps downward at the kink. Its complete global solution is

L(m)=6 if 0≤m≤8;   L(m)=m−2 if 8≤m≤11;   L(m)=20−m if 11≤m≤20;   L(m)=0 if m≥20.

For m<2 the original supply schedule already exceeds the floor. For 2≤m≤8 the kink is at or below 6 and the unconstrained optimum on the rising-wage branch survives. For 8<m<11, the left derivative at the kink is 22−2m>0 while the right derivative is 24−3m<0, so the kink is optimal. For 11≤m≤20 the flat-wage branch's optimum 20−m is at or below available supply m−2. For m≥20 every positive hire has nonpositive marginal profit, with strict concavity selecting zero. The formulas coincide at all boundaries. No local first-order condition is being extended through a kink.

At m=10, employment is 8 and the wage is 10. Revenue is 128, payroll 80, profit 48, worker surplus 32 and total surplus 80. At m=12, employment is again 8 but wage 12, profit 32 and worker surplus 48; total is still 80. At m=14, employment returns to 6; at m=20 it vanishes. These latter surplus comparisons assume the lowest-reservation-cost applicants are hired when jobs are rationed. With a different hiring rule, payroll and profit remain determined by employment, but worker opportunity cost and total surplus need not. The checked base and two-floor allocations are (L0,w0,profit0,worker0,L10,profit10,worker10,L12,profit12,worker12)=(6,8,54,18,8,48,32,8,32,48).

A moderate floor raises employment in this particular employer-power model; a high floor reduces it. Empirical policy analysis must identify enforcement, competing labor markets, hours, amenities, prices, entry and the relevant counterfactual. A difference-in-differences comparison requires credible untreated trends and spillover handling; observing employment rise after a reform does not itself establish either a causal effect or monopsony. The simple model excludes search dynamics, worker-specific wage offers and bargaining. These are extensions, not details that can be silently assumed away in an application.

4. Discrimination: mechanism, treatment and measurement

Taste-based discrimination models a decision maker acting as though employing a group imposes a subjective cost beyond its monetary wage. Statistical discrimination models decisions using group-conditioned beliefs about productivity when individual information is incomplete. These are explanations of choice rules, not ethical defenses. Beliefs may be wrong, and historical barriers can shape the information and opportunities that generate them. A wage regression's residual group coefficient is not uniquely one mechanism: omitted job characteristics, selection, measurement error and discrimination can all contribute. Conversely, controlling for occupation or education can remove channels through which prior discrimination operates.

For an explicit taste illustration, workers in each of two groups have the same known productivity 12 and inverse supply 4+L_g. A uniform-wage employer maximizes (12−w_A)L_A+(12−w_B−d)L_B, with subjective penalty d≥0 for B. Strict concavity gives L_A=4 and L_B=max{(8−d)/2,0}. At d=2 the employer hires (4,3), pays (8,7), and earns actual monetary profit 31, while its subjective objective is 25. Without the penalty both groups supply 4 at wage 8 and monetary profit is 32. This shows an explicitly specified nonmonetary motive can support unequal pay despite identical productivity; it does not prove free entry eliminates discrimination in every environment. Treating the discriminatory preference as socially deserving weight would be an additional ethical choice, not an automatic welfare calculation.

Worked case 4 — beliefs and randomized names answer different questions

Suppose output is 16 for a high-productivity worker and 8 otherwise. In a toy competitive risk-neutral hiring market, firms know population high-type probabilities 3/4 in group A and 1/4 in group B. An individual test reports high with probability 3/4 for a high type and 1/4 for a low type, identically across groups. The common signal accuracy is (prior A,prior B,accuracy)=(3/4,1/4,3/4). Bayes' rule for a high signal gives p_g(3/4)/[p_g(3/4)+(1−p_g)(1/4)]. Posteriors are 9/10 and 1/2, so wages equal expected productivity 76/5 and 12. For a low signal, the posteriors are 1/2 and 1/10, giving wages 12 and 44/5. Thus (w_A_high,w_B_high,w_A_low,w_B_low)=(76/5,12,12,44/5). A perfect test would reveal type and remove group conditioning in this maintained model; an uninformative test leaves the prior differences intact.

Now consider a distinct synthetic correspondence experiment: independently sampled vacancies receive one application each; identical résumé templates are randomly assigned one of two name cues, with no interference. We analyze callbacks, not productivity or eventual wages. Conditional on 1,000 independent applications in each cue group, there are 120 and 80 callbacks. The primitives are (n_A,n_B,c_A,c_B)=(1000,1000,120,80). The sample difference is .12−.08=.04. Under the explicitly maintained iid superpopulation model, an approximate unpooled standard error is √[.12·.88/1000+.08·.92/1000]≈.013387. The approximate normal 95% interval is .04±1.96SE, or about [.01376,.06624]. It is not an exact finite-sample confidence guarantee. Repeated applications to a common employer, vacancies linked within firms, selective reporting or template imbalance would require revisiting that design and variance calculation.

Randomization identifies the effect of the assigned cue on callbacks for the sampled design, if implementation and interference assumptions hold. It does not separately identify taste versus beliefs, actual productivity differences, the effect of a person's full social identity, or lifetime earnings. The historical study by Bertrand and Mullainathan (2004) used randomly assigned names on fictitious résumés. Its abstract establishes that design context; the numbers here are original and are not their reported estimates. Any actual fieldwork requires appropriate ethics review, data protection and a plan to minimize burdens on participants.

5. Mobility includes sorting and the distribution being measured

Occupational, geographic and intergenerational mobility are different objects. A worker moves if the value of the destination, net of moving costs and amenities, exceeds the origin alternative. Observed migrants need not be representative of those who stay. A policy that changes migration can also change wages, rents, congestion and the tax base. These are equilibrium responses; a small experiment's effect at existing prices need not equal the effect of moving everyone.

For intergenerational mobility, a regression of adult child log earnings y on parent log earnings x reports a persistence slope, not the effect of transferring goods to a parent. Shared environments, inherited attributes, networks and credit constraints can contribute to the association. Rank-based measures answer a different question and depend on the populations used to form ranks. Absolute mobility asks whether a child exceeds a stated parental real-income level; relative mobility concerns position within a distribution. Growth can raise absolute mobility while relative positions remain unchanged. Measurement age, temporary earnings variation, household versus individual units, price deflators and missing nonworkers all matter. Acemoglu's mobility lectures provide context for these measurement issues; the following distribution is constructed here.

Worked case 5 — selected migrants and noisy generations

Half a population has origin/destination earnings (6,8); the other half has (10,16). Moving costs 3 real goods per person and there are initially no other differences. The primitives are (origin low,destination low,origin high,destination high,cost)=(6,8,10,16,3). Low types stay because their net gain is −1; high types move because their net gain is 3. Observed migrants earn 16 and stayers earn 6, a gap of 10. The migrants' own gross causal gain at these fixed wages is 6, and their net gain is 3. The population gross gain if everyone were moved would be 4, net of cost it would be 1. None equals the observed gap.

Next let each destination wage fall by κm when the fraction m of the whole population moves. Individuals are atomistic; the moving cost stays 3. Low types never move because their net gain −1−κm is negative for κ≥0. High types move when 3−κm is positive. At κ=4 all high types move, m=1/2, and their net gain is 1. At κ=8, the equilibrium has m=3/8: a fraction 3/4 of high types moves and each is indifferent at the margin. Identical high types can mix or be assigned any subset of that mass, with the same aggregate. The complete equilibrium is m=1/2 for 0≤κ≤6 and m=3/κ for κ>6. These are worker-private gains; without specifying who receives or loses the wage difference, this reduced-form congestion schedule does not determine aggregate social welfare.

For a separate intergenerational process, let centered log earnings obey x_{g+1}=ρx_g+ε_{g+1}, with |ρ|<1, innovations identically distributed and independent across generations and of earlier earnings, mean zero and variance σ². Under the stationary distribution, variance V solves V=ρ²V+σ², so V=σ²/(1−ρ²). A stationary construction is x_g=Σ_{j≥0}ρ^jε_{g−j}, convergent in mean square because the variances sum. Cov(x_{g+1},x_g)=ρV. With (ρ,σ²,parent measurement-error variance)=(3/5,16/25,1), V=1. If recorded parent earnings are x*=x+u, with independent mean-zero error of variance 1, the population least-squares slope is ρV/(V+1)=3/10. The true slope is 3/5. Thus (stationary variance,true slope,measured slope)=(1,3/5,3/10). Classical parent measurement error makes this example appear more mobile. An alternative process ρ=4/5, σ²=9/25 also has stationary variance 1 despite different persistence. Mobility and cross-sectional inequality cannot be ranked using one coefficient alone.

Exercises

Use the stated synthetic domains and explain assumptions with each calculation. An unsupported numerical answer is incomplete. Open design questions are graded by their stated criteria.

  1. In case 1 raise tuition from 10 to 15, keeping all earnings and the 10% discount rate. Find the net present value and the initial liquidity shortfall. Can a fairly priced loan fix both the liquidity and profitability problems?
  2. In case 2 add a direct encouragement earnings effect of 2 to every type. Calculate the reduced form, first stage and Wald ratio. Then suppose the direct effect γ is only known to lie in [0,2], holding these new observed moments fixed. Derive the identified interval for the complier training effect under a common additive direct effect. Distinguish it from a confidence interval.
  3. The case-2 encouragement expansion costs 11 real goods per added trainee instead of 6. Keep its causal real-output assumptions and all compliance behavior. Find the net resource effect and break-even per-place cost. Explain why the population ATE is the wrong benefit for this rollout.
  4. In case 3 set the wage floor to 14. Calculate employment, available labor, profit, worker surplus and total surplus under lowest-cost rationing. Then select the six workers with reservation costs uniformly spanning [6,12] instead; this is feasible from the supply schedule. Recalculate worker and total surplus. Explain what employment data alone fail to reveal.
  5. A worker with time limit T=10 chooses l=0 or l∈(0,10], utility c−l²/2−K if working and utility c otherwise, budget c=wl+a, wage w≥0 and fixed utility cost K=8. Derive participation and hours for all w, including the tie. Repeat for K=18. Explain intensive versus extensive responses.
  6. In the taste example raise d to 6. Find each group's employment and wage, actual monetary profit and subjective objective. Compare with d=0. Explain why the observed gap does not, without the maintained model, identify d.
  7. In the signal model replace test accuracy 3/4 by 1/2, then by 1, preserving priors and output levels. Derive wages after both possible signals in each group. Does the perfect-test result prove that any real-world test eliminates discrimination?
  8. In the synthetic callback experiment, compute the risk ratio and the approximate difference-in-proportions interval. A redesign sends four applications to each employer and retains only employers returning at least one call. Identify two separate inferential failures and a defensible remedy for each. Do not claim the original standard error remains valid.
  9. In case 5 set κ=12. Derive equilibrium migration, the fraction of high types moving, and destination earnings of each type at those prices. Explain the selection and indifference conditions. If a policy pays a moving subsidy of 1 funded by outside lump-sum taxation, find the new equilibrium migration and the total subsidy bill. Do not infer aggregate welfare without further accounts.
  10. Keep the true intergenerational process in case 5. Average two independent parent earnings measurements, each with independent error variance 1. Find the resulting population slope. Contrast independent child-outcome measurement error with parent-regressor error. Propose a design to evaluate a parental-income grant, identifying the target outcome, assignment, timing and one threat that a persistence regression does not resolve.
Full solutions and evaluation criteria
  1. The initial opportunity cost becomes 20+15=35. Thus Δ=−35+200/11+2000/121=-35/121. The cash shortfall is 5+15−12=8. A loan of 8 with repayment 8.8 at date 1 makes enrollment feasible, but a fair loan has zero net present value and does not reverse the negative investment ranking. A grant can make private enrollment attractive while transferring the financing cost to others; it does not prove positive social output net of resource cost. Credit for the answer requires separating the cash shortfall from the 35-unit opportunity cost.
  2. The observed reduced form is 4+2=6, the first stage remains 2/5, and their ratio is 15. With a common direct effect γ, 6=(2/5)τ_C+γ, so (Wald,identified lower,identified upper)=(15,10,15). The endpoints correspond to γ=2 and γ=0; every intermediate value is feasible under that maintained additive model. This is an assumption-indexed population identification interval, not a sampling confidence interval. Heterogeneous direct effects or altered compliance would require a new decomposition rather than substituting the same bound mechanically.
  3. Output rises by (2/5)10=4. Resources used rise by (2/5)11=22/5, so net resources are (net gain,break-even cost)=(-2/5,10). Only compliers change training status in this rollout. Multiplying the population ATE 13 by extra participation counts effects for always- and never-takers who do not change treatment. The negative resource result need not alone settle an explicitly distribution-weighted objective, but the original positive efficiency claim has reversed.
  4. At m=14, labor demand is 20−14=6 and available labor is 14−2=12, so six labor units are rationed out. Revenue is 120−18=102 and payroll 84, giving profit 18. With lowest-cost hires, opportunity cost is ∫₀⁶(2+x)dx=30, worker surplus is 54 and total surplus is 72. The alternative hired interval has opportunity cost ∫₄¹⁰(2+x)dx=54, worker surplus 30 and total surplus 48. The checked vector is (employment,supply,profit,worker low-cost,total low-cost,worker alternative,total alternative)=(6,12,18,54,72,30,48). Everyone hired in the alternative interval has reservation cost at most 12, below wage 14, so it is feasible. Identical observed employment and payroll can hide different forgone alternatives; the surplus ranking requires the hiring composition.
  5. Conditional on working, maximize wl−l²/2−K. Strict concavity gives l*=min{w,10} for w>0. For 0<w≤10 the optimized work gain over nonwork is w²/2−K. With K=8, stay out for w<4, work four hours or stay out at w=4, and work min{w,10} for w>4. For w>10 the gain is 10w−50−8>0, so there is no later exit. With K=18 the analogous threshold is w=6, with either zero or six hours at the tie. At w=0, positive work is strictly dominated. The checked thresholds are (threshold K8,threshold K18)=(4,6). Hours conditional on participation rise smoothly until the time limit, while employment jumps at a threshold. Changing K shifts participation without changing the interior conditional first-order condition.
  6. Group A remains L_A=4, w_A=8. Group B has L_B=(8−6)/2=1, w_B=5. Actual monetary profit is (12−8)4+(12−5)1=23; subtracting subjective penalty 6 gives objective 17. Thus (L_A,w_A,L_B,w_B,money profit,objective)=(4,8,1,5,23,17). At d=0 both groups have employment 4 and wage 8, with monetary and subjective profit 32. Unequal supply schedules, productivity beliefs, amenities or bargaining could generate a gap in another model, so an observed wage difference alone does not identify this taste parameter. Full credit distinguishes the employer's chosen objective from money profit and from social welfare.
  7. With accuracy 1/2 the signal is independent of type, so posteriors equal the priors after either signal. Wages are 8+8(3/4)=14 in A and 8+8(1/4)=10 in B for both signals. With accuracy 1 the high signal reveals high type and pays 16 in either group; the low signal reveals low type and pays 8. All signals have positive probability because each prior is interior. The result vector is (uninformative A,uninformative B,perfect high,perfect low)=(14,10,16,8). This removes group conditioning only under truthful perfect measurement, correct beliefs, competitive risk-neutral pay and no taste or institutional barriers. Real tests can have measurement error, differential access and predictive targets that differ from actual job productivity.
  8. The risk ratio is .12/.08=3/2; it means 50% more callbacks relative to the B baseline, whereas the absolute difference is four percentage points. SE=√(.1792/1000) and the approximate interval is (risk ratio,SE,lower,upper)=(3/2,0.0133865604245,0.0137623415680,0.0662376584320). Multiple applications within employer can share shocks and interfere; randomize and analyze at an appropriate employer level or use a design permitting the specified within-employer contrast with cluster-aware inference and explicit interference assumptions. Conditioning on at least one callback is conditioning on a post-assignment outcome, which can change composition and induce selection bias; retain the full randomized employer/application sample, including all-zero outcomes, for the prespecified intention-to-treat target. Full credit requires both dependence/interference and post-treatment selection, with separate remedies. Clustering alone does not fix selection.
  9. Only high types can benefit. Their condition is 3−12m, so interior equilibrium has m=1/4, half of the high types. Destination wages are 8−3=5 for low types and 16−3=13 for high types; low types strictly stay because 5−6−3=−4, while high movers and stayers are indifferent because 13−10−3=0. With subsidy 1, high-type net gain is 4−12m and m=1/3, two thirds of high types. Low-type gain is 2−3+1−12m=−12m<0 there. The checked vector is (m0,high share0,w_low0,w_high0,m1,bill)=(1/4,1/2,5,13,1/3,1/3). The bill is subsidy times total moving mass, 1/3, paid by outside taxpayers. Including that transfer, the wage-setting counterpart and real moving costs is necessary for a welfare comparison; it is not valid to count the subsidy as newly created output.
  10. Averaging two independent errors gives error variance 1/2. The parent slope is ρV/(V+1/2)=(3/5)/(3/2)=2/5, nearer to 3/5 but still attenuated. Independent mean-zero child measurement error increases outcome variance without changing the population slope on a correctly measured parent regressor; correlated or nonclassical error need not share that property. For a grant study, randomly assign an eligible parental population to a funded grant versus the stated control, prespecify child outcomes at comparable ages, follow everyone including nonworkers, and analyze the intention-to-treat effect with assignment-level inference. Monitor attrition, spillovers and concurrent services; for example, differential child follow-up related to treatment and earnings can invalidate the observed-outcome comparison. A persistence regression alone does not separate the grant mechanism from shared family determinants. Full credit requires assignment, target, timing and a concrete identification threat; a proposal may reasonably leave long-run effects uncertain.

Teach-back

Explain to a budget committee why a positive wage difference between graduates and nongraduates is insufficient to price a tuition policy. Use one dated budget, one complier equation and one equilibrium response. Then explain how a wage floor can raise employment in case 3 without implying that every wage floor does so.

Answer guide

The dated budget includes tuition and lost earnings, while a liquidity constraint can prevent a profitable choice. The encouragement ratio equals the effect for the workers whose schooling changes only under random assignment, exclusion and monotonicity; it does not automatically equal the population ATE. Expansion can alter wages, rents, available training and the identity of participants. In case 3, the floor initially reduces the employer's marginal expenditure wedge, but once the floor exceeds 11 it moves employment down the flat-wage demand branch. The committee needs evidence on those maintained mechanisms and on the actual financing and beneficiaries.

Delayed retention

After one week, without notes: (1) distinguish opportunity cost from a cash shortfall; (2) derive the Wald numerator using compliance types; (3) derive marginal uniform-wage expenditure; (4) name the treatment and outcome in a résumé experiment; (5) explain why a low parent-child slope need not imply low inequality.

Retention key

(1) Foregone earnings belong in the alternative comparison but are not necessarily a bill due at enrollment. (2) Randomization and exclusion give E[τ(D(1)−D(0))]; monotonicity leaves only compliers, so it equals P(C)E[τ|C]. (3) Differentiate w(L)L to obtain w+Lw′. (4) The assigned résumé cue is treatment and callback is the specified outcome; neither actual identity nor lifetime pay is randomized. (5) Stationary variance σ²/(1−ρ²) depends on innovations as well as persistence, and regressor measurement error can attenuate the observed slope.
