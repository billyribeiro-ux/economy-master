Capability and prerequisites

Analyze collective decisions and institutional incentives with defensible identification. Determine who can decide, what they know, what they want, what can be enforced and who pays. Prerequisite: international trade and global production, together with the earlier social-choice, contracts and causal-inference tools. This chapter uses concavity, Bayesian updating, sequential incentives and dated budgets. Every numerical institution is synthetic. No country, political party or current law is being assessed.

A policy is not implemented by its welfare derivative

A technically attractive reform can fail because no coalition supports its financing, an official cannot be rewarded for the relevant performance, an agency measures the wrong task, or the state cannot collect the promised funds. Political economy studies these constraints as part of the explanation. It does not replace every public objective with cynicism. People may value public outcomes, professional obligations, income, reputation and continued office in different combinations; specify which motives matter in the model before drawing a conclusion.

An institution comprises decision rights, information, feasible actions, enforcement and expectations about future behavior. Formal rules are one part; actual capacity to implement them is another. A majority vote, a bureaucratic contract and a regulatory hearing aggregate interests differently. “The government” is rarely a single actor with one budget and one information set. Citizens, elected officials, administrators, courts, firms and future taxpayers can face distinct constraints.

Keep positive and normative analysis separate. A political equilibrium predicts behavior given the rules. An efficient allocation maximizes an explicitly stated objective over feasible resources. A legitimate procedure concerns rights, participation and authority, which cannot be reduced to a profit first-order condition. These objects may coincide in a special model, but that coincidence must be proved. The examples below examine mechanisms without claiming that any one model is a complete theory of institutions.

1. Voting requires an agenda and a financing rule

For an odd number of voters choosing a scalar policy, suppose each has a unique peak and strictly prefers moving toward that peak on either side. Order the peaks and let the middle peak be M. Against any alternative below M, the middle voter and everyone with a peak at least M prefer M; they form a majority. Against any alternative above M, the analogous majority with peaks at most M prefers M. Thus M defeats every distinct alternative in pairwise sincere voting. The proof includes the median voter in both counts and accommodates coincident peaks. It requires a common one-dimensional ordering and single-peaked preferences on it.

This Condorcet property does not by itself prove that parties converge to M, that voters report truthfully under every mechanism, or that a multidimensional agenda has a majority winner. Those are different games. Turnout, franchise, candidate entry, agenda control and information can alter the relevant electorate or feasible alternatives. The theorem's role is conditional prediction, not a claim that majority choice is automatically socially optimal. The earlier social-choice source supplies the method context; the funded example makes its assumptions visible.

Worked case 1 — the median ideal is not the sum of marginal values

Three citizens each own 20 goods. Public-service quantity G∈[0,30] costs G goods and is financed by an enforceable equal tax G/3. Their utilities are 20−G/3+aᵢlog(1+G), with (a1,a2,a3)=(1,2,4). Each derivative is −1/3+aᵢ/(1+G), strictly decreasing through zero, so the unique peaks are (2,5,11), all inside the budget domain. By the preceding majority argument, G=5 defeats every other feasible scalar proposal. The government collects exactly five and spends five; private consumption is 55/3 per citizen.

With equal cardinal welfare weights, summed utility is 60−G+7log(1+G). Its derivative vanishes at G=6 and its second derivative is negative everywhere. Thus (median policy,utilitarian policy)=(5,6). The welfare gain from choosing six instead of five is 7log(7/6)−1, about .07905. Yet the middle voter loses log(7/6)·2−1/3<0; the higher aggregate value is not a Pareto gain. Voting counts people, while this welfare objective sums their stipulated marginal utilities. Unequal financing shares would change individual peaks and must be written into preferences before invoking the theorem.

Without single-peakedness, even three voters and three policies can cycle. Let rankings be A>B>C, B>C>A and C>A>B. A beats B, B beats C and C beats A by two votes each. An agenda choosing the order of pairwise votes can therefore affect the winner. This counterexample does not refute the median theorem; it violates the preference restriction that made its majority argument possible.

2. Accountability combines incentives with credible selection

An incumbent's behavior can respond to the prospect of remaining in office. Observed performance also teaches voters about persistent traits. The first mechanism is moral hazard, the second selection. A performance-contingent reelection rule cannot simply be asserted when voters would later prefer to ignore it. Solve the final decision using their information, then check the incumbent's earlier choice. Olken's political-agency lecture develops that distinction; this example supplies its own probabilities and complete sequential comparisons.

Worked case 2 — a more informative report changes both effort and selection

There are two service periods and an election between them. Citizens value a service success more than failure and, at the election, care only about the next period's success probability. A new challenger is an independent draw from three types: committed diligent, committed inactive and opportunistic, with masses (1/4,1/4,1/2). Committed types take high and low effort respectively at either date by assumption. An opportunist pays private utility cost k=1/10 for high effort, receives a nonpecuniary benefit R=1 from holding office in period 2, and discounts that benefit by β=3/4. Low effort costs zero. The benefit is not a public cash payment. There is no further election, so a period-2 opportunist chooses low effort. Service succeeds with pH=4/5 under high effort and pL=2/5 under low effort.

After period-1 service, voters see a binary report, correct with probability α∈(1/2,1], independently conditional on success/failure. Its probability of reporting good news under action e is q_e=(1−α)+(2α−1)p_e. A high report is more likely under high effort. Consider reelection after good news and replacement after bad news. An opportunist's gain from high effort is βR(qH−qL)−k; with the stated primitives high effort is optimal exactly when α≥2/3. Select high effort at equality. These are the only two actions, so this is a global incentive test.

At α=3/4, qH=13/20 and qL=9/20. Opportunists exert high effort, making the total high-effort mass 3/4 and good-report probability 3/5. Bayes' rule gives the incumbent's committed-diligent posterior 13/48 after good news and 7/32 after bad news. The challenger prior is 1/4. Since only committed diligent types will work hard in the final period, good news makes reelection strictly better and bad news makes replacement strictly better. Thus the proposed voting rule is sequentially credible, and the earlier incentive test is supported by actual later choices. The checked quantities are (pH,pL,beta,R,k,alpha)=(4/5,2/5,3/4,1,1/10,3/4) and (qH,qL,Prgood,posterior good,posterior bad,effort advantage)=(13/20,9/20,3/5,13/48,7/32,1/20).

At α=3/5, the effort advantage becomes −1/25, so opportunists choose low effort. Now the high-effort mass is only 1/4. Report probabilities are qH=14/25, qL=12/25, and good news has probability 1/2. The diligent posteriors are 7/25 and 11/50, again respectively above and below 1/4, so the same reelection rule remains credible. The period-1 service success probability falls from 7/10 to 1/2. However, the probability of success under the elected period-2 official is 101/200 with the more accurate report and 253/500 with the less accurate report. The latter is slightly larger: inducing opportunists to resemble diligent types can reduce selection quality. More informative signals can improve current incentives while changing whom voters can distinguish. These are equilibrium effects in this model, not a general ranking of transparency policies. A welfare comparison also needs report costs, both periods' benefits, utility weights and the officeholder's effort costs.

To verify credibility beyond the two calculations, let h denote the first-period high-effort mass, either 1/4 or 3/4. The good-report probability is g=hqH+(1−h)qL. For every α>1/2, qH>qL and h<1, so (1/4)qH/g>1/4. Likewise (1/4)(1−qH)/(1−g)<1/4. Both signals have positive probability for the stated service probabilities. This proves the voter rule at every permitted information set. A terminal ban on reelection removes this particular continuation incentive; it does not prove that all officials then stop working, because committed types and other motives remain.

3. Lobbying, capture and resources spent influencing a rule

Lobbying can convey useful information, represent dispersed interests, or seek private advantages at others' expense. Capture means that an institution's decisions become systematically aligned with a particular interest beyond its stated public mandate. Corruption, lawful lobbying, administrative error and mere disagreement about public objectives are different categories. A model of influence cannot establish a legal offense or diagnose a real person from a correlation.

Organizing a large group can be difficult when each member receives only a small share of the benefit and can free ride on others' contributions. A concentrated beneficiary may therefore invest more in influence than a dispersed group with a larger aggregate stake. But concentration alone does not establish which policy will prevail: information, rules, participation costs and countervailing interests matter. The following reduced-form contest makes one such mechanism explicit. Its influence probability is stipulated; we do not claim to derive all legislative bargaining from it.

Worked case 3 — a countermeasure changes the lobby's best response

A proposed restriction would give a firm 9 goods of net operating gain and impose 12 goods of loss on consumers, relative to an unrestricted benchmark. These amounts already include product-market transfers and real production changes, so their consolidated effect is −3. The firm spends l≥0 real goods on influence, with no productive spillover. The restriction passes with probability P=l/(1+l). Consumers do not organize in this baseline. Firm expected gain is 9l/(1+l)−l. Its derivative is 9/(1+l)²−1 and second derivative is strictly negative; it goes to minus infinity at large l. Thus the unique global choice is l=2, P=2/3, private gain 4. Consolidated welfare change is −3P−l=−4. The primitives and result are (firm benefit,consumer loss)=(9,12) and (effort,probability,private gain,welfare change)=(2,2/3,4,-4).

Now a public authority spends e=1 real good, funded by a lump-sum taxpayer payment, on a countermeasure changing the probability to l/(1+e+l). Taking e as fixed, the firm maximizes 9l/(2+l)−l. Its global response is l=√18−2, about 2.243; passage probability is 1−2/√18, about .529. Influence spending rises even though passage becomes less likely. The consolidated welfare change is −3P−l−1=−2−2√2, about −4.828. Thus this particular countermeasure reduces passage but worsens the stated welfare objective because endogenous influence and public spending rise. Counting the tax and its funded real spending as two distinct costs would be another error.

More generally with private gain B>0 and e≥0, the unique choice is max{√[B(1+e)]−(1+e),0}. This follows from strict concavity and the derivative at zero. A sufficiently large e≥B−1 deters influence entirely in this model, but its resource bill may be large. Optimizing the authority's complete policy would require comparing that boundary and the entire induced-response objective; choosing e=1 has not done so. The example establishes a counterexample to an automatic welfare inference from lower capture probability, not a recommendation to tolerate capture.

4. Bureaucracy: reward the actual task, account for what is measurable

A bureau often has several tasks and incomplete performance measures. A target can improve measured service while leaving unmeasured quality unchanged; with interacting tasks it can also divert attention. The precise effect depends on the effort-cost technology. Risk, wage floors, professional motivation and the ability to charge an entry fee change implementable contracts. The earlier contracts source provides bounded measurement context. Here we solve a deliberately simple contract class instead of importing a universal rule that bonuses are good or bad.

Worked case 4 — a missing quality measure is an incentive constraint

A risk-neutral official chooses quantity q and quality e, each in [0,3], at utility cost (q²+e²)/2. The public benefit is q+2e in a fixed money-equivalent unit. The official has initial wealth 10, normalized outside utility zero relative to retaining that wealth, and can make enforceable payments within that wealth. Treasury has 20 goods available for salary reserves. Initially only q is contractible, so offer wage w=A+bq with b≥0 and A unrestricted. Participation is in expected utility before effort and any signal. The salary is a transfer between treasury and official; effort disutility and any audit resources are separate.

Global effort choice gives q=min{b,3}, e=0 by strict concavity. For b∈[0,3], choosing A=−b²/2 makes participation bind; expected salary is b²/2 and the treasury's net benefit is b−b²/2. Its maximum is b=1, A=−1/2, q=1,e=0, salary1/2 and net benefit1/2. For b>3 the minimum participating intercept is A=9/2−3b, leaving salary9/2 at q=3 and net benefit −3/2. Thus no omitted high-b contract improves the solution. At b=0, A=0 implements no effort and zero benefit. The entry fee implicit in negative A is feasible only because it is permitted; removing that institution changes the answer.

If both efforts were verifiable, direct choice maximizes q+2e−(q²+e²)/2, uniquely at (1,2), with cost5/2 and net benefit5/2. Now add a contractible audit z=e+η, where η=−1 or1 with equal probability independently of effort and the official is risk neutral. It costs 2/5 real goods per case. Use w=A+bq+dz, b,d≥0. On interior efforts the official globally chooses(q,e)=(b,d); the fixed payment A=−(b²+d²)/2 enforces participation. Selecting (b,d,A)=(1,2,−5/2) implements the full-information effort. Actual audit signals are1 or3, salaries1/2 or9/2, mean5/2. Net public benefit after salary and audit cost is 21/10. The results are (q-only quantity,q-only quality,q-only net,audited quantity,audited quality,mean wage,audited net)=(1,0,1/2,1,2,5/2,21/10).

Feasibility is not only an expectation: over all allowed efforts and signals, this offered audited contract pays between −9/2 and17/2. The official's wealth10 covers the most negative payment and treasury's wealth20 covers the largest positive payment plus the audit. Treasury can reserve17/2 before service; unused funds remain its property. Expected transfers cancel when official and taxpayer utilities are consolidated. Risk aversion, limited liability, manipulable audits or an effort-dependent signal bias would require a different optimal contract. This separable-cost example establishes missing incentives for quality; it does not prove quantity bonuses crowd quality out of an initially positive level.

5. Capacity is an investment, not a line in a statute

A legal tax rate does not ensure the information, staffing, courts and payment systems needed to collect it. Likewise legal rights require practical enforcement to affect contracts. Capacity is multidimensional: collecting revenue, delivering services and restraining arbitrary exercise of authority need not move together. The model of Besley and Persson treats institutional capacity as an investment constraining later policies. We use that distinction without adopting its broader comparative-statics or empirical claims.

Worked case 5 — maximizing spendable receipts is not maximizing welfare

A two-date revenue office chooses capacity k≥0 at date0 and a tax rate t∈[0,1] at date1. Each unit k costs one good immediately, financed by a safe outside loan at gross return one. Capacity makes fraction ρ=k/(1+k) of a statutory revenue base collectible. Conditional on t, the taxable base is 100(1−t), a stipulated reduced-form response, so collections are T=100ρt(1−t). Noncollected tax liabilities are not destroyed goods. No other tax, loan or initial government wealth exists. The office maximizes date-1 funds left after repaying the capacity loan, G=T−k, with G≥0; public spending then uses G goods. The primitives are (base scale,cost per capacity,gross loan return)=(100,1,1).

For every k>0, the unique maximizing tax rate is t=1/2. Substituting gives G(k)=25k/(1+k)−k, with derivative25/(1+k)²−1 and negative second derivative. The derivative starts positive and eventually negative, and G tends to minus infinity. Its unique global optimum is k=4, t=1/2, ρ=4/5, collections20 and spendable funds16. Hence (k,t,rho,collections,G)=(4,1/2,4/5,20,16). At k=0 every tax rate yields zero and G=0, worse than the positive optimum. Date0 the lender transfers four goods to pay the real capacity input; date1 collections twenty repay four and fund service sixteen. The safe loan is genuinely repayable in this deterministic model, not financed by its own accounting label.

This is the revenue office's objective, not a social-welfare theorem. The reduced taxable-base equation does not reveal how much response is lost real production versus relabeling, leisure or avoidance resources. Welfare also needs the benefits of G, private tax incidence, lenders' opportunity cost and distributional weights. An office paid on receipts alone might overtax relative to that objective. Conversely a low receipt rate may reflect a chosen low statutory tax, not low capacity. Distinguishing statutory base, effective collection, real costs and service outcomes is essential before comparing institutions.

6. Evidence about institutions must identify the changed mechanism

Institutions are chosen partly in response to the problems they are meant to solve. Comparing places with different enforcement or electoral rules can therefore mix treatment with selection, historical conditions and measurement. A credible design names the intervention, the unit exposed, timing, counterfactual and outcome. Randomized audit timing can identify the effect of receiving an audit under its design; it does not automatically identify a country's latent “corruption level” or the effect of an entirely different enforcement regime. Close-election discontinuities require local continuity and no precise manipulation; they may identify a local officeholder effect while saying little about changing the constitution.

Reported funds, independently verified deliveries, service quality and perceptions measure different objects. A discrepancy can reflect timing, prices, legitimate deductions, recording error or diversion. Reconcile the accounts and investigate the mechanism before naming it. The measurement lecture motivates this distinction between methods and interpretations; none of its country-specific estimates is imported here.

For implementation, document legal authority, decision rights, appeals, staffing, data access and a funded operating budget. Define who can verify the performance measure and how errors can be challenged. Preserve prospective outcomes that could disconfirm the favored mechanism, including displaced activity outside the measured program. If a reform changes entry, effort or reporting, evaluate those responses rather than holding them fixed without justification. A well-fitted score is not evidence that the institution will retain its behavior after incentives change.

Full credit requires assignment, measurement, spillovers, resources, sample selection and a concrete revision rule. These proposed checks do not establish diversion, legal wrongdoing or a welfare gain until evidence supports the relevant mechanisms and accounting.

Teach-back

Explain why a majority winner, an incentive-compatible bureaucracy and a revenue-maximizing administration can each fail to maximize the same social objective. Use one equation and identify the relevant missing assumption for each.

Answer guide

The median peak counts ordered individual preferences, whereas a utilitarian derivative sums their cardinal marginal values under a funding rule. A measured-task contract implements only rewards linked to contractible information; participation, risk and payment constraints bound it. A revenue office maximizes collections minus administration, omitting taxpayers' utility and service benefits. State the objective and feasible institution before comparing any of them to a planner.

Delayed retention

After a week, without notes: prove the median majority comparison; state the election incentive inequality and the need for sequentially credible voting; distinguish influence transfers from real costs; explain why an audit can fail to be worth its cost; and identify the missing welfare objects in a revenue-capacity calculation.

Retention key

A majority whose peaks lie at or above the median prefers it to any lower policy, with the symmetric comparison above. Office incentives require βR(qH−qL)≥k and actual voter preference for the promised later choice, derived from posteriors. Transfers cancel only after their recipients and payers are consolidated; resources consumed in influence remain costs. Audit information can improve incentives while consuming more resources than the resulting gain. Revenue alone omits public benefits, private incidence, behavioral resource effects, financing opportunity cost and welfare weights.

Source scope and extension

Two previously accepted primary method scopes are reused, and three new bounded text readings supply political-agency, measurement and capacity context. No source problem, historical estimate or current legal rule is reproduced. The chapter proves its small institutions, not a general result about the desirability of a regime, an interest group or a national reform. Economic history next asks how to investigate institutions and transformations when the records themselves are incomplete and the counterfactual cannot be randomized.
