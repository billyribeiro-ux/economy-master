A wrong answer is an observation about one response. A useful diagnosis explains which reasoning may have produced it and proposes a task that could distinguish that explanation from its rivals.

Capability. Diagnose a misunderstanding using actual reasoning, design corrective practice and fresh retrieval, and interpret delayed learning evidence without mistaking a score change for a causal effect of instruction. Prerequisites are analytical case discussion and grading, feedback and remediation. Conditional probability and weighted growth are restated below. All learner responses, rosters and teaching schedules are original synthetic illustrations; none is an observed classroom result.

1. Diagnose a process, not a person

A misconception is an incorrect relationship or rule that a learner uses to reason. A single wrong number may instead reflect an arithmetic slip, ambiguous wording, a misread unit, a missing prerequisite or a transient lapse. Call a misunderstanding persistent only when relevant evidence shows recurrence across appropriately changed tasks and conditions. Even then, describe the observed reasoning and scope rather than assigning a fixed label to the learner.

A diagnostic question is designed so that the response helps choose the next teaching action. Ask for the denominator, a diagram or a reason, not only a final option. A distractor can embody a plausible error, but choosing it does not uniquely reveal its cause. Ask a short discriminating follow-up. For example, a learner who writes the correct fraction but divides badly needs different practice from one who selects the wrong conditioning group. A correct number with an invalid reason also warrants investigation.

CMU's guidance on performance-based prior-knowledge assessment recommends tasks tied to needed skills and treats these diagnostic assessments as ungraded opportunities to inform teaching. We use that bounded professional advice here. The numerical coding of responses below is for examining evidence, not a recommendation to penalize a learner for revealing a gap. Our invented questions are not validated concept inventories.

Keep four entries in a diagnostic note: the exact task and conditions; the response evidence; at least one rival explanation; and the next probe with the response that would change the diagnosis. Avoid “does not understand probability” when the actual evidence is “used all defaulters as denominator after being asked about flagged applicants.” That narrower description gives an instructor something concrete to test.

2. Retrieval, correction and delayed evidence serve different purposes

Retrieval practice asks the learner to produce previously studied knowledge or a method without first seeing its answer. Recognition selects among supplied possibilities; it can be useful, but may expose more cues. Retention evidence comes from performance after a delay under documented conditions. Transfer evidence concerns use when a consequential feature changes, such as the relevant population or an external cash contribution. Neither is established merely because an answer key is available.

The September 2007 IES practice guide Organizing Instruction and Study to Improve Student Learning recommends revisiting content through active quizzing and spacing study. Its official recommendation page labels content re-exposure through quizzes strong evidence and spacing moderate evidence; pre-questions and quizzes used to identify study needs have different, minimal-evidence labels there. These are the guide's scoped synthesis judgments, not effect sizes or proof that our exact economics questions, intervals or learners will benefit. We do not infer an optimal universal delay or import uninspected linked studies.

Our proposed cycle is: attempt privately without the answer; record the reasoning; compare with a correct explanation; repair the specific error; later attempt a changed task independently. Return corrective feedback after the attempt, and ask the learner to explain the correction. Provide a route to disclose uncertainty rather than encouraging repeated confident guessing. An open-key correction is useful practice; retain its assisted status. If the outcome of interest is independent retention, collect the delayed response before revealing that task's key. These choices are original protocol proposals, not already tested interventions.

Performance is what happened on a particular task under particular conditions. Improved delayed performance can be evidence consistent with learning if the task samples the intended capability, but task difficulty, cues and assistance may differ. A causal instructional effect compares outcomes under alternative instructional conditions for a defined target and requires an identification argument. One group's before/after improvement does not supply the missing counterfactual.

3. Worked diagnosis: reverse the condition, change the denominator

Case 1 — A screening rate is not a default rate among flags

Complete diagnostic task. In a fictional completed cohort of 1,000 loan applicants, all default outcomes and screening flags are known. Fifty applicants defaulted; the screen flagged 40 of those 50. Of the 950 who did not default, it flagged 95. An analyst says, “The screen caught 80% of defaults, so 80% of flagged applicants defaulted.” Is the conclusion correct? Construct a two-by-two count table, calculate the two conditional proportions, and explain the conditioning groups. Inputs are (1000,50,40,95), ordered total, defaults, flagged defaults and flagged nondefaults. This is retrospective accounting within the supplied cohort, not a validated forecast or a recommendation about actual credit decisions.

Full answer. Here P denotes the proportion for a uniformly drawn member of this complete cohort. P(flag|default)=40/50=4/5, but P(default|flag)=40/135=8/27, approximately 29.63%. The result vector is (4/5,8/27). The same numerator appears in both because it is the intersection; the denominator changes from the default row to the flagged column. The cohort's default share is 1/20 and its false-flag proportion among nondefaults is 1/10. For prevalence b, true-flag rate a and false-flag rate f, the flagged fraction is ab+f(1−b), so

P(default|flag)=ab/[ab+f(1−b)].

This follows by partitioning flags into default and nondefault cases, then dividing the default-and-flagged share by all flags. Require a,b,f∈[0,1] and a positive denominator. With no flags, the conditional proportion is undefined, not zero. For the given cohort, the formula yields (4/5)(1/20)/[(4/5)(1/20)+(1/10)(19/20)]=8/27. It does not estimate a causal effect of flagging, or justify transporting the proportion to another applicant population.

Full synthetic learner response. “Forty of fifty is eighty percent. The question asks how often a flag is right, so I use the given eighty percent. The 95 other flags do not matter because they did not default.” This response uses the default row after the question switches to the flagged column. It supports a provisional diagnosis of reversed conditioning or omission of false flags, but it does not prove that the learner has a persistent inability to reason probabilistically.

4. Worked diagnosis and evidence audit: average the right quantities

Case 2 — Two accounts, one aggregate growth rate

Complete diagnostic task. Two accounts begin a period at 100 and 900 currency units. Their simple returns over that same period are 20% and 0%, respectively. There are no deposits, withdrawals, transfers, fees or other cash flows. Calculate each ending balance and the growth rate of total wealth. A learner says, “There are two accounts, so the return is (20%+0%)/2=10%; total wealth becomes 1,100.” Evaluate the reasoning. Inputs are ((100,1/5),(900,0)).

Full answer and derivation. The accounts end at 120 and 900, for total 1,020 versus initial 1,000; growth is 20/1,000=1/50=2%. Let initial balances Vᵢ≥0 have positive sum V, and let same-period simple returns be rᵢ≥−1. Without external cash flows, ending total is ΣVᵢ(1+rᵢ), so aggregate growth is

[ΣVᵢ(1+rᵢ)−ΣVᵢ]/ΣVᵢ=Σ(Vᵢ/V)rᵢ.

The weights are initial wealth shares, here 1/10 and 9/10, not equal account counts. The checked vector is (120,900,1000,1020,1/50). An equal-account mean is a different statistic. It matches this aggregate formula when the balances are equal, or can coincide accidentally for particular returns; equality on one example does not validate an unconditional averaging rule.

Full synthetic learner response and rival diagnoses. “Both accounts belong to the same person and there are two rates, so each rate gets one vote. The larger account cannot matter because a percentage already adjusts for size.” This explicitly treats a rate as independent of the monetary amount it applies to. A different learner might know the weighting rule but misread 900 as 100, or might be answering the equal-account-average question instead of the wealth-growth question. Ask: “How many currency units does each account gain?” The correct answer is 20 and 0. Then ask: “What total did the 20-unit increase grow from?” The answer is 1,000. If the learner supplies those values but divides by 200, inspect the chosen denominator; if they restate the target as average account performance, clarify the requested statistic.

Feedback, repair and boundary probe. “The 20% applies to only 100 units, giving 20 units of gain. Add monetary gains before dividing by the initial total. Write the 100 and 900 above the two rates so their weights remain visible.” Full correction: “The gain is 100×0.2+900×0=20; initial wealth is 1,000, so its growth is 2%, even though the equal-account mean is 10%.” Next ask what happens with initial balances 500 and 500 at the same returns. Ending balances are 600 and 500; total 1,100 and growth 10%. Explain why this boundary agreement cannot distinguish a correct weighting method from the original erroneous general rule.

Fresh retrieval and transfer. Independently use starting balances 250 and 750 with returns −20% and +20%, no cash flows: ((250,-1/5),(750,1/5)). Endings are 200 and 900, so total growth is 100/1,000=1/10, although the equal-account mean is zero: (200,900,1/10,0). A second transfer asks: “Return to the original 100/900 accounts, but add 100 of external money after returns are earned at period end. Does the ending total 1,120 imply a 12% investment return?” Answer: the total-balance increase is 12%, but removing that known end contribution leaves investment growth (1,120−100−1,000)/1,000=2%. The two quantities are (3/25,1/50). Contributions at unspecified earlier dates would require a more carefully defined return measure; do not use this end-flow shortcut without the timing condition.

Delayed task and full key. At a proposed fourteen-day check without the worked answer, supply accounts 300 and 100 returning +10% and −10%, with no external flows: ((300,1/10),(100,-1/10)). Endings are 330 and 90, total 420 against 400, so growth is 1/20=5%; the equal-account mean is zero. Checked values are (330,90,1/20). Use the same two-point evidence rule on the original and delayed tasks: 1 for correct monetary aggregation with initial and ending totals, and 1 for the correct total-growth denominator/result with an explanation of the wealth weights. Immediate corrected practice uses that rule too but retains its assisted label. Equal point scales and a common blueprint do not prove equal item difficulty.

5. What would strengthen a learning claim?

Before looking at a score change, specify the target capability, scoring rule, task versions, elapsed time, aids and exposure. Retain denominators and missingness. Inspect whether an old example was rehearsed or the later task changed the reasoning demand. A matched task blueprint supports a comparison but does not establish empirical equivalence. A delayed unfamiliar application under similar independent conditions is more directly relevant to retention and transfer than repeating an exposed answer; the learner's work still has to be observed.

The missing-score argument generalizes. If S is the observed sum for m of N learners, N>0, and every missing outcome is in [L,U] with no other joint restriction, the full mean is bounded by (S+(N−m)L)/N and (S+(N−m)U)/N. Summing the individual bounds proves necessity; setting every missing outcome to L or U proves endpoint attainability. These are descriptive support bounds. Calling them “95% confidence” would require an additional probability argument that this accounting does not supply.

For a causal comparison, define instructional packages and the relevant counterfactual. Consider a hypothetical design that uniformly assigns four of eight learners to a retrieval-plus-feedback package and four to an equal-time restudy package. Assume no spillovers, complete follow-up, stable treatment versions and the same independent delayed task and scoring conditions. Let Zᵢ indicate retrieval-package assignment and Yᵢ(1),Yᵢ(0) be fixed potential delayed scores. The difference in group means is (1/4)Σ[ZᵢYᵢ(1)−(1−Zᵢ)Yᵢ(0)]. Each learner has assignment probability 1/2, so its expectation is (1/8)Σ[Yᵢ(1)−Yᵢ(0)], the eight-learner average package effect. This proof uses linearity of expectation, not independence of the fixed-size assignment indicators.

A realized difference need not equal that average effect exactly. Randomization does not make an eight-person demonstration representative of all learners, establish a p-value without a specified analysis, or isolate retrieval from any other difference between the packages. If feedback differs too, the estimand concerns the combined package. The original six-person register has no such assignment and cannot inherit this argument. Any real study requires appropriate authorization, consent and design review; the hypothetical protocol records no actual intervention.
