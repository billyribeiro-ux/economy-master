General equilibrium and existence

A household can choose optimally at quoted prices while its proposed purchases cannot be supplied. A firm can maximize profit while no buyer can finance its output. General equilibrium asks whether one price system can make all those choices compatible, including the ownership income that pays for demand. Existence is a theorem about that compatibility. It does not identify which equilibrium will occur or how a market reaches it.

Capability. Reconcile exchange and production accounts; prove the finite-dimensional chain from Sperner’s lemma to Brouwer’s and Kakutani’s fixed-point theorems; apply it to a precisely specified private-ownership economy; and diagnose failures of existence, uniqueness and iterative convergence. Six worked cases and twelve solved exercises connect the proof to quantities and counterexamples.

Prerequisites. Preference representation and choice supplies continuous utility and convex preferences; Consumer and producer duality supplies budget and profit problems. Uncertainty and information distinguishes a deterministic bundle from a lottery over bundles. We use finite-dimensional compactness, continuity and convexity from convex optimization and real analysis. No fixed-point theorem or Nash existence theorem is assumed in the proof chain below.

1. Choices, ownership and the accounts that must add up

There are finitely many goods ℓ=1,…,L, households i=1,…,I and firms j=1,…,J, with I≥1 and possibly J=0. Household i consumes xi∈ℝ+L and owns endowment ωi. A firm’s net supply yj has negative input components and positive output components. Its technology is Yj. All quantities refer to the same declared commodity, date and location; the static model has no unrecorded borrowing or transfers. Let θij≥0 be household i’s share of firm j, with Σiθij=1 for each firm.

At a nonzero price vector p≥0, define profit, household income and aggregate excess demand by

πj(p)=maxy∈Yj p·y,   mi(p)=p·ωi+Σjθijπj(p),
z(x,y)=Σixi−Σiωi−Σjyj.

A competitive equilibrium consists of prices and choices such that every firm maximizes profit, every household chooses a most-preferred bundle among x≥0 with p·x≤mi(p), and z=0. The maximum notation presumes existence, which must be established under the relevant assumptions. At prices with multiple optima, demand and supply are sets, called correspondences, rather than single-valued functions.

Walras accounting. At actual profit-maximizing supplies p·yj=πj(p). Summing the household budget inequalities and using the ownership sums gives

p·Σixi≤p·Σiωi+Σjπj(p)=p·(Σiωi+Σjyj),
so p·z≤0.

If all budgets are exhausted, equality gives Walras law, p·z=0. This is a value identity, not the vector equality z=0. With positive prices, clearing all but one market and Walras law clears the last; a zero price prevents that inference for its commodity. Omitting distributed profits or counting input purchases twice breaks the accounting rather than refuting the identity.

Multiplying every price by t>0 multiplies profits and incomes by t and leaves all affordable sets and maximizers unchanged. Thus normalize prices to the simplex Δ={p≥0:Σℓpℓ=1}. In two-good calculations with p₂>0 it is often easier to set p₂=1; that is the same relative-price information in a different normalization. An existence proof must still handle, or exclude, the boundary of Δ.

Worked case 1: exchange, income feedback and a new endowment

Two households have endowments ((4,1),(1,4)) and Cobb–Douglas first-good shares (2/3,1/3): u₁=x₁2/3x₂1/3 and u₂=x₁1/3x₂2/3. Write p=(r,1), r>0. At wealth m>0 and share 0<α<1, a bundle with both goods positive is feasible and has positive utility; an axis bundle has zero utility. An optimum is therefore interior and spends m. Maximizing α log x₁+(1−α)log x₂ on the budget gives x₁=αm/r and x₂=(1−α)m. The logarithm is strictly concave on the positive orthant, so its stationary budget solution is the unique global optimum.

Here m₁=4r+1 and m₂=r+4. Total first-good demand is (2/3)(4r+1)/r+(1/3)(r+4)/r=3+2/r. Supply is 5, so the unique positive relative price is 1. Incomes are (5,5), and household allocations are ((10/3,5/3),(5/3,10/3)). Each bundle costs its owner’s income; aggregate consumption is (5,5). The second market clears directly and also follows from Walras law.

Now give household 1 one more unit of the second good, making its endowment (4,2). Wealth changes with the equilibrium price: m₁=4r+2, m₂=r+4. First-good demand is 3+8/(3r), so r=4/3. Incomes become (22/3,16/3), and allocations become ((11/3,22/9),(4/3,32/9)), summing to (5,6). Holding incomes at their old values would miss the endowment-price feedback.

No zero-price equilibrium was omitted. If one good is free, the strictly positive endowment supplies positive income in the other good. Buy a positive amount of that priced good and increase the free good without bound; utility has no finite maximum. The direct argument therefore establishes uniqueness for each of these two economies. Cobb–Douglas preferences are weakly monotone on the whole orthant, but an increase along an axis can leave utility zero. These cases are proved directly; they are not applications of the stronger global strict-coordinate-monotonicity theorem in Section 6.

Worked case 2: production profits belong in somebody’s budget

The goods are input/leisure and consumption output. Each household owns (2,1/2) and has utility √(x₁x₂). Firm ownership shares are (1/4,3/4). The firm has compact generator K={ (−n,q):0≤n≤4, 0≤q≤2√n }, followed by free disposal: Y=K−ℝ+2. K is compact and contains zero. It is convex because the hypograph of the concave function 2√n is convex; mapping n to −n preserves convexity. Its capacity is 4.

Normalize the output price to 1 and write the input price as w>0. Profit maximization sets q=2√n. With t=√n∈[0,2], profit is 2t−wt². Completing the square gives an unconstrained maximizer t=1/w. Thus for w>1/2, input n=1/w², output q=2/w and profit π=1/w. For 0<w≤1/2, capacity binds: (n,q)=(4,4) and π=4−4w. In this capacity branch the available leisure is zero, whereas positive wealth makes both households demand positive leisure. It cannot be an equilibrium.

In the interior branch total income is 4w+1+1/w. Half is spent on leisure, so its market equation is (4w+1+1/w)/(2w)=4−1/w². Equivalently 4w²−w−3=0, with roots (1,−3/4). The sole admissible wage is 1. Net supply is (−1,2), profit 1, and individual incomes are 2w+1/2+θiπ, hence (11/4,13/4). Demands are ((11/8,11/8),(13/8,13/8)). Their sum (3,3) equals the aggregate endowment (4,1) plus net supply (−1,2). This accounts for leisure consumed, input used, output produced and distributed profit exactly once.

Give each household half the firm. Total income and the common expenditure shares stay the same, so wage and production remain unchanged, while incomes become (3,3) and allocations ((3/2,3/2),(3/2,3/2)). Ownership affects distribution here. Its lack of a price effect relies on the identical expenditure shares; it is not a general ownership-irrelevance theorem. If profits are instead omitted, aggregate demand at w=1 is (5/2,5/2) against resources (3,3), giving p·z=−1. A unit of value has disappeared from household budgets.

At zero wage, positive income in output permits unbounded desired free leisure. At zero output price and a positive wage, households can buy positive leisure and increase free output without limit. Those prices give no household optimum. As in case 1, the boundary behavior of √(x₁x₂) is handled directly rather than attributed to the later stronger preference assumption.

2. A combinatorial route to a fixed point

Existence arguments need not specify an adjustment process. The proof begins with a finite counting result and then passes to a limit. For integer d≥0 let Δd={x∈ℝd+1:xi≥0, Σi=0dxi=1}, the d-dimensional simplex with vertices e₀,…,ed. A face is the convex hull of a subset of the vertices. A compatible triangulation is a finite subdivision into d-simplices whose union is Δd, whose intersections are common faces, and whose restrictions to original faces are triangulations there. The mesh is the largest diameter of a small simplex.

Why arbitrarily fine compatible triangulations exist. Let T have vertices v₁,…,vd+1. For each ordering i₁,…,id+1, form the chain Fk={vi₁,…,vik} and the simplex with vertices b(Fk), their barycentres. To see coverage, express x=Σλivi, sort λi₁≥⋯≥λid+1≥λid+2=0, and set

μk=k(λik−λik+1),   k=1,…,d+1.
μk≥0,   Σkμk=Σiλi=1,   x=Σkμkb(Fk).

The last identity follows because the coefficient of vir on its right is Σk≥rμk/k=λir. The chain’s barycentres are affinely independent: their coordinates in the ordered vertices have triangular columns with nonzero diagonal. Ties among adjacent λ’s set the corresponding μ’s to zero. The nonzero μ’s identify exactly the vertex sets above each strict coordinate drop. Thus two ordering regions meet in the face generated by their common surviving barycentres. If x lies on an original face, all outside λ’s are zero and only barycentres of that face survive. Subdividing adjacent simplices consequently agrees on their shared face. This proves the required coverage and compatibility, not just a picture in dimension two.

For nested nonempty faces F⊂G, b(G)=(|F|/|G|)b(F)+(1−|F|/|G|)b(G∖F). Hence

‖b(G)−b(F)‖≤(1−|F|/|G|) diam(T)≤[d/(d+1)] diam(T).

Every pair of vertices in a new simplex comes from nested faces. A convex hull has diameter no greater than the largest distance between its vertices, so each barycentric subdivision contracts mesh by at most d/(d+1) for d≥1. Repetition gives mesh tending to zero. For d=0 the simplex is a single point.

Sperner’s lemma. Label each triangulation vertex by one index in {0,…,d}, requiring that coordinate vi=0 forbids label i. Then the number of d-simplices carrying all d+1 labels is odd, and in particular at least one exists.

Proof by dimension and parity. For d=0 there is one vertex and its sole permitted label is 0. For d≥1, mark each (d−1)-facet whose vertices carry all labels 0,…,d−1. Count incidences between marked facets and d-simplices. A fully labeled d-simplex contributes one marked facet, obtained by deleting the vertex labeled d. A simplex using exactly labels 0,…,d−1, with one of them repeated, contributes two. All other simplices contribute zero. The incidence total modulo two is therefore the number of fully labeled d-simplices modulo two.

An interior facet has exactly two incident d-simplices and a boundary facet has one. To justify the incidence fact, take a point in the relative interior of the facet and a sufficiently small ball avoiding every other lower-dimensional face. The facet divides the ball into two sides. Coverage supplies a cell on each available side, while disjoint cell interiors permit at most one on each side; on the outside boundary only one side is available. Thus interior contributions are even. A marked boundary facet cannot lie on an original face opposite any i<d, because that face forbids label i. It must lie on the face opposite d. The restricted triangulation of that face satisfies the (d−1)-dimensional labeling rule, and by induction its number of marked facets is odd. The incidence total, and hence the number of fully labeled d-cells, is odd. This completes the induction.

The inspected Mathew note, sections 1–2 illustrates the two-dimensional parity and limiting ideas. The arbitrary-dimensional subdivision, mesh bound and induction needed here have been proved explicitly above. No claim about the note’s later valuation material is needed.

Worked case 3: a finite labeling certificate

On Δ² take f(x₀,x₁,x₂)=(x₁,x₂,x₀). At a nonfixed vertex label by the smallest i with vi>fi(v). Use the corners and edge midpoints in the order ((1,0,0),(0,1,0),(0,0,1),(1/2,1/2,0),(1/2,0,1/2),(0,1/2,1/2)). Number them 0,…,5. The four small cells are ((0,3,4),(1,3,5),(2,4,5),(3,4,5)).

Vertexv−f(v)Label
e₀(1,0,−1)0
e₁(−1,1,0)1
e₂(0,−1,1)2
m₀₁(0,1/2,−1/2)1

The label vector is (0,1,2,1,0,2). Only the central cell (3,4,5) is fully labeled. The numbers of marked {0,1} edges incident to the four cells are (2,0,0,1). Their total is odd: an interior marked edge contributes twice and a boundary marked edge once. The central centroid is (1/3,1/3,1/3); direct substitution shows it is the fixed point of this particular linear cycle. Generally a fully labeled cell is a finite certificate used in a limiting argument, not a claim that its centroid is an exact fixed point.

3. Brouwer’s theorem, including the compact-convex extension

Theorem. Every continuous f:Δd→Δd has a fixed point. For d=0 this is immediate. For d≥1 take the sequence of compatible triangulations just constructed. If a vertex v ever satisfies f(v)=v, the proof is finished. Otherwise Σi[vi−fi(v)]=0 and not all terms vanish, so at least one term is strictly positive. Label v by one such coordinate, for definiteness the smallest. A zero coordinate cannot receive its own label, since fi(v)≥0.

Sperner gives a full cell at each subdivision level n, with a vertex vn,i labeled i for every i. Compactness gives a subsequence of vn,0 converging to x. Since the mesh tends to zero, every vn,i along this same subsequence converges to x. The labeling inequalities and continuity give xi≥fi(x) for every i. Both coordinate sums equal 1, so every inequality is equality: f(x)=x. The chosen cells need not be nested. Compact subsequences and vanishing diameters are sufficient.

Projection lemma. If K⊂ℝn is nonempty compact and convex, every z∈ℝn has a unique nearest point PK(z). Existence follows by minimizing the continuous squared distance on compact K. If distinct a,b were both minimizers, their midpoint would be feasible and

‖z−(a+b)/2‖²=[‖z−a‖²+‖z−b‖²]/2−‖a−b‖²/4

would be strictly smaller. Write a=PK(z). For y∈K, the segment a+t(y−a) is feasible for 0≤t≤1. The right derivative at t=0 of its squared distance from z is nonnegative, giving (z−a)·(y−a)≤0. For b=PK(z′), apply this inequality with y=b and the analogous inequality for z′ with y=a. Adding and rearranging yields

‖a−b‖²≤(z−z′)·(a−b)≤‖z−z′‖‖a−b‖.

Thus ‖PK(z)−PK(z′)‖≤‖z−z′‖, including a=b. Projection is continuous; uniqueness and continuity have not been presumed.

To extend Brouwer, choose a>0 so K⊂[−a,a]n. For n≥1 the simplex with vertices v₀=−a1 and vi=v₀+2na ei, i=1,…,n, contains that cube: every cube point has nonnegative coordinates xi+a whose sum is at most 2na. A continuous map f:K→K extends to the simplex by g(z)=f(PK(z)). Its values lie in K and hence in the simplex. The simplex theorem, transported by affine coordinates, gives z=g(z). Since g(z)∈K, z∈K and PK(z)=z, so f(z)=z. For n=0 there is one point. We have proved Brouwer on every nonempty compact convex finite-dimensional set, even one with empty ambient interior.

4. From a continuous function to a correspondence

A correspondence F:K⇒K assigns a subset F(x) of K to each x. Its graph is {(x,y):y∈F(x)}. It is upper hemicontinuous at x if, for every open set O containing F(x), all values F(z) for z sufficiently close to x are contained in O. This prevents new distant choices from appearing near x. It does not require any arbitrarily chosen single-valued selection to be continuous.

Graph qualification. With compact range K and closed values, closed graph is equivalent to upper hemicontinuity. If a closed graph failed upper hemicontinuity, some open O⊃F(x) would admit xn→x and yn∈F(xn)∖O. Compactness supplies ynk→y; the complement of O is closed, so y∉O, whereas the closed graph gives y∈F(x), a contradiction. Conversely, if xn→x and yn→y with yn∈F(xn) but y∉F(x), compact closed F(x) has positive distance from y. An open neighborhood of F(x) separated from a small ball around y contradicts upper hemicontinuity for sufficiently large n. The compact-range hypothesis in the first direction cannot silently be dropped.

Kakutani’s theorem. If K is a nonempty compact convex subset of a finite-dimensional Euclidean space, F has a closed graph and every F(x) is nonempty, compact and convex, then some x satisfies x∈F(x).

Proof. For ε>0 compactness gives finitely many points x₁,…,xN∈K such that every x∈K is within ε/2 of at least one xj. Choose yj∈F(xj). Define continuous tent weights

wj(x)=max{0,ε−‖x−xj‖},   aj(x)=wj(x)/Σkwk(x),
gε(x)=Σjaj(x)yj.

The denominator is positive by the covering property. Convexity of K makes gε a continuous K-valued function, so the Brouwer theorem just proved gives xε=gε(xε). No continuity of the selections yj as a function of their sites was asserted.

Let εn↓0 and take a convergent subsequence xεn→x. Every active site, meaning one with positive weight at xεn, lies within εn of that point. Thus all active sites approach x uniformly. Given δ>0, upper hemicontinuity says every active selected value yj eventually lies within distance δ of F(x). Choose a nearest bj∈F(x) for each such yj. Convexity puts b=Σajbj in F(x), and

dist(xεn,F(x))≤‖Σjaj(yj−bj)‖≤Σjaj‖yj−bj‖<δ.

Since δ is arbitrary and F(x) is closed, the limit x belongs to F(x). This proves the theorem. Finiteness of the nets constructs continuous approximations; it is the limiting argument that proves existence.

Worked case 4: four hypotheses that cannot be erased

On K=[0,1], set F(x)={1} for x<1/2, F(1/2)={0,1}, and F(x)={0} for x>1/2. Its graph is the union of the two closed segments [0,1/2]×{1} and [1/2,1]×{0}, so it is closed. All values are nonempty compact, but the middle value is not convex. Below 1/2 the image 1 differs from x; above it the image 0 differs from x; at the midpoint neither image equals x. There is no fixed point. Replacing the middle value by [0,1] repairs convexity and gives the unique fixed point 1/2.

If instead F(1/2)={0}, every value is a convex singleton, but closed graph fails: xn=1/2−1/n for n≥3 has yn=1, and the limit pair (1/2,1) is absent. This variant has no fixed point either. On the disconnected domain K={0,1}, the swapping map 0↦1, 1↦0 is continuous in the relative topology and has no fixed point; the domain is not convex. Finally, on the noncompact domain [0,1), f(x)=(1+x)/2 is a continuous self-map. Its only algebraic fixed-point candidate is 1, which is excluded. None of these claims depends on a failed numerical search.
