Volume 18 · Chapter 3

Organizations, incentives and culture

Capability. Design responsibilities, information flows and incentives that support an institution's stated purpose, and explain where a seemingly attractive performance measure or delegation rule can fail. Prerequisites: the strategy and resource constraints specified in Chapter1 and Chapter2, with principal-agent reasoning and global incentive comparisons. The two organizations, contracts and decision records below are original synthetic cases. No real workforce response, compensation policy or organizational improvement is claimed.

1. Structure assigns decisions; incentives influence how they are used

An institution's strategy can specify a valuable purpose while its daily rules reward incompatible behavior. A service team may be asked to solve problems well but paid only for recorded completions. A business unit may possess local information but lack authority to use it. Giving it authority can help, yet also allows decisions that serve its own reward rather than the institution's objective. Organizational design connects purpose, decision rights, information, rewards and review.

A principal delegates a decision and values its consequences. An agent takes the decision with objectives or information that may differ. A decision right specifies who may choose, commit resources, challenge, approve or stop an action. A performance measure is an observable indicator used to evaluate work; it may only partially represent the purpose. An incentive constraint requires the intended behavior to be preferred by the decision maker under the actual contract and information. A participation constraint requires that accepting the arrangement be at least as attractive as the outside option.

The bounded MIT contract-theory materials previously inspected for this library discuss hidden effort, incentive and participation constraints, and the allocation of attention across tasks. We reuse that context through Holmström's lecture slides; the finite contracts and proofs below are our own. The result of an incentive calculation depends on what the agent can choose, what can be verified, how payment is enforced and which outside options are available. A local first-order condition is not enough when the agent can stop, switch tasks or exploit a boundary.

Write the institutional objective before choosing the measure. A count of outputs may be useful evidence, but it is not identical to their quality, distribution or longer-term consequence. A measure becomes a target when rewards depend on it; then ask what behavior can raise the target without delivering the purpose. This is a design question, not a presumption that employees act dishonestly. Even diligent agents can reallocate scarce time toward the activity their work agreement emphasizes.

Separate information from authority. A person can observe a problem without being authorized to spend, and can be authorized to spend without having the relevant information. A usable role map specifies both. A reporting line should name the decision it changes, the evidence available before the decision and the person who checks consequences afterward. “Everyone owns quality” is incomplete if no one can stop a release or correct a misleading measure.

2. Culture is reflected in repeated practices and credible responses

Here culture means shared expectations about how people interpret priorities and respond to one another in recurring situations. It includes what receives attention, how disagreement is handled, what happens after an error and which actions senior people actually reward. A stated value is evidence of an aspiration. Consistent decisions and people's experience of those decisions are different evidence. Do not infer a healthy culture from a slogan, a survey average or one successful delivery.

Make desired norms actionable. For a research unit, a proposed norm of intellectual honesty could mean preserving inconvenient findings, separating an exploratory claim from a tested one, and giving a named reviewer authority to require a correction. For a financial operations team, a proposed norm of reliable service could mean escalating an unresolved reconciliation before a deadline instead of recording a premature completion. These are designs whose implementation and effects must be assessed. They are not universal employment rules or a substitute for the actual institution's legal and professional obligations.

Rewards can be monetary, but also include assignment choice, promotion evidence, attention and access to resources. Treat them as a system: a written quality rule can be undermined if every consequential decision rewards volume alone. At the same time, do not assume every beneficial action must be purchased with a bonus. Professional commitments, task interest, peer expectations and selection into a role may matter. A model can represent one motive to expose a mechanism without estimating its strength in an actual workforce.

For a proposed change, state a mechanism and an evidence plan. Record baseline work, output definitions, quality failures, resource use and who may change the measure. Inspect actual cases where the score and the purpose diverge. Give affected people a route to challenge an erroneous record and correct all affected evaluations consistently. A leader must distinguish a performance problem from a measurement error, inadequate resources or unclear responsibility; the same observed score can arise from different causes.

3. Two complete organizational decisions

Case A — a completion bonus crowds out an unmeasured task

Organizational brief. A fictional support unit divides effort between recorded completions e and unrecorded quality work q, both nonnegative with e+q≤1. The capacity unit is normalized available effort per period, not a measured employee hour. The institution values output V=e+3q. The agent receives payment be for completions, b≥0, and has utility U=be+q/2−(e²+q²)/2. The q/2 term represents a stipulated direct professional motive for quality; it is not a causal estimate of culture or a claim that actual workers have identical preferences. Effort is privately chosen; e is verifiable for payment, while q is not contractible in this exercise. The institution's net objective is P=e+3q−be. Payment is a transfer between these two parties, but is a cost in the principal's stated objective. Agent outside utility is0.

The full primitive tuple(quality value,quality motive,capacity) is (3,1/2,1). The principal may choose any nonnegative b. There is no wage floor or fixed salary requirement in this toy model, no stochastic output and no other employment benefit. Those omissions limit application; an actual compensation decision would need its own feasible contract and policy review. The zero-effort choice is feasible and gives0, so the agent's optimum satisfies participation automatically; participation need not bind.

Agent's complete global solution. The objective is strictly concave on a compact convex feasible triangle, so a maximum exists and is unique. Complete the squares: U=(b²+1/4)/2−[(e−b)²+(q−1/2)²]/2. Thus maximize utility by finding the feasible point closest to(b,1/2). If0≤b≤1/2, the unconstrained optimum(e,q)=(b,1/2) is feasible. If1/2<b<3/2, capacity binds; settingq=1−e gives derivative b+1/2−2e, so e=(b+1/2)/2 andq=(3/2−b)/2, both positive. To verify this branch globally, its gradient has equal components b−e=1/2−q=(b−1/2)/2≥0, so for any feasible change the gradient's product with that change is nonpositive: the candidate already uses total capacity1. Strict concavity makes it the unique maximum.

Ifb≥3/2, the candidate is(e,q)=(1,0). Its gradient is(b−1,1/2). For a feasible alternative(e',q'), e'−1≤−q', hence the gradient product is(b−1)(e'−1)+q'/2≤(3/2−b)q'≤0. Concavity again gives global optimality, and strict concavity gives uniqueness. Atb1/2 and3/2 adjacent formulas agree. The three regimes are therefore complete, including the corners. Increasing the completion bonus initially uses slack capacity, then reduces quality; the timing of that trade-off follows from the stipulated motive and capacity, not a universal empirical threshold.

Implementation and culture record. Proposed sequence: agree definitions and authority, collect baseline case evidence under actual permissions, test a bounded authorized change, review both counted and uncounted work, and record the decision to continue, amend or stop. A leader who praises only the completion total after a documented quality failure contradicts the stated purpose. A leader who investigates the failure, corrects the record and changes the reward sends a different signal. Neither practice has occurred merely because this paragraph recommends it. No causal test or realized workforce response is supplied.

Case B — delegation is valuable only if the local decision serves the purpose

Organizational brief and timing. A fictional service division chooses Small S or Large L before its operating outcome. The state is H or D with probability1/2 each. Headquarters knows the distribution but not the state; the local manager sees the state before choosing. Small generates8 units of institutional operating value in either state. Large generates20 in H and−12 in D. These are net operating values before the manager's payment and any verification cost. The manager incurs private effort cost1 for L and0 for S, is risk neutral, has outside utility0 and is paid nonnegative wages. All actions/payoffs are stipulated, not observed forecasts.

Inputs(state-H probability,Small value,Large-H,Large-D,Large effort,audit cost) are (1/2,8,20,-12,1,2). A baseline central order can require S and pay0, giving manager utility0; assume acceptance when indifferent. The manager's local information cannot simply be transmitted as a verified fact. An outcome audit, costingd=2 regardless of state, makes operating value contractible and the associated payment enforceable; it does not reveal the state before the action. Without that audit, the institution can pay an action bonus but cannot enforce the outcome-based formula below. The audit is a modeled technology, not a legal claim about any actual contract.

Centralization and naive delegation. A fixed L has expected operating value(20−12)/2=4, below S8 even before any payment required to induce its effort. The central baseline therefore orders S with principal payoff8. Under delegated authority with wage2 for L and0 for S, the manager receives utility1 from L and0 from S in either state. They always choose L: the private signal is present but not used in the institution's interest. Principal expected payoff is4−2=2. Merely transferring the decision right reduces value in this arrangement. The tuple(centralSmall,Large gross mean,naive delegated net) is (8,4,2).

Audited value-sharing contract. Consider w=α max{V,0}, whereV is realized operating value and0≤α≤1; the principal pays the fixed audit costd. The manager in H compares20α−1 for L with8α for S, choosing L if12α≥1. In D they compare−1 for L, whose wage is0, with8α for S, so S strictly wins for everyα≥0. Thus α≥1/12 implements the desired state-dependent choices if the manager chooses L when exactly indifferent in H. Without that tie convention, strict implementation requiresα>1/12. The decision rule and whether ties are enforceable or predictable belong in the model and in the decision record.

Atα1/12 with the stated tie rule, operating values are20 in H and8 in D. Expected gross value14; expected wage14/12=7/6; expected effort cost1/2. Manager expected utility7/6−1/2=2/3≥0, and utility in each state is also nonnegative. Principal net14−7/6−2=65/6. The tuple(α,gross mean,wage mean,manager utility,principal net) is (1/12,14,7/6,2/3,65/6). This exceeds central baseline8 by17/6. The manager's utility and the principal's objective count different costs; neither should be mislabeled total social surplus.

Global contract comparison and boundary. Forα<1/12, the manager chooses S in both states. The principal pays8α andd for no informational improvement, obtaining8(1−α)−d≤8−d. Forα≥1/12 under the tie convention, the principal gets14(1−α)−d, decreasing inα. Hence the best audited contract isα1/12, value77/6−d. Compare with the unaudited central baseline8: audited delegation is preferred iff d<29/6, tied at29/6 and inferior above it. For d2 the gain is17/6. Results (77/6,29/6,17/6). This is global within the stated proportional, nonnegative payment class and alternatives; arbitrary contracts or a different information technology are outside the comparison.

If ties cannot be relied upon, there is no smallest strictly implementingα. The supremum of principal payoff approaches77/6−d asα decreases toward1/12 from above, but that value is not attained in the strict-implementation subset. A concrete feasible choiceα1/10 gives principal14·9/10−2=53/5 and manager utility14/10−1/2=9/10: (1/10,53/5,9/10). It still beats8 atd2. A leader should not call an unattained supremum an implemented contract.

Complete authority and coordination map. Headquarters owns the purpose, permitted S/L menu, resource envelope and audit expenditure. The local manager owns the state-informed action inside that menu and records the decision and relevant information without claiming that a report is already verified. The independent verification function checks actual operating value under the agreed method; it does not receive authority to alter the target after observing the result. The payment owner applies the authorized formula to verified value, records disputes and retains corrections. Headquarters reviews deviations and may change the future menu, audit or compensation through the actual process. A stop/escalation condition is an unavailable audit, a cost above the approved amount, a state outside the two-state model or a payoff definition that no longer matches institutional value.

Full board decision memo. “Retain the central Small baseline unless local information can be used under an authorized and credible incentive/verification arrangement. The simple Large-action bonus gives expected principal value2, below baseline8, because it rewards Large in both states. In the supplied audited model, α1/12 with explicit favorable-state tie behavior gives65/6 after audit cost2; if strict incentives are required, α1/10 gives53/5. Both are conditional model comparisons. Verification cost at or above29/6 eliminates the modeled gain of the weak implementation benchmark, and unsupported ties can invalidate its attainment claim. Before commitment, the accountable owners must verify what can actually be observed, paid and challenged, confirm the full costs and consider omitted states. Do not treat possession of local information as sufficient evidence that delegation improves outcomes.”

Why the organization is more than a bonus. This contract relies on a clear decision menu, timely local information, capacity to implement the chosen action, credible measurement and a payment process. Removing any of those can defeat the comparison without changing the printedα. Coordination requires that the people responsible for action, verification and resource approval know which decisions depend on their work. A culture that suppresses adverse-state reports can also change the information reaching the decision process; no numerical contract analysis excuses investigating that actual behavior.

4. Evaluate a proposed organization as a set of linked hypotheses

Begin with a small number of consequential choices. For each, specify its owner, information before action, feasible alternatives, resource limit, incentive, verification and escalation. Then ask the agent's question: “What is my best feasible response under these rules?” Check corners, ties, outside options and ways to change the recorded measure. Ask the principal's separate question: “Does that response serve our objective after all costs?” Finally ask which assumptions are observable and what evidence would contradict them.

Distinguish a coordination failure from an incentive conflict. Two units may agree on the objective yet lack a shared schedule or consistent definition. A common decision log and a named integrator may resolve that problem. If the units gain from incompatible choices, information sharing alone may not change behavior; rights, rewards or the feasible menu may also need revision. Adding committees has costs in time and blurred responsibility, so specify the decision each meeting exists to make and who acts afterward.

Incomplete contracts also leave residual choices. The accepted bounded Hart account of incomplete contracts and control motivates the importance of rights over unresolved decisions and the trade-offs of integration. We do not infer that either centralization or decentralization always dominates. The cases deliberately show a favorable local-information opportunity and an unfavorable incentive arrangement inside it.

A useful review compares the intended mechanism with actual behavior and records alternatives. More recorded output may reflect better work, reassignment, changed classification or reduced attention elsewhere. An adverse outcome may reflect a poor decision, an unmodeled state or bad luck under a sensible decision. Preserve enough dated evidence to evaluate those possibilities. The leadership task is to improve the institution's decisions while keeping both accountability and uncertainty visible.

2. Global inequalities, not just stationary points

For1/2≤b≤3/2, e=(b+1/2)/2,q=(3/2−b)/2 sum1 and are nonnegative. Both gradient components equalλ=(b−1/2)/2≥0. At any feasible(e',q'), ∇U·((e',q')−(e,q))=λ(e'+q'−1)≤0. Concavity gives U(e',q')≤U(e,q)+that product≤U(e,q). Atb≥3/2 and(1,0), feasibility gives e'−1≤−q', so gradient product≤(3/2−b)q'≤0, proving the corner. Strict concavity means two distinct maximizing points are impossible: their midpoint would have strictly greater utility. The formulas coincide at both boundaries, so there is a single optimum there. Credit the complete feasible-domain proof and uniqueness argument; a derivative along one boundary alone does not check all alternatives.

3. Change the value function, not the behavioral model

The agent's choices remain the same. With quality weight1, on0≤b≤1/2 net is b+1/2−b²=3/4−(b−1/2)², maximized at1/2 with3/4. In the middle regime e+q=1, so net1−b(b+1/2)/2=1−b²/2−b/4 has derivative−b−1/4<0. Atb≥3/2, net1−b also decreases. Thus globallyb1/2 remains best, now giving net3/4. Results (1/2,3/4). This changes the principal's relative valuation of quality, not the agent's preferences or an observed institutional mission. A numerical invariance of optimalb in these two cases does not make the quality weight irrelevant in general. Credit all branches, global comparison and the objective/behavior distinction.

Sources, boundaries and continuation

The two linked primary references are reused in their previously recorded bounded inspection scopes: Holmström's contract lecture context and Hart's discussion of incomplete agreements and control. No fresh complete-document review, empirical culture effect or general optimal-organization theorem is claimed. All models, role maps, memoranda, exercises and proofs here are original. Actual incentives may involve risk, multiple periods, teams, legal constraints and unmeasured motives beyond these cases; a scoped model helps identify those questions rather than answering them by assumption.
