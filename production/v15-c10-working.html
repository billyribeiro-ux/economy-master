Capability and prerequisites

Assess health and education provision and demographic change using explicit choices, information and financing. Prerequisite: environmental, energy and climate economics, especially externalities, dated costs and distribution. Also use development and capabilities and experimental identification. All populations, services and parameters are synthetic. These models teach economic mechanisms; no numerical example supplies a clinical recommendation, current population forecast or personal insurance or retirement advice.

More spending is neither the outcome nor its explanation

A government observes rising care expenditure, uneven learning and a growing retiree share. It proposes cheaper insurance, more early schooling and a pension funding reform. Each intervention can change resources and incentives, but the same spending total can conceal very different outcomes. Insurance moves consumption across health states; treatment uses real inputs; education produces skills over time; pensions move resources across ages and generations. An account that combines these objects without identifying who receives and pays can produce an apparent benefit out of a transfer.

Distinguish health status, service use, financial protection and provider income. Similarly distinguish education inputs, enrollment, measured skills, later opportunities and earnings. Changes in utilization or attendance are intermediate outcomes. They can be useful evidence of a mechanism without establishing a health or learning benefit. A demographic projection describes births, survival and migration under stated assumptions; it does not by itself explain why families choose particular investments or who bears an aging population's fiscal adjustment.

For every policy ask three questions. What is the feasible choice before and after it? Which decision maker changes behavior, with what information? What evidence identifies the claimed effect and supports transferring it to the target population? The five cases answer these questions in deliberately small environments whose full solutions can be inspected.

1. Insurance participation changes the risk pool

A person with wealth W faces an unavoidable monetary loss L in the bad health state, where 0<L<W. Nonmonetary health consequences are unchanged by insurance. With utility √c and illness probability p, uninsured expected utility is (1−p)√W+p√(W−L). A full-insurance contract pays exactly L in the bad state and charges premium P beforehand, leaving consumption W−P in either state. The maximum acceptable premium is therefore V(p)=W−[(1−p)√W+p√(W−L)]². This is a willingness to pay for financial protection, not a valuation of eliminating illness.

Let Δ=√W−√(W−L)>0. Then V′(p)=2Δ(√W−pΔ)>0 for p∈[0,1], so higher-risk people will pay more. Insurer expected cost is pL. A pooled premium must cover the cost of the people who actually enroll. Calculating cost using the entire population and then assuming everybody buys can violate precisely this participation condition.

Einav and Finkelstein's 2018 review distinguishes selection into insurance from responses of care use to coverage. Its bounded methodological discussion motivates our separation of this fixed-loss model from the utilization model in the next section. We adopt no empirical effect estimate from the review.

Worked case 1 — a pooled average cost that cannot sustain its pool

A unit continuum contains equal masses of low and high risks. Individual risks are independent with exact aggregate fractions, so insurers face no aggregate claims uncertainty. Set (W,L,pL,pH,low-risk share)=(16,12,1/4,3/4,1/2). Each person knows their risk. Insurers cannot condition their offers on risk and may sell only the same standardized full-insurance contract at a uniform nonnegative premium; partial coverage and additional screening menus are excluded. Insurers have no administrative cost and compete for customers. A person buys when indifferent; an active equilibrium contract must break even and admit no profitable lower-premium entrant offering identical coverage.

Uninsured utilities are (low,high)=(7/2,5/2). Reservation premiums are (VL,VH)=(15/4,39/4), while actuarial costs are (low,high,population average)=(3,9,6). At premium six, low risks decline and high risks buy, so the insurer loses three per enrollee. The initially proposed average cost ceases to be the insured average cost.

For P≤15/4 both groups buy and average cost is six; no such premium breaks even. For 15/4<P≤39/4 only high risks buy, at cost nine; break-even requires P=9. At higher premiums nobody buys. Thus (active premium,insured mass)=(9,1/2) is the unique active zero-profit outcome within this contract class. An entrant charging below nine either attracts high risks at a loss or attracts both groups below their pooled cost. Charging more does not win customers. No-offer inactivity is not competitive: an offer strictly between nine and39/4 would profitably attract high risks.

Consider instead a public program restricted to the same common contract that pays a uniform subsidy s per enrollee directly to insurers. An outside treasury with an endowment of9/4 goods per member of the unit population funds the payment; it does not tax this insured population. Universal enrollment at gross premium six requires 6−s≤15/4, so the smallest subsidy is (s,net premium,total fiscal bill)=(9/4,15/4,9/4). Low risks are indifferent and enroll under the stated tie rule; high risks strictly prefer coverage. Each person's payment plus the treasury contribution equals six, covering the aggregate claims bill. The subsidy changes distribution and risk protection, not the underlying loss probabilities or real treatment requirements. Its payer's opportunity cost must enter a welfare assessment. A compulsory unsubsidized premium of six would make low risks worse off; universal enrollment alone is not a Pareto criterion.

Risk adjustment, contract menus, administrative costs, heterogeneous preferences and public outside options can alter this result. A comparison of insured and uninsured claims does not identify the response to coverage: the high-risk group already differed before buying. An experiment or credible instrument must specify whether it changes an offer, enrollment, generosity or provider access. These are different treatments, and the relevant exclusion and compliance conditions depend on that distinction.

2. Patient and provider incentives are separate choices

For a conditional-on-illness utilization model, let q∈[0,6] be units of a service, with money-equivalent benefit H(q)=aq−q²/2 and constant resource cost c per unit. A patient paying fraction λ∈[0,1] of the bill chooses q to maximize H(q)−λcq; the premium is already paid and unaffected by this individual's choice. Strict concavity gives q(λ)=min{6,max{a−λc,0}}. The efficient quantity for this specified benefit function is q(1), because total resources cost cq. A response to the out-of-pocket price is conventionally called ex post moral hazard; it is not an accusation of dishonesty or proof that all additional care lacks benefit.

This model deliberately assumes that benefit is known and correctly perceived, services are available at the stated resource cost, and there is no liquidity constraint or externality. If a patient misunderstands benefit, delays valuable care because of cash constraints, or affects others' health, the private derivative and welfare derivative change. The calculation below isolates a price wedge; it does not derive an optimal real-world insurance contract.

Worked case 2 — the same service under three payment institutions

Set (a,c,illness probability,coinsurance,wealth)=(6,2,1/2,1/4,20). A competitive insurer reimburses fraction1−λ of expenditure and charges the expected reimbursement as a premium before illness. With an exact continuum risk pool, the premium finances all claims; it is not revised after individual use. At full patient payment q=4, H=16 and net resource surplus is8. At λ=1/4, (q,patient bill,insurer reimbursement,premium)=(11/2,11/4,33/4,33/8). Wealth twenty covers both premium and the possible patient bill.

At the insured quantity, (H,total resource cost,net surplus,loss relative to efficient care)=(143/8,11,55/8,9/8). The exact loss is (q−4)²/2. Expected patient payments plus premiums are c times expected use; insurer reimbursement is not an extra social resource cost. The consumer is risk neutral in this utilization example, so this example supplies no offsetting risk-smoothing gain and does not claim that they would optimally choose this coinsurance contract. That missing tradeoff was intentionally separated from case1.

Now change who decides q. A public purchaser delegates the quantity to a provider, patients receive the resulting benefit, and patients do not buy additional care. The provider's objective is payment minus cq, with no altruistic term; the service cap remains six. Under a fee of three per service, profit is q, so the unique global choice is q=6. Treasury pays18, provider uses12 real goods and retains6, patients receive benefit18. Hence (q,treasury payment,provider profit,total surplus)=(6,18,6,6). A treasury endowment of twenty covers the contract. Consolidating patient benefit, taxpayer expense and provider receipts leaves H−cq, not H minus both payment and cost.

Under unconditional capitation K=8 per enrolled patient, the provider instead maximizes8−2q and chooses q=0. To implement q=4, suppose the service quantity is verifiable and an enforceable contract pays eight only if q≥4, otherwise zero. Then delivering four earns zero profit, more than any greater quantity; delivering zero also earns zero. Specify a provider tie rule accepting the contract and delivering its required minimum, or pay a strictly positive arbitrarily small participation rent to make delivery strictly preferred. Under the tie convention, (q,payment,profit,total surplus)=(4,8,0,8). The institution that verifies delivery does the essential work. If measured visits can replace actual valuable service, this contract no longer implements the assumed benefit function.

Claims about payment reform need outcome measurement beyond billed quantities. Random assignment of a payment rule can identify the induced service and spending changes in that setting. Measuring patient outcomes, access and selection into providers is additionally necessary for welfare. Report the budget paid by the purchaser, the provider's real inputs and financial margin separately. A lower price paid to a provider is partly a transfer; a fall in valued care can harm patients even when the purchaser saves money.

3. Education produces a stock through a sequence of investments

Years enrolled and money spent are inputs or exposures, not interchangeable measures of skills. Let an initial skill stock h₀>0 evolve as h₁=h₀+e and h₂=h₁(1+l), where e,l≥0 are early and late investment quantities. Both investments cost one resource unit per unit, and the gross interdate return is one. A sponsor has four units committed to this learner across the two dates; saving unused early funds for late investment is feasible. The objective here is final skill subject to that fixed resource budget, not a claim that skill units already measure lifetime welfare.

Late investment has marginal product h₁; early investment raises that marginal product. This is dynamic complementarity: ∂²h₂/∂e∂l=1. Cunha and Heckman's official abstract motivates stage-specific skill formation and dynamic complementarity. Our two-stage technology and budget are original. We do not infer a universal early-versus-late policy ranking or reproduce their empirical findings.

Worked case 3 — allocation over time and selection into investment

Let (initial skill,total investment budget)=(1,4). Because either investment raises final skill, the budget binds: l=4−e. Final skill is (1+e)(5−e)=9−(e−2)² on e∈[0,4]. Therefore (early,late,final skill,all-early skill,all-late skill)=(2,2,9,5,5). The complete-square identity proves the global optimum and both corner comparisons. Concentrating all funds at the early date is not optimal merely because early investment makes later investment more productive.

For general budget B≥0, h₂=(h₀+e)(1+B−e) has second derivative−2. The unique allocation is e*=min{B,max{(1+B−h₀)/2,0}}, l*=B−e*. A sufficiently large initial stock makes all-late investment optimal; sufficiently small B and h₀ can make all-early optimal. The conclusion depends on costs, the technology and the stock at the decision date. A lower late-stage price or a borrowing constraint could alter the feasible comparison.

To isolate selection, consider a different observational population with equal masses at h₀=1 and h₀=3. Everyone receives late investment l=2. High-stock learners receive e=2 and low-stock learners e=0. Observed final skills are3 and15, so the treated-minus-untreated difference is12. Yet the causal effect of changing e from0 to2, holding l=2 fixed, is6 for either type: (observed difference,average causal effect,baseline selection component)=(12,6,6). Random assignment of e among this same population would eliminate that baseline selection component in expectation. The observed association does not double the technology's return.

A randomized offer with incomplete take-up would identify an offer effect first. A stable assignment effect at one early/late allocation also need not identify the entire production surface. To forecast a different allocation, collect baseline skills, actual inputs at both dates, attrition and outcomes on a consistent scale, and test the predicted responses on allocation combinations held out from estimation. Changes in test preparation can raise a score without the intended skill; a measurement model and multiple relevant outcomes help distinguish the two.

4. Fertility and per-child investment share a time budget

Fertility decisions involve autonomy, health, uncertainty, relationships and institutions as well as resources. The following model is a conditional economic approximation for parents who choose positive planned family size. It does not value people by an educational score or represent childlessness, integer births or every household's preferences. Let n>0 be a continuous planned family-size index, s>0 education expenditure per child and c>0 parental consumption. Utility is log c+βlog n+γlog s, with β>γ>0. The terminology “quantity and quality” in older models refers to family size and specified child investments, not human worth.

The parent has time T>0, wage w>0 and nonlabor income Y≥0. Each child uses τ>0 units of parental time and f>0 goods before education expenses. Available paid work is T−τn≥0. The goods budget is c+n(f+s)=w(T−τn)+Y. Thus c+Fn+ns=I, where F=f+wτ and I=wT+Y. The opportunity cost of child time depends on the wage; it is not an additional cash bill to add again after this rearrangement. The time cap is n≤T/τ.

With total education expenditure E=ns, preferences become log c+(β−γ)log n+γlog E, a strictly concave function on positive (c,n,E). The budget c+Fn+E=I is linear. Without a binding time cap, the unique optimum allocates expenditure shares proportional to(1,β−γ,γ): c=I/(1+β), n=(β−γ)I/[(1+β)F], E=γI/(1+β), and s=γF/(β−γ). This transformation supplies a global proof despite the product ns in the original budget. At zero c,n or E utility tends to minus infinity; the optimum is interior unless the explicit upper time cap binds. If it does bind, set n=T/τ and split I−Fn between c and E in ratio1:γ. A binding-cap candidate has positive residual resources; otherwise it cannot be optimal in the positive-utility domain.

The MIT human-capital notes, family-investment section give method context for jointly choosing family size and education under resource prices. The wage, nonlabor-income and explicit time-cap formulation here is our own complete example.

Worked case 4 — a wage increase raises child investment while reducing planned births

Choose (beta,gamma,T,Y,f,tau,w0,w1)=(2,1,1,12,2,1/4,4,8). At the initial wage, I=16 and F=3, giving (parent consumption,n,s,paid work)=(16/3,16/9,3,5/9). At the higher wage, I=20 and F=4, giving (parent consumption,n,s,paid work)=(20/3,5/3,4,7/12). Both family-size choices lie below the time cap4. Direct substitution into the original cash budget verifies that earnings plus nonlabor income pay consumption and all child goods.

On this interior branch, dn/dw=[(β−γ)/(1+β)](Tf−τY)/(f+wτ)². Here Tf−τY=−1, so the derivative is negative even though income rises. The higher wage raises the time cost of children relative to education goods. In contrast, ds/dw=γτ/(β−γ)>0. If Y were zero, Tf−τY would be positive and fertility would rise in this particular model. Income and substitution effects cannot be read from the sign of a wage–fertility correlation alone.

A per-child subsidy would alter the full price F and require a payer, while a school grant might alter the education price and family choices jointly. A policy that predicts its budget using the original n and then changes n can underestimate fiscal exposure. A demographic assessment must also distinguish desired births, realized births, child survival and migration. Reproductive constraints and unequal decision power require separate models and evidence; the unitary-parent objective does not establish that observed outcomes were freely chosen.

5. Aging changes the denominator of a pension promise

Let N be the current working cohort, Nprev the preceding cohort and σ∈(0,1] its survival fraction into retirement. There are O=σNprev retirees. With wage w, payroll rate t∈[0,1] and pension b per retiree, a pay-as-you-go system without other funds must satisfy twN=bO. If g=N/Nprev, then b=twg/σ. A smaller entrant cohort or greater survival lowers benefits at a fixed rate and wage. Keeping b fixed instead requires t=bσ/(wg), provided that rate is feasible.

Acemoglu's overlapping-generations lecture distinguishes transfers to current retirees from investments returned to the contributing cohort. Our demographic accounting holds wages fixed and does not claim to solve the lecture's general-equilibrium capital accumulation model. Migration, labor-force participation, productivity and retirement timing can all change the relevant worker-to-retiree ratio.

Worked case 5 — a pension adjustment and an actually funded annuity

Start with (previous workers,current workers,survival,wage,tax)=(100,100,4/5,10,1/5). There are80 retirees, collections200 and pension5/2. If the working cohort is only80 while survival remains4/5, fixed-tax collections are160 and pension2; maintaining pension5/2 needs tax1/4. If survival also rises to1, there are100 retirees and fixed-tax pension8/5; maintaining5/2 needs tax5/16. Thus (initial pension,smaller-cohort pension,smaller-cohort required tax,longer-survival pension,longer-survival required tax)=(5/2,2,1/4,8/5,5/16). These are alternative same-period scenarios using the stated previous cohort. No benefit can be promised independently of its financing identity.

For a separate funded mechanism, a young person earns w and saves s in a fair life annuity. A safe storage technology produces R goods next period per good invested. An exact continuum survival fraction σ is known to all, no administrative costs or bequests exist, and the annuity pays nothing after death. Investing aggregate savings Ns produces RNs; dividing by σN survivors gives survivor consumption cold=Rs/σ. This is a resource-funded mortality credit, not a return generated by an accounting entry. The MIT annuity notes state the no-bequest and actuarial-fairness framework. We derive the two-date logarithmic case independently.

Maximize log(w−s)+βσlog(Rs/σ) for 0<s<w, with β,R>0. The second derivative is strictly negative and utility tends to minus infinity at either endpoint, so its first-order condition gives the unique global maximum s=βσw/(1+βσ). Let (w,beta,R,sigma)=(10,1/2,11/10,4/5). Then (saving,young consumption,survivor consumption)=(20/7,50/7,55/14). At σ=1, (saving,young consumption,old consumption)=(10/3,20/3,11/3). Greater survival raises saving but lowers consumption conditional on survival here because each investment pool must support more survivors. A welfare comparison across survival probabilities also requires the value assigned to living; this preference normalization is not a social valuation of lives.

Replacing the baseline pay-as-you-go contributions with new personal saving does not finance the existing80 retirees. If all200 goods previously collected are redirected to funded accounts while their promised pension remains5/2, the transition leaves an immediate bill of200 goods. New taxes, reduced benefits, an existing asset reserve or explicitly serviced borrowing must cover it. Comparing long-run returns without that transition account cannot establish a gain for every generation. Real annuity markets add selection, insurer risk, administrative costs and bequest motives; none is included in the fair benchmark.

A complete design separates financial protection and health outcomes from service use; skills and later opportunities from school attendance; and pension benefits and taxes from accounting balances. It defines eligible cohorts, dates, price units, survivor treatment and welfare weights including outside taxpayers. An illustrative insurance lottery randomizes subsidy offers before enrollment and follows everyone offered or not offered; it estimates an assignment effect while separately documenting selection and use. Education allocation can be randomized across early/late schedules of equal funded cost, with stable skill measures and follow-up attrition tracking. Demographic scenarios should expose the assumed survival, migration, participation and wage paths instead of reporting one forecast as certain.

    For the same policy plan, recalculate premiums and subsidy spending using induced enrollment and care, and pension taxes using the resulting worker/retiree counts. Include provider capacity, real inputs and administrative costs. A funded staged rollout can reserve resources for the next cohort and halt expansion if independent health or learning outcomes fail a prespecified criterion or if the revised fiscal bill exceeds the reserve; thresholds must be chosen before seeing outcomes. Grade six elements: distinct outcomes, credible identification, complete payer/resource accounts, behavioral fiscal feedback, uncertainty/transport and an operational revision rule. Alternative designs earn full credit when they defend those objects. Enrollment can rise without better health, a score can respond to preparation rather than the intended skill, and an account can balance only by transferring costs to an omitted cohort. None alone establishes aggregate welfare improvement.

Teach-back and delayed retention

Teach someone why a loan or premium receipt differs from a resource benefit, why early and late education inputs can be complements without “all early” being optimal, and why a funded account does not pay a pre-existing pension promise. Name the payer, behavior and missing evidence before stating a policy preference.

After one week, reconstruct the reservation premium, the patient utilization condition, the projected education optimum, the transformed fertility budget and the pension identity without notes. After one month change one primitive: raise the low-risk share, change coinsurance, raise initial skill, remove nonlabor income, or increase survival. Explain exactly which branch changes and check every original budget. The key formulas are V=W−[(1−p)√W+p√(W−L)]²; q=clip(a−λc,0,6); e*=clip((1+B−h₀)/2,0,B); c+Fn+E=I with n≤T/τ; and twN=bσNprev. Each requires its stated domain and choice maker.

Deeper work adds heterogeneous health needs, imperfect information, school and provider capacity, household bargaining and general-equilibrium demographic feedback. The sources supply bounded method context. These complete small models are starting points for testing mechanisms, not numerical forecasts for an actual population.
