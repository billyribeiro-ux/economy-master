Teaching cases and leading discussion

Capability. Lead a discussion that makes a decision, its evidence and its assumptions inspectable. Ask a sequence of questions, respond to actual reasoning, involve learners through accessible routes, and close with a synthesis that distinguishes settled calculations from unresolved judgments.

Prerequisites. Use criterion-based feedback and reassessment, expected values and conditional probability from Volume 3, and the distinction between welfare weights and empirical effects from Volume 15. All cases and dialogue below are original synthetic teaching materials. No classroom, participant performance or institutional decision is represented as observed.

1. A case is a decision with a reasoning burden

A teaching case supplies a situation in which a learner must use disciplinary ideas to answer a consequential question. It needs a decision maker, feasible alternatives, relevant information and a criterion for judging a recommendation. An entertaining story with no analytical task is insufficient. An incomplete case can be useful when the learning objective is to identify missing evidence, but the instructor must know which conclusions are determinate and which remain conditional.

A discussion plan is an ordered set of reasoning tasks plus possible responses to the contributions that arise. It is not a script requiring students to say the instructor's preferred words. Begin by solving the case, constructing a plausible contrary recommendation and identifying the exact disagreement. Does it concern arithmetic, probability, a feasibility constraint, distributional values or an unsupported causal claim? A teacher cannot facilitate that distinction reliably without doing the underlying analysis.

The CMU Eberly Center discussion guidance recommends planning around objectives, varying questions, managing participation and leaving time for synthesis. We use these as bounded professional suggestions, not evidence of a causal learning gain from our cases. The HBS Christensen Center guidance distinguishes questioning, listening and responding and encourages planned openings with responsive follow-up. Its examples and institutional practices are not copied or imposed here. The concrete protocols below are our own designs to be evaluated through actual learner work.

Use three visible records. The case record contains supplied facts and their provenance. The argument record contains deductions and explicitly added assumptions. The decision record contains the criterion, chosen action, strongest objection and trigger for reconsideration. A blank cell is often useful: it identifies information the case has not supplied. Never fill it with an invented statistic merely to keep the discussion moving.

Prepare a question sequence that builds something. Ask first for the alternatives and units, then for a comparison, then for the assumption carrying the comparison, then for a changed condition. After a contribution, ask a single specific follow-up and allow preparation time. A pause can mean calculation or formulation; it does not establish disengagement. If no one can identify the quantities, return to the case record rather than escalating to a more abstract debate.

2. Participation is evidence of reasoning, not a race to speak

Before the session, distribute the complete accessible case and ask every learner for a short initial recommendation with a calculation and a question. In the room, give a brief individual writing period before comparison in pairs. Invite contributions that perform different functions: identify an input, reconstruct a calculation, challenge an assumption, represent an affected group or synthesize a disagreement. Rotate these analytical functions. Do not require a learner to speak for an identity group or disclose personal financial circumstances.

Set a clear interaction rule: state the claim being challenged and the reason, without attacking the speaker. A substantive objection is welcome even when it reverses the teacher's provisional answer. When two students talk past each other, ask one to restate the other's criterion and let the other confirm or correct it. The goal is accurate disagreement, not forced consensus. Correct a false computation explicitly; acknowledge a legitimate difference in objectives explicitly too.

If a few voices dominate, interrupt the pattern without humiliating the participants: “We have that expected-value argument on the board. Let us hear a check of its probability assumption from someone who has not yet contributed.” Offer a written route and use it in the synthesis. A verbal discussion objective may still require observed oral practice, but an economics-reasoning objective need not award a higher grade merely for speed, accent, confidence or number of turns. Tell learners which capability is being assessed and retain assistance or access limits with the evidence.

A usable participation record might contain: contribution locator; claim; supporting reasoning; response to another argument; revision after challenge; observation mode; prompts supplied. Do not infer that silence means no understanding, or that frequent speech proves it. A final independent case variant helps distinguish the shared discussion's product from each learner's own reasoning. Any recording or personal-data collection needs the actual applicable consent and institutional process; a written case exercise does not itself authorize it.

3. Two complete discussion packages

Case 1 — A capacity decision with uncertain demand

Learner packet. A fictional cooperative must choose one indivisible action today: no expansion O, a small expansion S or a large expansion L. Demand tomorrow is low or high. The table gives all net payoffs in thousands of currency units at the same valuation date; costs have already been deducted. There are no further financing constraints, outside effects or later changes of action. The cooperative may use a probability p for high demand, but the table itself supplies no probability estimate.

The primitive payoff rows are ((0,0),(4,10),(-8,22)). For the first round, an analyst proposes 1/2 as p, without claiming it is an empirical estimate. A second decision rule uses only p∈(3/10,7/10) and selects the action with the greatest worst expected payoff over that interval. Prepare a recommendation under each rule, say whether the outside option can win, and explain what additional information could change your choice.

Complete analysis. Expected payoffs are E[O]=0, E[S]=4+6p and E[L]=−8+30p. Their difference E[L]−E[S]=−12+24p. Thus S is uniquely best for p<1/2, S and L tie at p=1/2, and L is uniquely best for p>1/2. This covers p∈[0,1]: S always exceeds O because 4+6p≥4, so O never ties for the maximum. The first-round vector is (0,7,7). A learner who selects S to avoid a negative low-state outcome may have a different objective from expected payoff; ask them to name that objective rather than mark an unsupported claim that S has a larger mean correct.

Both nonconstant expected payoffs rise with p, so their minima over the supplied closed interval occur at its lower endpoint. The worst-expected-payoff vector is (0,29/5,1), making S uniquely best under the declared ambiguity rule. This is not the same operation as taking the worst realized payoff over the two states: those minima are (0,4,-8). The rules agree on S here, but their numerical objects differ. Neither rule estimates a true probability from the payoff table.

Instructor's 50-minute question sequence. Use 5 minutes for individual case annotation, 8 for payoff/units checking, 12 for the p-dependent comparison, 10 for competing criteria, 10 for information and 5 for the exit synthesis. These proposed durations sum to 50; they are not tested timing norms. Ask: “What has already been subtracted?” Expected answer: all costs in the stated net payoffs. “At p=1/2, what can the mean criterion decide?” It excludes O but cannot break the S/L tie. “Which equality changes the preferred action?” The threshold p=1/2. “Does a range mean equal probabilities within the range?” No probability distribution over p has been supplied. “What does the worst-expected rule optimize?” The minimum conditional mean across admissible p values, not the single worst state.

Branching dialogue. Learner: “Large wins because 22 is the biggest number.” Instructor: “Show the low-state consequence and specify the rule that ignores it.” A defensible response would name maximization of the best possible payoff; it is not the supplied mean rule. Learner: “Then no answer is possible because probability is unknown.” Instructor: “Which conditional ranking can you prove for every p on either side of one half?” The complete response is the threshold partition above. Learner: “Small is safer, therefore its expected payoff is higher.” Instructor: “Separate the low-state payoff from the mean at the analyst's p.” The repaired statement is that S and L have equal means at that p but different state exposures. Ask a second learner to restate the repaired claim before moving on.

Information extension with a full answer. Keep prior p=1/2 and risk-neutral mean maximization. A signal is H with probability 3/4 in high demand and 1/4 in low demand, and otherwise is L. The signal's conditional probabilities are (3/4,1/4), ordered P(H|high), P(H|low); its fee is 2 thousand, paid regardless of its report. Bayes' rule gives P(H)=1/2, P(high|H)=3/4 and P(high|L)=1/4. Choose L after H (conditional mean 29/2 exceeds S's 17/2), and S after L (11/2 exceeds L's −1/2). The ex ante payoff before the fee is (29/2+11/2)/2=10, after the fee 8, against 7 without information. The ordered result is (1/2,3/4,1/4,10,8,1): signal probability, two posteriors, gross mean, net mean, gain. The greatest fee leaving acquisition weakly preferred is 3. This is a decision value under stipulated probabilities, not a prediction that a real survey attains those accuracies.

Perfect information would permit S in low demand and L in high demand, producing mean 13 and value 6 above the original 7. The imperfect signal's gross value 3 is lower. Enumerating the two contingent actions proves the stated signal policy is optimal: after each report choose the highest conditional mean, and averaging those maxima is at least as good as any other report-dependent selection. Paying a fee does not change which action is best after a report because the same fee has already been incurred.

Case 2 — Equal aggregate value, unequal distribution and an evidence update

Learner packet, first release. A fictional council serves two groups of 20 residents each, called P and R solely for this example. It must choose program A, program B or no program O. All quantities are one-period monetary-equivalent benefits in common currency units; there is no discounting. Each program costs 80, financed by a tax of 2 on every resident, with no collection cost or behavioral response. Per-person benefits (P,R) are (6,2) under A and (3,5) under B. O has no benefit, cost or tax. These are stipulated conditional effects, not estimates from an actual evaluation; the labels do not supply information about actual people's preferences.

The input is (20,20,6,2,3,5,80), ordered group sizes, A benefits, B benefits and cost. Each group contains identical residents only inside this model. The council can use aggregate net benefit, a welfare weight w≥0 on each P resident relative to weight 1 on each R resident, or a separate minimum-benefit constraint. These are alternative declared decision rules, not hidden empirical parameters.

Complete first-release answer. Tax is 80/40=2 per person. Net per-person benefits are ((4,0),(1,3)) for A and B. Both aggregate to (80,80), exceeding O's zero. Weighted sums are W(A)=80w and W(B)=20w+60. Their difference is 60(w−1), so B wins for 0≤w<1, A/B tie for w=1, and A wins for w>1; O is never optimal because B always has at least60. At w=2, the two weighted values are (160,100). A council that instead requires at least2 net units per P resident permits A and excludes B and O. That constraint must be disclosed; it is not a consequence of equal aggregate benefits.

Discussion task. Write one recommendation under aggregate net benefit, one under w=2, and one under the stated floor. Identify what changes between the arguments. A complete answer says that the aggregate rule ties A and B and needs an additional rule to choose; the weighted rule chooses A; the floor permits only A. The disagreement is about priorities/feasibility under shared effects, not about whether 20×4 equals80. No majority vote can change that multiplication. The floor is an authored scenario condition, not a claim about an actual legal entitlement.

Second release, supplied after the initial recommendation. Keep all other inputs fixed, but the supported per-person P benefit of A is now only known to be b∈(2,6). No probability distribution over b is available. Benefits for R under A remain2, and B retains its stipulated (3,5). Ask learners to revise their recommendation and explicitly state which of the three decision rules they are using. This release broadens uncertainty about the effect rather than changing anyone's values.

Complete update. Net P benefit under A is b−2∈[0,4]. At w=2, W(A)=40(b−2)∈(0,160), while W(B)=100. Therefore A beats B iff b>9/2, ties at b=9/2 and loses below it. An unconstrained rule maximizing the worst weighted sum over the supported interval chooses B, since its guaranteed100 exceeds A's guaranteed0. That result does not say B wins for every b: at b=6, A yields160. A requirement that the P net benefit be at least2 for every supported b cannot be met by A (its minimum is0), B (its P net is1) or O (zero). The robust floor leaves 0 feasible actions. Do not quietly choose B while claiming the floor has been honored. The institution must seek a genuinely new feasible option, better evidence or an authorized change in the constraint, and must disclose any delay or default consequence.

Complete 45-minute facilitation plan. Allocate 5 minutes to individual recommendations, 8 to the tax and net-benefit table, 8 to comparing criteria, 10 to the evidence update, 8 to a critique of revised recommendations and 6 to synthesis. Ask one question at a time: “Who bears the stated cost?” Each resident pays2. “Where did the distributional preference enter?” In w or the explicit floor, not the benefit table. “Does wider uncertainty imply zero effect?” No: it supplies a range, including positive effects. “Can a recommendation be best under one rule and infeasible under another?” Yes; B is robustly best without the floor and fails the floor. Invite a written challenge before asking a respondent to defend a particular rule.

Dialogue and repair. Learner: “A is objectively better because P deserves twice the weight.” Instructor: “Write the weight next to the criterion. Which conclusion would a person sharing the table but using w=1 reach?” Answer: a tie. Learner: “Then every answer is just an opinion.” Instructor: “Which answers are ruled out by the arithmetic or the declared floor?” Wrong totals, an undisclosed probability average and a claim that B satisfies the floor are ruled out. Learner: “The uncertainty means the program failed.” Instructor: “State the worst and best supported b and the decision threshold.” The interval straddles9/2; no observed failure has been supplied. Another learner should summarize this distinction before the class votes on any proposed policy.

4. Close the discussion without pretending all disagreement vanished

A synthesis should identify the decisive comparison, assumptions that could reverse it, and unresolved questions with an evidence route. Ask learners to correct the board, then submit an individual recommendation naming the strongest objection. Preserve the first recommendation and the revision: a justified change of mind can be evidence of reasoning, while an unchanged answer can also be justified. Do not score conformity to the majority or the instructor's preferred welfare weight.

Use a simple claim audit after discussion. Label each statement supplied, derived, conditional or unsupported. “Large has mean14.5 at p=.75” is derived conditional on the table and probability. “A real market has p=.75” is unsupported here. “A fails the robust floor over b∈[2,6]” is derived. “The actual council is legally permitted to remove that floor” is not established. This audit makes room for disagreement without tolerating fabricated evidence.

Review facilitation separately from the case's answer. Did the sequence expose the intended assumption? Did the teacher correct a misleading inference? Could learners enter through the available modes? Did the closing task reveal independent reasoning? Record actual evidence before drawing conclusions. A busy room, unanimous vote or smooth performance is not sufficient proof of transfer or a causal teaching effect. If a discussion failed, identify a particular instruction, missing prerequisite or access barrier and test a revised design rather than blaming an unobserved learner trait.

Exercises

  1. For Case1, set p=2/5. Compute all means and the optimal action. Write an opening question, a diagnostic follow-up to “22 is largest,” and a complete two-sentence synthesis.
  2. Keep the original signal and prior but set its fee to 4. Give the contingent policy, net mean and acquisition decision. Explain why refusing the signal before buying it differs from ignoring it after the fee is sunk.
  3. Define regret as the best expected payoff at p minus the chosen action's expected payoff. On the original p interval, compute each action's maximum regret and the minimax-regret choices. Contrast this answer with the largest-worst-mean answer, and supply a teacher follow-up that exposes the difference.
  4. At the first release of Case2, use w=1/2 with no floor. Compute weighted values, choose an action and write a respectful response to a learner who calls the opposing weight “bad mathematics.” Include a check with a determinate answer.
  5. After the second release, add a new program C with per-person benefits (4,4) and the same cost80/tax2. At w2 calculate its weighted value and test the floor2. Compare A,B,C under worst-weighted-value maximization with the floor, and state which new facts were assumed.
  6. A session has (30,8,10,3): total minutes, reserved synthesis minutes, learners and promised uninterrupted minutes per learner. Show whether that promise fits. Design a complete feasible alternative with an individual writing phase, pair work, a limited plenary and synthesis; retain an individual reasoning check without claiming everyone spoke publicly.
  7. A fictional discussion poll records six votes for A and four for B in Case2. An instructor reports, “The vote estimates the welfare weight and proves that A works.” Repair the claim, write three actual follow-up questions with answer guides, and supply a concrete participation criterion that does not reward mere agreement.
  8. Write a full closing memo for the second release of Case2 under w2 and a mandatory robust floor2. Include the recommendation/status, calculation, uncertainty, missing option and next evidence request. Respond to a participant who wants the facilitator to announce B just to end the discussion.
Complete solutions and evaluation criteria

1. A conditional recommendation

At p2/5, the means are (0,32/5,4), so S is uniquely best. Opening: “Which net outcomes and probability enter today's comparison?” Follow-up: “If low demand occurs, what does L return, and how does the stated probability weight that result?” Full synthesis: “Under p2/5 and mean maximization, S yields6.4 against L's4 and O's0. L's larger high-state payoff alone is insufficient, and a change of p above1/2 reverses the preferred expansion.” Credit the arithmetic, an actual diagnostic question and the stated reversal condition; a leading demand to pick S is not facilitation.

2. Information can be useful but too expensive

The signal still prescribes L after H and S after L. Gross mean remains10, but net mean becomes 6, below the no-signal7; do not buy at fee4. After purchase, the fee is common to all contingent actions and already incurred, so using the report remains better than deliberately ignoring it: ignoring yields7−4=3, using yields6. This does not justify the original purchase. Credit both decision dates and the distinction between acquisition and subsequent response.

3. Different ambiguity criteria can choose differently

The best mean is S's below1/2 and L's above it. S's regret is0 below the threshold and24p−12 above it; L's is12−24p below and0 above. Their maximum regrets are both24/5, at opposite interval endpoints. O's regret is the best mean, increasing to L's13 at p7/10. Thus maximum regrets (O,S,L) are (13,24/5,24/5), and both S and L minimize maximum regret. Piecewise affine functions attain these endpoint or threshold extrema, proving completeness. The worst-mean rule uniquely chose S. Follow-up: “Are you protecting a payoff floor or limiting distance from the best action that would be chosen if p were known?” The answer identifies different objectives; it is not an arithmetic contradiction. Credit the definition, all three maxima, the tie and a specific diagnostic question.

4. A weight is not a multiplication error

At w1/2, A=40 and B=70, recorded as (40,70), so B wins without the floor. Response: “Your concern may be about whose benefit receives priority. The calculation using a declared weight can be correct even if you reject that weight; identify the criterion you would defend and recalculate under it.” Check: “At w1, what is the difference W(A)−W(B)?” Answer0, so both equal80. Credit the correct comparison, respect for the disagreement and a determinate check. Calling every normative objection mathematical error, or every numerical error opinion, loses the distinction.

5. A genuinely new feasible option

C has net benefits(2,2) per person and W(C)=20×2×2+20×2=120. It meets the P floor exactly. On A's uncertain interval, A fails the robust floor; B also fails it; C is the only feasible listed program and therefore the chosen feasible optimum. Without the floor, C still exceeds B100 and A's worst0, though at b6 A's value160 is larger than C's120. This comparison assumes C is truly available, its benefits and common cost are supported as stipulated, and the same tax/effects apply. Inventing C's numbers in an actual council meeting would not create that option. Credit the equality boundary, all alternatives and the new-evidence distinction.

6. Match the participation promise to the time

The promised individual turns need30 minutes, but only22 remain after synthesis; the shortfall is 8. A feasible plan is individual written argument4 minutes, paired comparison6, plenary12, synthesis8: (4,6,12,8), totaling30. Use the last two synthesis minutes for every learner to submit an independent recommendation and assumption; the first six synthesize shared comparisons. Only a limited number of public contributions fit the plenary. Collect each written argument and pair notes, invite contrasting analyses and record which capability each mode demonstrates. Do not claim ten observed oral performances. Credit a feasible total, real analytical prompts and honest evidence scope rather than a participation slogan.

7. A poll is a record of choices under its conditions

Repair: “Six participants selected A and four B in this classroom poll; their reasons, criteria and correctness have not yet been established. The poll neither identifies a welfare parameter nor estimates program effects.” Ask: “Under what w do A and B tie at the first release?” Answer1. “What must hold for A to exceed B at w2 after the update?” Answer b>9/2. “Does B satisfy a P net-benefit floor2?” No, its net P benefit is1. A usable criterion is “states a relevant assumption, supports a comparison and accurately responds to a contrary argument,” evidenced by actual written or spoken work. A learner may satisfy it while favoring either policy under a disclosed appropriate rule. Credit all three question/answer pairs and the distinction between votes, preferences, competence and effects.

8. An honest unresolved decision

Memo: “No listed action satisfies the required floor of2 net units for each P resident in every supported state. A gives b−2∈[0,4], B gives1 and O gives0. Although B maximizes the worst weighted sum among unconstrained listed actions at w2, choosing it would violate the stated constraint. The interval is an evidence limit, not a probability forecast or an observed zero effect. Request an actually feasible program or financing/benefit redesign, or evidence that raises A's supported lower benefit to at least4; disclose implementation deadlines and the consequences of delay. Any change to the floor requires the responsible decision maker's authority rather than a facilitator's convenience.” At b≥4, A's P net is at least2; this establishes feasibility, not that A exceeds B on unconstrained weighted value (that requires b≥9/2 for weak preference). Response to the participant: “We can end with a precise infeasibility finding and next action. Announcing B would conceal the distinction the case asks us to understand.” Credit the full feasibility proof, separate optimality threshold and actionable request. A rubric without the memo does not answer the task.

Teach-back and delayed retention

Teach-back. Lead a five-minute exchange about Case1 in which someone recommends L because of its high payoff. Ask for the rule, reconstruct the mean threshold, then compare worst-state and worst-expected criteria. Answer guide: show −12+24p, threshold1/2, low-state minima(0,4,−8) and interval minima(0,29/5,1). A sound instructor identifies the actual disagreement, corrects the inference without attacking the speaker, and ends with a conditional recommendation. An evaluator must observe the real exchange; this script is only preparation.

Delayed retention check. With Case2's cost and group sizes unchanged, suppose A's P benefit is now exactly4, R benefit2, w2 and floor2. Give the weighted values and feasible choice; explain why a higher weighted alternative can be excluded. Full answer: A has P net2, R net0, weighted80. B remains100 but P net1 fails the floor; O also fails. A is the only feasible listed action. The difference from the b9/2 threshold reflects a constraint, not inconsistent arithmetic. Request an unaided answer before showing this guide, and change the boundary if it has been memorized.
