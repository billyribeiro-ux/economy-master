Preference representation and choice theory

A dataset can be perfectly consistent with utility maximization and still leave the next decision undetermined. A finite menu can identify which alternatives tie without identifying a cardinal scale. A finite consumption grid can even hide a preference that admits no real-valued utility on the full commodity space. The task is to state what was observed, prove what its assumptions imply, and preserve the distinction between existence, identification and prediction.

Capability. Prove finite and a bounded continuous representation theorem; diagnose lexicographic nonrepresentation; recover preferences from sufficiently rich complete choice reports; complete restricted finite reports; and construct a continuous, concave, strictly monotone rationalizer from finite budget observations satisfying GARP. Then establish exactly how two such rationalizers can disagree beyond the data. All six cases and twelve exercises below are original, synthetic mathematical examples.

Prerequisites. Use the proof and continuity tools of Volume 2, Chapter 1 and Chapter 2, the convexity inequalities of Chapter 9, and the compact-subsequence theorem of Chapter 10. Volume 5, Chapter 1 introduces preferences and demand. Volume 3, Chapter 10 distinguishes utility over certain outcomes from expected utility over lotteries. All new representation, menu and constructive revealed-preference arguments are supplied here.

1. What a utility representation says

On a nonempty set X, write x≽y for “x is weakly preferred to y.” Completeness means x≽y or y≽x for every pair, including identical alternatives. Transitivity means x≽y and y≽z imply x≽z. Together these define a weak order. Define indifference x∼y by both comparisons, and strict preference x≻y by x≽y and not y≽x. A real-valued function u represents ≽ when x≽y if and only if u(x)≥u(y). Thus strict preference corresponds to > and indifference to equality. This is a statement about every pair in the declared domain.

Finite representation theorem. Every weak order on a finite nonempty X has a real-valued representation. To prove it, first note that ∼ is reflexive by completeness, symmetric by definition and transitive by two applications of preference transitivity. It therefore partitions X into disjoint indifference classes. Comparisons between classes do not depend on representatives: replacing x by x′∼x and y by y′∼y preserves x≽y by transitivity. Two different classes cannot be indifferent; completeness orders them strictly, and transitivity orders any three consistently. A highest class exists: start with one class and compare each remaining class with the current highest, replacing it when the new one is higher. The final class dominates every earlier class by transitivity. Remove it and repeat. Assign successive integer ranks from the lowest to the highest class and give every member its class rank. This function represents the original weak order. Conversely, ≥ on real numbers is complete and transitive, so any represented relation is a weak order.

Ordinal uniqueness on the range. If u and v represent the same preference, define f(u(x))=v(x). This is well defined because equal u values mean indifference and therefore equal v values. If u(x)>u(y), strict preference gives v(x)>v(y); f is strictly increasing on u(X), and v=f∘u. Conversely, every strictly increasing f on u(X) preserves every comparison. This statement needs neither differentiability nor an extension of f to all real numbers. A merely nondecreasing map can erase a strict comparison.

Worked case 1: all choices on six alternatives

Let X={a,b,c,d,e,f}, with rank vector in that order (2,1,0,2,1,0). For each of the 63 nonempty menus B, report the full maximizing set C(B)={x∈B:u(x)=maxy∈Bu(y)}. The classes from low to high are {c,f}, {b,e}, {a,d}. Thus C({a,b,c,d})={a,d}, C({b,c,e,f})={b,e}, and C({c,f})={c,f}. Reporting only a from the first menu would change the information: it would no longer be the complete choice set for this preference.

The transformation v=u² is strictly increasing on the attained range {0,1,2}, producing vector (4,1,0,4,1,0) and all the same certain choices. It need not be increasing on all of ℝ. Now consider a half-a, half-c lottery and sure b. Expected u values are (1,1); expected v values are (2,1). A nonlinear ordinal transformation of certain-outcome scores does not in general preserve an expected-utility extension. Positive affine changes v=αu+β, α>0, do preserve expected comparisons because E[v]=αE[u]+β. The finite ordinal theorem does not license applying arbitrary nonlinear transformations inside expectations.

The preference and ordinal distinctions follow the scoped choice and utility material in Wolitzky’s MIT notes, slides 14–18. The proofs and numerical examples here are original presentations; the later sections state more restrictive hypotheses whenever they supply a more specific constructive theorem.

2. A continuous representation constructed along a diagonal

Take the consumption domain X=ℝ+L, with finite L≥1. Coordinates are nonnegative quantities; utility labels have no physical unit. Say the weak preference graph is closed if xn≽yn, xn→x and yn→y imply x≽y, with all limits in X. Say preference is strictly coordinate-monotone if x≥y coordinate by coordinate and x≠y imply x≻y. This condition is stronger than local nonsatiation and rules out indifference when only one good increases.

Sufficient orthant theorem. A complete, transitive, closed-graph, strictly coordinate-monotone preference on X has a continuous representation. Write 1 for the all-ones vector. For x≠0 let M=maxixi>0. Monotonicity gives M1≽x≽0. Define closed subsets A={t∈[0,M]:t1≽x} and B={t∈[0,M]:x≽t1}. Completeness gives A∪B=[0,M]; M∈A and 0∈B. Here is the required intersection argument without presuming a utility exists. Let t*=inf A. Closedness and the definition of an infimum give t*∈A. If t*=0, it is in B already. If t*>0, every t<t* is outside A, hence in B; a sequence increasing to t* and closedness imply t*∈B. Thus t*1∼x. At x=0 choose t*=0. Two distinct diagonal values cannot both be indifferent to x, because the larger diagonal bundle strictly dominates the smaller. The value t* is unique.

Define u(x)=t*. If x≽y, substituting indifferent diagonal bundles and using transitivity gives u(x)1≽u(y)1, hence u(x)≥u(y); otherwise strict monotonicity would give the reverse strict comparison. If u(x)≥u(y), monotonicity on the diagonal, with reflexivity when equal, and transitivity give x≽y. This proves representation.

For continuity, let xn→x in X and tn=u(xn). The bounds 0≤tn≤maxixni make the sequence bounded. Along any convergent subsequence tnk→t, both xnk≽tnk1 and its reverse pass to limits by closed graph. Therefore x∼t1 and uniqueness forces t=u(x). If the whole sequence did not converge to u(x), a subsequence would stay at least some ε>0 away; boundedness supplies a convergent further subsequence, contradicting its unique limit. Hence u is continuous relative to X, including its boundary. No convexity or differentiability has been used.

Worked case 2: a continuous preference that dislikes a mixture

On ℝ+², let V(x)=x₁+x₂+|x₁−x₂|/2, and compare bundles by V. Continuity of V gives a closed weak graph. If d≥0 and d≠0, the absolute-value inequality gives

V(x+d)−V(x) ≥ d₁+d₂−|d₁−d₂|/2 ≥ (d₁+d₂)/2 > 0.

Thus the diagonal theorem applies. Since V(t,t)=2t, its diagonal normalization is D(x)=V(x)/2. In the order (4,0), (0,4), (2,2), (3,1), the resulting values are (3,3,2,5/2). The first two bundles are indifferent and their midpoint is strictly worse. Their upper contour at level 3 is therefore nonconvex. A quasiconcave representation would have convex upper contours, and those contours depend on preference rather than its labels, so no quasiconcave representation of this preference exists. In particular, no concave representation exists, since concavity implies quasiconcavity. Continuous representation does not require convex preferences.

For any representation, convex upper contour sets are equivalent to quasiconcavity: if u(x),u(y)≥k, convexity keeps their mixture in that contour; setting k=min(u(x),u(y)) gives the quasiconcavity inequality, and the argument reverses. Concavity is a stronger property of a particular numerical representation. An increasing transformation preserves contours but need not preserve concavity. Our diagonal theorem is a sufficient result on the entire orthant under strict coordinate monotonicity; it does not prove a general representation theorem on every Euclidean subset. This scope matters when using the statement on MIT slide 17 or its discussion of shape on slides 19–28.

3. Why a finite grid can conceal nonrepresentation

On [0,1]², lexicographic preference ranks x above y when x₁>y₁, or when x₁=y₁ and x₂>y₂; identical bundles tie. It is complete and transitive: a first-coordinate advantage survives comparison, and when first coordinates tie, the second-coordinate real order is transitive. Every positive gain in the first coordinate dominates every possible second-coordinate loss. This is a precise preference, but its full domain matters.

No real-valued representation exists. Suppose u represented it. For each t∈[0,1], the real interval It=(u(t,0),u(t,1)) is nonempty. If t<s, lexicographic preference gives u(t,1)<u(s,0), so It and Is are disjoint. Every nonempty real interval contains a rational: for a<b choose an integer n with n(b−a)>1 and then an integer k strictly above na but below nb, so a<k/n<b. The rationals are countable by enumerating integer numerators and positive denominators in successive finite sets |k|+n≤m and discarding duplicate fractions. Fix such an enumeration and assign to each It its first rational. Disjointness makes this an injection from [0,1] into a countable set.

For completeness, [0,1] is uncountable. If infinite binary sequences were listed, the sequence whose nth digit differs from the nth digit of sequence n would be absent. These sequences inject into [0,1] through (bk)↦Σk≥12bk/3k. At a first differing digit k, its contribution 2/3k exceeds the largest possible opposite tail 1/3k; thus distinct sequences have distinct images. The injection into the rationals is impossible. This contradiction rules out any real utility, including discontinuous ones; it is stronger than demonstrating failure of a continuous representation.

Worked case 3: finite coding and the failure of a small weight

For n≥3, xn=(1/2+1/n,0) strictly outranks y=(1/2,1). But xn→(1/2,0), which is strictly below y. The weak graph is not closed. On the finite grid {(i/4,j/4):i,j∈{0,1,2,3,4}}, however, the code u(i/4,j/4)=5i+j represents lexicographic order. It assigns the 25 points distinct ranks 0 through 24. If i increases by at least one, its contribution rises by at least 5 while j can fall by at most 4; otherwise j alone decides. This proves all 625 ordered comparisons, which the checker also enumerates.

No fixed ε>0 makes x₁+εx₂ represent lexicographic preference on the full square. Let a=min(ε/2,1/2). Then a>0 and a<ε, so (a,0) is lexicographically above (0,1) but receives a lower weighted score. A successful finite code does not approximate this exact full-domain preference uniformly in the sense of preserving every comparison. The domain change, rather than numerical precision, creates the obstruction.

4. From complete choice reports to a weak order

A menu is a nonempty subset B of X. A complete choice report gives a nonempty C(B)⊆B, intended to include every maximizing alternative. Exact rationalization by a weak order means C(B)={x∈B:x≽y for every y∈B}. This chapter does not interpret an unreported tie as evidence of strict preference unless the report is expressly complete.

Correspondence WARP: for any reported B,D and x,y∈B∩D,
x∈C(B) and y∈C(D) imply x∈C(D).

The condition applies when x was chosen while y was available and y is later chosen while x is available: x may not then be excluded. The quantifiers include ties. Under an exact weak-order rationalizer, x≽y from the first menu and y≽z for every z∈D from the second. Transitivity gives x≽z for every z∈D, so x is also chosen. This proves necessity on any menu family.

Rich-domain converse. Suppose every singleton, pair and triple is reported, along with any other menus in the domain, and every reported choice set is nonempty. Define xRy when x∈C({x,y}). Singletons give reflexivity and pair nonemptiness gives completeness. Two applications of WARP establish a useful equivalence: if x is chosen from B and y∈B, then xRy, since if x were absent from C({x,y}), y would be chosen there and WARP would force x to be chosen there too. Conversely, if xRy and y∈C(B), with x∈B, WARP applied to the pair and B forces x∈C(B).

To prove transitivity, suppose xRy and yRz but not xRz. Repeated alternatives make transitivity immediate, so take three distinct alternatives and choose w∈C({x,y,z}). If w=x, the first equivalence gives xRz, a contradiction. If w=y, xRy and the second equivalence force x into the triple choice set, giving the same contradiction. If w=z, yRz first forces y into that choice set and then xRy forces x. These cases exhaust nonempty triple choice. Hence R is transitive.

For any reported B, a chosen x is R-above every available y by the first implication. Conversely, if x is R-above all y∈B, select z∈C(B), which exists by assumption; xRz forces x∈C(B). Thus R exactly rationalizes all reports. Any exact rationalizer must agree with every pair choice, so this weak order is unique. For an infinite universe the argument still applies to the supplied menus, but nonempty choice was assumed; a weak order alone need not attain a maximum on every infinite menu. The triple-domain hypothesis is used in the proof, not decoration.

Restricted finite-menu criterion. When the menu domain is incomplete and X finite, draw a directed weak edge from each chosen x to every available y; mark it strict if y is excluded. Exact weak-order rationalization is possible if and only if there is no directed cycle containing a strict edge. Necessity follows because weak comparisons compose transitively and one strict step makes the composed comparison strict: if a≻b≽c but c≽a, transitivity would imply b≽a, a contradiction; the other placement of the strict step is analogous. A cycle would say an alternative is strictly above itself.

For sufficiency, allow paths of length zero and put two vertices in one class when each is reachable from the other by weak edges. Reflexivity, symmetry and path concatenation prove that this is an equivalence relation. A strict edge within a class would close a prohibited cycle. Between the classes, induced edges form a finite acyclic directed graph: a quotient cycle would join all its classes by mutual reachability. Such a graph has a vertex with no incoming edge; otherwise following incoming predecessors indefinitely in a finite set repeats a vertex and creates a cycle. Remove such a vertex and repeat to obtain a list. Give earlier classes higher integer ranks, and give each member its class rank. Every weak edge descends or stays within a class; every strict edge descends strictly. Cochosen alternatives have edges in both directions, hence share a class and a utility level. Every excluded member of the same menu lies strictly below each chosen member. The resulting rank utility therefore makes all and only the reported choices maximize. Different orders of otherwise unrelated classes can yield distinct rationalizers.

Worked case 4: three completions and an impossible triple

On X={a,b,c,d}, report C({a,b,c})={a,b}, C({b,c,d})={b}, and C({a,d})={a}. These reports imply a∼b, both strictly above c and d, and impose no comparison between c and d. Accordingly, exactly three weak orders fit: c≻d, d≻c, or c∼d below the top class. With consecutive ranks beginning at zero, the vectors in a,b,c,d order are ((2,2,1,0),(2,2,0,1),(1,1,0,0)). The number of normalized weak orders is 3; arbitrary increasing relabelings do not constitute new preferences.

Now report only C({a,b})={a}, C({b,c})={b}, C({a,c})={c}. Correspondence WARP holds on this restricted pair family: two different pairs share at most one alternative, so they cannot exhibit its forbidden two-alternative reversal. Yet the implied strict cycle a≻b≻c≻a rules out any weak-order rationalizer. Adding a nonempty report for {a,b,c} cannot fix this: choosing a would force a into the pair {a,c}, choosing b would force b into {a,b}, and choosing c would force c into {b,c}. Every nonempty triple choice contains at least one of these contradictions. All 7 possible nonempty triple choices fail WARP. Adding singletons changes neither conclusion.

The rich-domain theorem follows the scoped hypotheses of MIT slides 6–13; the finite graph proof above supplies the completion logic directly. The next budget experiment observes selected maximizers rather than complete correspondence values.

5. Revealed preference from selected budget choices

For observations t=1,…,T with finite T≥1, let xt∈ℝ+L be the selected bundle and pt∈ℝ++L its strictly positive price vector. A price has units of currency per unit of its good. Income mt=pt·xt is currency; the complete feasible budget is Bt={x≥0:pt·x≤mt}. The data say xt is one maximizer over Bt. They do not say it is the unique maximizer, or that other selected bundles affordable there were rejected under a complete choice report. Preferences are assumed fixed across observations; prices, budgets and quantities are taken as exact here.

Let tRDs mean pt·xs≤pt·xt: observation s was affordable when t was selected. Mark this edge strict when the inequality is strict. Let R be its reflexive transitive closure: tRs means there is a finite path of direct edges from t to s. The following conditions use budget observations, not the correspondence convention of Section 4.

Why local nonsatiation matters. Utility is locally nonsatiated on X if every relative neighborhood of every x contains y with u(y)>u(x). If u rationalizes the budgets and tRDs, maximization gives u(xt)≥u(xs). If the edge is strict, its expenditure slack δ=pt·xt−pt·xs>0 leaves a relative neighborhood of xs inside Bt. For example, take a Euclidean radius smaller than δ/‖pt‖. Local nonsatiation supplies a point y in that neighborhood with u(y)>u(xs), and maximization then gives u(xt)≥u(y)>u(xs). Along a path utilities weakly decline; a strict edge makes a strict decline. A strictly affordable reverse edge would contradict that ordering. Hence every locally nonsatiated rationalizer satisfies GARP.

Equivalently, GARP means no direct-affordability cycle contains a strict edge. A GARP violation gives a path t→…→s followed by a strict edge s→t. Conversely, in any cycle with a strict edge, start just after that edge and follow the other edges back to its start; the path plus the strict reverse edge violates GARP. Finite reachability can be computed by adding a relation i→j whenever paths i→k and k→j are already known. After allowing successive intermediate vertices, induction shows that the relation contains exactly paths using those vertices. Repeated vertices can be removed from a path, so finitely many such updates suffice.

Worked case 5: passing pair comparisons and failing a chain

There are three goods and three observed bundles x¹=e₁, x²=e₂, x³=e₃, where ei has one unit of good i and zero of the others. The price rows, and hence the cross-expenditure rows pt·xs, are

P=((1,4/5,6/5),(6/5,1,4/5),(4/5,6/5,1)),   (m₁,m₂,m₃)=(1,1,1).

Besides self edges, direct affordability gives 1→2, 2→3, 3→1, all strict. No directly affordable pair has a strict reverse edge, so the tie-permitting budget pair WARP passes. But 1R3 through 2, while at observation 3, x¹ costs 4/5<1. This violates GARP. The closure reaches every observation from every other observation. A pair-only test has missed a longer inconsistency.

Change only p³₁ from 4/5 to 6/5. The repaired price matrix is ((1,4/5,6/5),(6/5,1,4/5),(6/5,6/5,1)). Its only nonself direct edges are 1→2 and 2→3. Its transitive closure, recording true as 1 and false as 0, is ((1,1,1),(0,1,1),(0,0,1)). Every reverse expenditure along this chain is 6/5≥1, so GARP passes. This is a stated change to the dataset, not an unnoticed rounding tolerance.

Dropping local nonsatiation changes the first result. The utility w(x)=max(0,x₁+x₂+x₃−2) is globally nonsatiated: some sufficiently large bundle has greater utility than any given bundle. Yet every original budget bundle has total quantity at most 5/4, because all prices are at least 4/5 and income is 1. Thus w is zero throughout every budget, and all selected bundles maximize it despite the GARP failure. The flat region is not locally nonsatiated. “Always a better bundle somewhere” is too weak for the strict-affordability proof.

For scope, Varian (1982), pages 946–949 and Appendix I supports the finite revealed-preference and constructive routes. Bergstrom’s three-page notes provide the previously inspected discussion of ties and strict affordability. We use the explicitly local condition just proved and do not silently equate it with global nonsatiation.

6. Constructing an Afriat certificate

For finite positive-price data, an Afriat certificate consists of real labels Ut and strictly positive multipliers λt satisfying, for every ordered pair t,s,

Us ≤ Ut+λtpt·(xs−xt). (A)

The product λt times expenditure has utility-label units; a multiplier is not an observed monetary willingness to pay. With finite T, a common positive rescaling of all U and λ by 1/mintλt makes every λ at least 1 without changing (A). Adding a common constant to every U also preserves the system. Allowing λ=0 instead would admit the meaningless all-zero solution for every dataset, including the inconsistent one.

GARP constructively implies feasibility. Collapse the mutual-reachability classes of R, as in the finite graph proof. The quotient is acyclic. Repeatedly choose a remaining class E with no incoming path from another remaining class, assign it, and remove it. Such a class exists by the finite predecessor argument. This order puts revealed-higher classes earlier, with any unrelated classes ordered arbitrarily. Let B contain the already assigned indices.

For the first class set Ui=1 and λi=1 for all i in it. Within a class iRj and jRi; GARP applied to jRi gives pi·(xj−xi)≥0. Equal labels and positive multipliers therefore satisfy every internal inequality. For a later class E define

UE=mini∈E,j∈B{Uj, Uj+λjpj·(xi−xj)}.

This is a finite minimum, and it immediately supplies every old-to-new inequality in (A). For i∈E and j∈B, let dij=pi·(xj−xi). It is strictly positive: if dij≤0 then iRDj, hence iRj; when j’s class was selected, i’s still-unassigned class would have been an incoming predecessor, contrary to the selection rule. The classes differ, so mutual reachability cannot evade the contradiction. Now put

λE=max{1, maxi∈E,j∈B(Uj−UE)/dij},   Ui=UE, λi=λE for i∈E.

Every denominator is positive, so this finite multiplier exists and is positive. Its definition gives every new-to-old inequality Uj≤UE+λEdij. Within E, equal labels and the nonnegative cross differences implied by GARP handle internal inequalities. Old-to-old inequalities remain unchanged. These are all possibilities, completing the induction. Each step removes a nonempty class, so the procedure terminates after at most T steps. Labels may become negative, which is harmless; a common shift can make them positive if desired. This proof constructs feasibility rather than appealing to an unexecuted linear-programming solver.

Certificate to utility. Define affine functions Lt(x)=Ut+λtpt·(x−xt) and u(x)=mintLt(x) on ℝ+L. A finite minimum of continuous functions is continuous; for example, |min at−min bt|≤max|at−bt|. For θ∈[0,1], each affine term at θx+(1−θ)y is θLt(x)+(1−θ)Lt(y)≥θu(x)+(1−θ)u(y); taking the minimum proves concavity.

If x≥y and x≠y, every δt=λtpt·(x−y) is positive. Since there are finitely many terms, δ=min δt>0, and u(x)=min[Lt(y)+δt]≥u(y)+δ>u(y). Thus the envelope is strictly coordinate-monotone. Increasing any one coordinate by an arbitrarily small positive amount stays in the orthant and raises utility, proving local nonsatiation.

At xs, (A) says every Lt(xs)≥Us, while the self term Ls(xs)=Us. Hence u(xs)=Us. For x affordable in budget s, u(x)≤Ls(x)≤Us. This proves that xs maximizes u over its entire budget, including boundary bundles. The vector difference is xs−xt in (A); reversing it would invalidate the evaluation argument. No derivative, interior first-order condition or unique-maximizer claim is needed.

We have proved the finite equivalence: a locally nonsatiated rationalizer exists ⇔ GARP holds ⇔ a strictly positive Afriat certificate exists ⇔ a continuous, concave, strictly coordinate-monotone rationalizer exists. The final existence statement is stronger in shape than the first, but it does not establish that every rationalizer, or the decision maker’s unknown true preference, is concave. With positive prices each budget is closed and bounded and the envelope is continuous; nevertheless, the direct inequality already proves the observed maximum without a separate existence argument.

The observations alone therefore permit at least two different unique counterfactual demands. An additional selected choice at this new budget would distinguish these particular rationalizers if it equaled one candidate rather than the other, under the maintained fixed-preference and complete-budget assumptions. It would not automatically identify all preferences consistent with the enlarged dataset.

7. Using the results as evidence

First determine whether a record reports all choices or only a selection. Then specify the feasible set, whether prices and quantities are measured exactly, and whether preferences are stable. A missing credit constraint, a changing household composition or an omitted good changes the experiment; a graph calculation cannot repair that specification. For complete restricted menus, report the strict-cycle criterion and the set of remaining comparisons. For selected linear-budget choices, report the cross-expenditure matrix, a path witness for failure, or a positive certificate and its envelope for success.

A passed exact GARP test is an existence statement under these assumptions. It is not a confidence level, proof that measurement error is absent, or identification of a unique demand outside the data. Any tolerance or stochastic alternative would define a different statistical exercise, which this chapter does not undertake. Similarly, allowing a zero price would remove the positivity used to prove strict coordinate monotonicity of the envelope; allowing a zero multiplier would destroy the certificate theorem as stated. None of these changes should be hidden inside a numerical comparison.

The accompanying author check uses exact rational arithmetic on the declared finite cases and tests independent graph paths, certificate inequalities and global-bound coefficients. Its finite checks support arithmetic and implementation; they do not replace the written proofs for infinite commodity domains. The source preparation inspected MIT’s stated general representation result without treating it as its proof, and inspected Varian’s constructive Appendix I while correcting the envelope evaluation direction from the defining inequality. Bergstrom is reuse of an earlier scoped inspection. No claim is made to have inspected the original Afriat paper, statistical revealed-preference extensions or empirical welfare applications.

Solutions, proofs and evaluation criteria

  1. The classes are {c,f} below {b,e} below {a,d}. The requested choices are {a,d}, {b,e}, {c,f}. The only attained labels are 0,1,2; squaring is strictly increasing on that set, so the highest label in any nonempty menu and all alternatives sharing it remain exactly the same. This proves all 63 menus at once. By contrast f(t)=min(t,1) on the attained range is nondecreasing but merges labels 1 and 2. In {a,b} the original choice {a} becomes {a,b}. The failure is loss of a strict comparison, not a change in how ties are arbitrarily broken.

  2. Indifference is an equivalence relation: completeness gives reflexivity, the definition gives symmetry, and preference transitivity gives transitivity. Comparisons are unchanged by choosing other representatives, and distinct classes are strictly and completely ordered. Iteratively selecting a highest class, which exists by finite successive comparison, orders all classes. Assigning increasing integer ranks represents the relation. Conversely, a representing real function inherits the complete, transitive real order. If u and v represent the same relation, equal u values imply equal v values, so f(u(x))=v(x) is well defined. Strictly ordered u values imply strictly ordered v values; thus f is strictly increasing on u(X). The converse follows immediately by preservation of inequalities.

    The lottery has expected u equal to (2+0)/2=1, equal to sure b’s 1. Under u² it has (4+0)/2=2, above sure b’s 1. The representation theorem orders certain alternatives only; extending a score by expectation adds structure on lotteries. Positive affine transformations commute with expectation up to the same positive scaling and constant, but the tested nonlinear transformation does not preserve the expected comparison.

  3. For x=0, strict coordinate monotonicity makes only diagonal t=0 indifferent to x. For x≠0, let M=max xi. Monotonicity brackets x between 0 and M1. Closed graph makes A={t:t1≽x} and B={t:x≽t1} closed in [0,M], and completeness makes them cover the interval. With t*=inf A, closedness gives t*∈A. If t*=0 it belongs to B; otherwise every t<t* belongs to B, and a sequence approaching t* from below gives t*∈B. Thus t*1∼x. Strict diagonal monotonicity and transitivity prohibit two such t values. Substitution of indifferent diagonal bundles proves x≽y iff t(x)≥t(y).

    For xn→x, 0≤t(xn)≤max xni supplies a bounded sequence. Every convergent subsequence passes both weak comparisons to the limit by closed graph and hence has limit t(x) by uniqueness. A subsequence staying a positive distance away would itself have a convergent further subsequence, a contradiction. This proves continuity, including boundary points. Completeness supplies the covering cuts; graph closedness supplies their closedness and the limit comparison; monotonicity supplies brackets and uniqueness; transitivity supplies equivalence substitutions; finite dimension and compact subsequences justify the final argument. Convexity is unused.

  4. For d≥0, the reverse triangle inequality bounds the absolute-value change below by −|d₁−d₂|. Consequently V(x+d)−V(x)≥d₁+d₂−|d₁−d₂|/2≥(d₁+d₂)/2, positive for d≠0. On the diagonal V(t,t)=2t, so the indifferent label is D(x)=V(x)/2. The four values are 3,3,2,5/2. Both (4,0) and (0,4) belong to the weak upper contour of (4,0), while their midpoint (2,2) does not. A quasiconcave representation would make that contour convex, a contradiction. Any representation must preserve the same weak comparison with (4,0), so relabeling leaves this nonconvex contour intact. Concave representations are also excluded since their upper contours are convex.

  5. Assume a real representation u. For every t, lexicographic preference makes (u(t,0),u(t,1)) nonempty. For t<s these intervals are disjoint and ordered because u(t,1)<u(s,0). Rational density follows by choosing n with n(b−a)>1 and an integer between na and nb. Rational countability follows from enumeration of finite sets of numerator/positive-denominator pairs. Assign each interval the first rational in that enumeration. This injects [0,1] into ℚ. Binary diagonalization shows the set of binary sequences cannot be listed; mapping them to ternary sums Σ2bk/3k injects them into [0,1], since the first differing digit dominates the opposing tail. Thus [0,1] is uncountable, contradicting the injection. No continuity assumption on u was used.

    The sequence (1/2+1/n,0), n≥3, is preferred to (1/2,1) but converges to a worse bundle, violating closed graph. On the grid, an increase of i by one gains 5 while the maximum j loss is 4; equal i leaves j decisive. This proves the finite code. For any ε>0, a=min(ε/2,1/2) satisfies 0<a<ε. Lexicographic preference ranks (a,0) over (0,1), whereas x₁+εx₂ ranks them oppositely. Finite-grid representability and full-domain nonrepresentability are consistent.

  6. For any reported B,D and x,y∈B∩D, WARP requires that x∈C(B) and y∈C(D) imply x∈C(D). Under exact weak-order maximization, x≽y and y≽z for every z∈D, so transitivity makes x a maximum in D. For sufficiency define xRy iff x∈C({x,y}). Singletons and nonempty pairs make R reflexive and complete. WARP makes any chosen x weakly R-above every available y: if a pair excluded x, it would choose y and WARP would restore x. WARP also gives xRy and y∈C(B), x∈B ⇒ x∈C(B).

    If xRy and yRz but not xRz, distinct x,y,z have some w chosen from their triple. A choice w=x directly implies xRz; w=y forces x into the triple by xRy; w=z first forces y and then x. Each contradicts not xRz. Repeated alternatives use reflexivity. Thus R is transitive. Every chosen point is R-above all available points; any point R-above them all joins the choice set by applying WARP to a chosen z, which exists by the general nonemptiness assumption. This proves exact rationalization. Pair choices identify R uniquely. Nonempty triples establish transitivity; nonempty general menus supply z in the converse inclusion. The latter is an assumption about reports, not a theorem that every weak order attains maxima on all infinite sets.

  7. A weak-order rationalizer cannot contain a directed cycle with one strict edge because composition gives an impossible strict self-comparison. Conversely, mutual reachability partitions a finite graph into equivalence classes; a strict edge internal to a class would close a forbidden cycle. The quotient is acyclic and has a class with no incoming edge, since otherwise repeatedly following predecessors repeats a vertex. Repeated removal yields an order; assign decreasing ranks along it. Cochosen alternatives are mutually reachable and tie, while chosen-to-excluded edges are strict and cross classes. These ranks exactly reproduce each reported complete set.

    The positive reports require a∼b strictly above c and d. The only freedom is c≻d, d≻c or c∼d; normalized vectors are (2,2,1,0), (2,2,0,1), (1,1,0,0). For the negative reports, different pair menus never contain the same two alternatives, so correspondence WARP cannot reveal a pair reversal; yet a≻b≻c≻a is a strict cycle. Any nonempty triple report contains a, b or c. If it contains a, comparison with {a,c} forces its excluded a to be chosen there. If it contains b, comparison with {a,b} forces b there; if it contains c, comparison with {b,c} forces c there. At least one contradiction occurs for each of the seven nonempty subsets, including multivalued choices.

  8. Each bundle is a unit vector, so column s of cross expenditures selects the sth coordinate of every price row. Both matrices are therefore exactly the stated price matrices, with diagonal incomes 1. The original nonself edges are 1→2, 2→3 and 3→1, each costing 4/5. Every corresponding reverse expenditure is 6/5, so pair WARP passes, but the closure is the all-ones matrix. The chain 1→2→3 with 3→1 strict violates GARP; rotations give the other two witnesses. Repairing p³₁ to 6/5 removes only 3→1. The nonself closure edges are 1R2, 2R3 and 1R3. Their reverse costs are p²·x¹=p³·x²=p³·x¹=6/5. All are at least their reverse budget income 1, so GARP and pair WARP pass. Self comparisons hold with equality. This accounts for every path in the repaired closure.

  9. Strict affordability gives slack δ>0. In a relative ball of radius smaller than δ/‖p‖ around the cheaper observed bundle, continuity of the expenditure functional keeps every point affordable. Local nonsatiation supplies a better point in that ball; maximization of the selected bundle makes its utility strictly higher than that of the cheaper bundle. A revealed path gives weak utility inequalities, so a strict reverse edge would contradict them. This is the GARP necessity proof.

    In the original budgets, p≥(4/5)1 and expenditure≤1 imply Σx≤5/4<2. Therefore w=max(0,Σx−2) is identically zero there and rationalizes every observation. It is globally nonsatiated by moving sufficiently far in a positive coordinate, but a small neighborhood of an observed bundle stays in its flat region, so it is not locally nonsatiated. Finally U=0, λ=0 satisfies the algebraic inequalities even for those GARP-violating observations and yields a flat envelope; it cannot serve as the theorem’s certificate. If all λ>0 and T is finite, c=1/min λ is finite and positive; multiplying every U and λ by c preserves inequalities and gives λ≥1.

  10. The repaired classes are ordered 1,2,3. The algorithm starts (U₁,λ₁)=(1,1), then takes U₂=4/5 and λ₂=1, then U₃=3/5 and λ₃=2, using reverse differences 1/5 throughout. The A slack matrix in case 6 verifies all nine inequalities. In the tied dataset both directions cost exactly 1, so both observations form one class. The algorithm gives (1,1) and (1,1). Both affine terms equal x₁+x₂, which rationalizes both observed selections from their common budget. This is precisely the tied case a no-cycle rule stricter than GARP would unnecessarily exclude.

    For either dataset, a finite affine minimum is continuous by the min/max difference inequality. Each term evaluated at a mixture equals its affine mixture and is at least the corresponding mixture of the two minimum values; taking its minimum proves concavity. For a nonzero nonnegative increment, every strictly positive λp yields a positive plane increment; their finite minimum is positive, so the envelope strictly increases and is locally nonsatiated. Afriat inequalities make every term at xs at least Us, with its own term equal; for every affordable x, the own-budget term is at most Us. This proves full-budget maximization without derivatives. In A’s first budget, x¹ and (0,5/4,0) both spend 1 and both have utility 1; uniqueness fails.

  11. Substituting B yields slack rows (0,1/10,9/10), (1/10,0,0), (1/10,2/5,0); all are nonnegative. A’s values at (5/4,0,0) and (1,0,1/4) are 5/4 and 6/5; B’s are 5/4 and 13/10, so their strict rankings reverse. They cannot be related by an increasing transformation despite fitting all observations.

    For the new budget, min(L₁,L₂,L₃)≤wL₁+(1−w)L₂ for every w∈[0,1]. With A and w=2/3 the right side is −1/15+(16/15)Σx−x₂/5≤19/15. At (7/6,0,1/12) planes 1 and 2 equal 19/15 and plane 3 equals 47/30>19/15. Every maximizer must make both inequalities equalities: Σx=5/4, x₂=0, and L₁=L₂. Solving those equations gives only this candidate. With B and w=4/5, the bound is −13/50+(32/25)Σx−6x₂/25≤67/50. At (4/5,0,9/20), planes 1 and 2 equal 67/50 and plane 3 equals 97/50. The same equality logic gives the unique B candidate. These are global analytic certificates, not sampled approximations to the feasible set.

  12. Illustrative full-credit memo. The dataset records selected budget maximizers; it does not report entire choice correspondences, so neither unreported ties nor unique observed demand follow. The price and income definitions and complete linear budgets must be verified; omitted constraints or changing preferences would change the maintained model. Local nonsatiation, rather than merely a better bundle somewhere, is required for the strict-affordability necessity argument. Pair WARP alone misses the original three-observation cycle, but the repaired closure passes GARP and certificate A supplies an explicit continuous, concave, strictly monotone rationalizer. This is existence of such a rationalizer, not identification of the consumer’s true concavity: B also fits, reverses an unobserved ranking and has a different unique demand at the proposed budget. No probability law, sampling mechanism or statistical confidence procedure has been specified, so the 95% statement is unsupported. A useful next observation is a carefully documented selection at the new budget, with stable preference and feasible-set assumptions; it can distinguish A’s and B’s candidates while leaving other rationalizers possible.

9. Teach-back and delayed retention

Teach-back. In three minutes, explain to a student why the same pair of words, “utility maximization,” leads to different tests for complete menu reports and selected budget observations. A satisfactory answer defines equality with all maxima in the first experiment, shows why excluding an available tied alternative conveys strict information there, and explains why a selected budget choice may share its maximum with unrecorded bundles. It then gives the strict three-pair cycle and the locally nonsatiated budget chain as different failures. An answer that merely names WARP and GARP without their data conventions is incomplete.
