Oral transfer and teach-back · 20 points

After the written and practical work, explain each changed contract without consulting its answer. Allow approximately twenty minutes for all five prompts; this is an uncalibrated guide. Each is worth four points. Require at least 17/20 and three points in every item, with any critical error remediated. The grader should ask which observation would change the conclusion, rather than rewarding fluent repetition.

  1. A new label on an old asset. An analyst proposes to fix a company’s missed payment by reclassifying a late receivable as a current asset and reversing its allowance. Its contractual collection date, collectible amount and account access have not changed. Explain the separate accounting, value and cash questions, the evidence that could justify an estimate revision, and what would actually repair the deadline.
  2. The forecast changes, the traded prices do not. A one-date operating asset pays 120/60 CU in U/D. Traded Arrow prices remain 0.3/0.6, but the analyst changes the physical U probability from 0.8 to 0.2. Compute the change in expected asset payoff and explain whether the supplied market value changes. Describe an additional fact that would justify revaluation.
  3. Fund the implementation cost. An investor has 100 CU, must pay a two-CU fee immediately from that cash, and can allocate the remaining amount without shorting between assets with physical mean returns 6% and 10%. The mandate requires expected final wealth at least 108, with no later contributions or borrowing. Is the target feasible? State the exact resource calculation and a transparent revision the decision maker could consider.
  4. A previously omitted state. A stock costs 80 CU; the one-period bank gross return is one. Stock outcomes 100, 80 and 60 are all possible. Only the stock and bank account are traded, with fractional long/short positions permitted. A call pays max(S−80,0). Explain why the half-share/30-CU debt hedge valid at the extreme outcomes fails in the middle. Construct strictly positive state prices consistent with the traded assets and characterize the call values they permit.
  5. Approval is earlier than delivery. A payment needs five CU at 10:00. There is no available cash. A separately funded lender signs an irrevocable five-CU facility at 09:00, but the contract and payment system deliver funds only at noon. There is no other borrowing or deferral right. State what the signature establishes, what it does not establish, the actual deadline outcome, and the evidence needed for a different feasible plan.
Oral answers and scoring criteria
  1. An asset’s classification does not change its collection date or turn a carrying value into cash. An allowance revision needs evidence about recoverable amount under the applicable stated recognition policy; unchanged evidence does not justify an opportunistic reversal. Even a justified upward value revision would not pay the earlier obligation. Repair needs an actual funded sale, timely advance or contribution, or an effective change in the obligation’s due date, with the new cost, recourse and rights recorded. Credit four: classification/cash distinction one; evidence-based estimate revision one; actual dated repair one; new claims/costs and authority one. Merely reporting higher equity earns no funding credit.
  2. The physical expected payoff falls from 0.8(120)+0.2(60)=108 to 0.2(120)+0.8(60)=72 CU. The supplied traded-price value remains 0.3(120)+0.6(60)=72 in either forecast. Equality between the new physical mean and the price is a numerical coincidence, not a pricing principle. Changed claim payoffs, traded state prices, enforceability or access could require revaluation; a different physical forecast alone does not override the stipulated replicating prices. Credit four: both means one; correct unchanged price one; distinct roles/coincidence one; concrete revaluation evidence one.
  3. Only 98 can be invested. A long-only mixture’s mean return cannot exceed 10%, so maximum expected final wealth is 98(1.10)=107.8 CU, a net expected return of 7.8% on the original 100. The 108 target is infeasible even before considering risk. The decision maker could reduce the target, negotiate a lower funded fee, supply new capital with explicit performance accounting, or authorize a different opportunity set and reassess its risks. None is silently included in this mandate. Credit four: investable amount one; global maximum argument one; exact shortfall/return one; explicit rather than hidden mandate revision one.
  4. Half a share less debt 30 pays 20,10,0 while the call pays 20,0,0, so the middle-state error is ten CU. Any stock/bank payoff is affine in S; the two extreme equations uniquely select that failed middle value, proving nonreplication. Let state prices be a,1−2a,a. They sum to one and price the stock at 100a+80(1−2a)+60a=80; strict positivity requires 0<a<1/2. The corresponding call value is 20a, ranging over the open interval (0,10). These are positive pricing extensions, not a unique selected price or an assertion that all investors agree. The endpoints would require a zero state price despite all three states being possible. Credit four: middle-state error one; nonreplication argument one; valid positive price family one; range and nonuniqueness one.
  5. The signature establishes the promised contractual advance under the stated terms, not beneficiary cash at 10:00. The actual first-cutoff cash is zero and the five-CU payment is missed. Noon receipt cannot undo that history. A revised plan needs demonstrable earlier usable delivery, a distinct funded bridge with its own repayment, or an effective agreed deadline change. Check who owns the money, the account/location and authorization, timestamps, settlement finality and later obligations. Credit four: commitment/delivery distinction one; actual failure one; funded or authorized alternative one; concrete evidence and later claims one.

Delayed retention · 50 points

After 14–21 days, attempt these five new cases without notes or the solutions, allowing about ninety minutes. These timing and spacing suggestions are authored study choices. Each case is worth ten points; require 43/50 overall and at least eight in each case. If a mechanism fails, reconstruct it from the relevant chapter, explain the correction, and solve a newly changed contract before a later retry. A remembered final number is not sufficient.

  1. Allowance and the cash bridge. Under a stipulated teaching accounting policy, revenue is 130 CU and receipts are 110. Gross receivables begin at 20 and end at 40; the allowance begins at zero and a new provision of six creates an ending allowance of six. There are no charge-offs. Cash operating costs are 60 and depreciation is ten. There are no taxes, financing interest or other accruals. Opening cash is ten, capital expenditure is 25, new borrowing is five and dividends are 15. Derive net receivables and income. Reconcile operating cash both using the change in gross receivables and using the change in net receivables. Compute FCFF under these zero-tax/no-interest assumptions and closing cash. Explain the tempting double count and distinguish the provision from an actual payment.
  2. A financed acquisition of contingent claims. An operating opportunity delivers 120/60 CU at the sole terminal date. Strictly positive traded Arrow prices are 0.3/0.6, respectively. A particular investor can negotiate one acquisition for an outlay of 62; this is a limited opportunity, not a claim that 62 is a maintained no-arbitrage quote for freely traded identical assets. A new, fairly priced senior debt claim has face 80, actual recovery min(80,operating payoff), no recourse elsewhere and no transaction costs. Equity receives the residual. The acquisition uses debt proceeds plus the investor’s own cash, and has no other cash balance. Compute operating value, debt proceeds, equity value, the investor’s required contribution and its NPV. Show statewise and present-value conservation. Explain why physical U probability 0.8 does not justify raising debt proceeds to its expected face payment.
  3. A constrained allocation has a different optimum. Two assets have mean one-period returns 0.06 and 0.10 and covariance matrix [[0.01,0.005],[0.005,0.04]]. Weights are w and 1−w; there is no shorting, borrowing, contribution, fee or cash holding. Derive portfolio variance and prove its global minimum over 0≤w≤1. Compute the mean at that minimum. Now require portfolio mean at least 0.08. Determine the entire feasible weight set and its minimum-variance member, including its variance. Explain why changing the mandate changes the solution without changing the covariance estimates, and identify a limitation of treating the supplied estimates as known future risks.
  4. The hedge pays correctly but needs margin. A stock costs 80 CU and ends at 100 or 60, with no dividend. A zero-rate bank account and unrestricted fractional positions give a frictionless call with strike 80 a replication cost of ten: buy half a share for 40 and borrow 30. Consider a seller who receives the ten-CU base premium and uses it entirely for that replication. The bank has separately funded the 30-CU loan at inception. Before maturity it requires five CU of segregated cash margin; it cannot lend or spend that collateral, pays no interest on it, and returns all five before final loan/option settlement. The seller has no other cash. A distinct lender has five CU available, can advance the margin shortfall before its cutoff, and receives gross repayment 1.04 after collateral return. The seller may additionally charge x CU at inception and retain it as cash for margin, with 0≤x≤5; the buyer has separate available cash of 15 and this is a proposed private quote, not an assertion of a competitive equilibrium. Verify the base hedge in both states. Trace the margin and bridge when x=0, then derive the smallest x leaving nonnegative terminal seller cash in both states under this fixed policy. Explain why this amount is a policy-specific funding break-even charge, not a universal no-arbitrage option price.
  5. The FX hedge’s recovery arrives late. A company begins with four H and collects a 20-F invoice before a 36-H supplier payment is due. At the stipulated stressed spot rate one H per F, a dealer with twenty H of separate available cash buys all twenty F before that cutoff. The company already holds a cash-settled short-20-F forward struck at two H per F; its contractual positive payoff is twenty H. Zero inception premium was a contractual assumption, not evidence of fair counterparty-risk pricing. On counterparty default, three H of its separately posted collateral is controlled, transferred and usable before the supplier cutoff. The remaining unsecured claim is seventeen H and recovers 50%, but only after that cutoff; this recovery excludes the collateral. No other cash arrives. A bridge provider has nine H available before the cutoff and requires repayment at gross factor 1.05 after the late recovery. A separate donor has one H of available cash and may make an irrevocable nonrepayable grant g∈[0,1] before the supplier cutoff, buying no claim. Compute ultimate forward recovery, early payment gap, and the late deficit with a bridge alone. Derive the minimum grant and corresponding bridge that pay every stated obligation on time; prove necessity and sufficiency. Do not count collateral twice or erase the bridge’s interest.
Retention solutions, proofs and rubrics
  1. Net receivables 34; income 54; operating cash 50; FCFF 25; closing cash 25 CU. Gross receivables reconcile as 20+130−110=40. The provision reduces net receivables to 40−6=34 and income to 130−60−10−6=54. It is an expense/allowance entry, not a cash disbursement. Using gross receivables, operating cash is 54+10+6−(40−20)=50. Using net receivables instead, it is 54+10−(34−20)=50. The latter change already incorporates the six-unit allowance increase, so adding the provision again would incorrectly produce 56. Direct receipts less paid costs, 110−60=50, provide an independent check. With the explicitly zero tax and financing interest, FCFF is operating cash less capital expenditure, 50−25=25. Ending cash is 10+50−25+5−15=25. Debt issuance and dividends belong to financing; the new allowance is neither a receipt nor a source available to fund either. Credit ten: receivable and income values two; both valid bridges three; double-counting explanation two; FCFF one; closing cash and classification two.
  2. Operating value 72; debt proceeds 60; equity value 12; own cash two; investor NPV ten CU. In U, 120 is allocated as debt 80 and equity 40; in D, 60 goes to debt and equity gets zero. Operating value is 0.3(120)+0.6(60)=72. Debt raises 0.3(80)+0.6(60)=60, rather than its promised face 80. Equity is worth 0.3(40)+0.6(0)=12, and 60+12=72 matches the priced operating resources. The seller receives the negotiated 62, funded by lender proceeds 60 and investor cash two. Investor NPV is 12−2=10, equivalently operating value 72 less acquisition cost 62. The financing division has not created another ten of total value. Under physical U probability 0.8, the debt’s mean payment is 0.8(80)+0.2(60)=76 and mean operating payoff is 108; neither is its current price. The debt’s default-contingent payoff and the supplied state prices determine its proceeds. Positive NPV describes this available acquisition under the stated market and access assumptions, not proof that such an opportunity persists in a frictionless competitive market. Credit ten: both actual state allocations two; three coherent values three; funded contribution and NPV two; face/expectation/price distinction two; opportunity-scope limitation one.
  3. Unconstrained minimum w=7/8; constrained minimum w=1/2. Variance is 0.01w²+0.04(1−w)²+2(0.005)w(1−w)=0.04w²−0.07w+0.04. The covariance matrix is positive definite: its first diagonal entry is positive and determinant is 0.000375=3/8000>0. Completing the square gives V(w)=(1/25)(w−7/8)²+3/320. The unique global minimum lies within [0,1], at w=7/8 with variance 3/320=0.009375. Its mean is 0.1−0.04(7/8)=13/200=0.065. The target 0.1−0.04w≥0.08 is equivalent to w≤1/2, so feasible weights are [0,1/2]. On that entire interval V′(w)=0.08w−0.07 is negative; hence the unique constrained minimum is its right endpoint w=1/2, variance 3/200=0.015 and mean 0.08. The more demanding expected-return mandate excludes the former solution. Estimated means/covariances can shift, especially in stress; the calculation is conditional and says neither that realized return meets the target nor that estimation error has been priced away. Credit ten: variance derivation two; global minimum proof and variance two; mean one; complete feasible set two; constrained optimum/variance two; estimation/realized-return limitation one.
  4. The additional break-even charge is 5/26 CU, approximately 0.192308. Half a share returns 50 or 30; repaying the original zero-rate loan 30 leaves 20 or zero, exactly the call obligation. At inception, base premium ten plus that original loan thirty funds the forty-CU share purchase. There is no residual base-premium cash. With x=0 the separate bridge delivers five, all of which is posted as margin. Its return of five precedes the bridge repayment of 5(1.04)=5.2, so the seller loses 0.2 despite correct derivative replication. The margin principal is not a trading expense, and the original thirty-CU loan has not disappeared.

    With additional cash x, the seller posts x+(5−x)=5 and borrows only 5−x from the distinct provider. This is between zero and five and fits that provider’s endowment. After the original hedge/option payments cancel in each state, remaining seller cash is 5−1.04(5−x)=1.04x−0.2. Thus nonnegative terminal cash is equivalent to x≥0.2/1.04=5/26. Equality is feasible: the bridge is 125/26, its repayment is exactly five, and all collateral return is allocated once. The buyer pays 10+5/26=265/26, below its supplied cash 15. Every smaller x produces a terminal funding loss under this same policy. This is a private break-even condition given asymmetric funding, the imposed collateral process and the decision to retain the extra charge. It is not a replacement for the frictionless replication theorem, a claim that the quote is competitively accepted, or a universal option price across traders with different funding access. Credit ten: original two-state hedge two; dated margin/bridge path two; shortfall at x=0 one; sharp charge with necessity/sufficiency three; private-policy versus market-price scope two.

  5. Minimum grant 19/21 H and bridge 170/21 H. The forward’s ultimate collected amount is collateral three plus unsecured recovery 0.5(20−3)=8.5, totaling 11.5 H. Its original twenty-H claim is not fully paid. The dealer actually supplies twenty H for the invoice’s twenty F, using its separate twenty-H endowment. Before the supplier cutoff, only 4+20+3=27 H is usable, so the gap is nine. A bridge of nine pays the supplier, but repayment later is 9(1.05)=9.45 against late cash 8.5, leaving a deficit of 0.95 H. Even ultimate unfinanced cash, 4+20+11.5=35.5, is below the supplier’s 36; timing is not the only problem.

    For any early grant g, the smallest bridge that can pay the supplier is b=9−g. Taking a larger bridge would increase later interest and cannot improve final feasibility: if b exceeds that minimum, the unused early cash carries, and final cash is 27+g+b−36+8.5−1.05b=g−0.5−0.05b. The first-date constraint b≥9−g therefore implies final cash≤1.05g−0.95. Necessity is g≥0.95/1.05=19/21. At equality choose b=9−19/21=170/21, which is below the provider’s nine-H capacity; the grant is below the donor’s one-H capacity. The supplier receives exactly 36 at its cutoff, with zero company cash left. Late recovery 8.5 equals 1.05(170/21), so the bridge is paid in full and final cash is zero. The donor supplies the grant from its own endowment and receives no reimbursement; it is not another disguised loan. This proves feasibility of all named company obligations in the supplied state, not the counterparty’s default frequency or the forward’s fair inception price. Credit ten: recovery base and total two; early cash gap one; bridge-only late deficit two; global lower bound on grant two; equality with dated cash and provider budgets two; scope one.

From a wrong answer to a new capability

Keep a correction log with four entries: the exact failed proposition, the violated account or assumption, a reconstructed derivation, and the result of a fresh transfer. Useful routes are accruals and earnings quality for retention 1; acquisition economics and state prices for retention 2; constrained allocation and execution costs for retention 3; collateral funding and replication for retention 4; and credit recovery, funding and cross-border claims for retention 5. Change the binding assumption—collection date, recovery priority, investment constraint or funding term—before claiming that the original error is repaired.

Where the connected arguments are taught

  • From transactions to a forecast: double entry, long-lived assets, financing obligations, ownership, relevant costs, business ratios, linked forecasts, comparability and forensic integration.
  • From operating choices to value: incremental investment, terminal economics, financing frictions, liquidity and payout, comparables, flexibility, private financing, restructuring and capital allocation.
  • From return evidence to an investable mandate: equilibrium pricing, factors, predictability, performance evaluation, dynamic allocation, illiquidity and investment policy.
  • From ideal hedges to qualified model use: rate sensitivity, term-structure measures, stochastic calculus, diffusion replication, hedge sensitivities, numerical error, incompleteness and model governance.
  • From a claim to institutional performance: intermediation, tail risk, operational and legal evidence, long-horizon promises, regulation and incentives, systemic transmission and risk governance.

What passes from the finance core into graduate work

An accounting identity disciplines what can be claimed about a company. A valuation model states which future claims are priced and under what assumptions. A portfolio decision chooses exposures subject to an investor’s objectives and resources. A hedge redistributes state payoffs and creates its own financing and execution needs. An institution must actually perform the resulting obligations within its authority. None of these steps can substitute for the others.

The next task is to assess how such models meet evidence. Advanced econometrics separates the object of interest from what a dataset identifies and what an estimator computes; graduate theory develops the assumptions behind equilibrium and incentive arguments; graduate macro-finance connects household, firm and intermediary decisions over time. Preserve the habits practiced here: exact units and dates, explicit information sets, funded decisions, reproducible calculations and conclusions limited by their evidence.

Passing this package demonstrates performance on its supplied tasks under the recorded conditions. It does not establish every frontier result, universal examination success, legal authority, investment returns or an institutional appointment. The library’s complete production plan remains separate from the chapters and assessments already written and reviewed.
