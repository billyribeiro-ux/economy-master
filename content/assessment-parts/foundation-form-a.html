Foundation Form A — From a model to a defensible decision

Allow 180 minutes, closed notes and solutions, with a basic nonprogrammable calculator. Complete all four 25-point domains; each has five parts worth 5 points each. Show reasoning, dates and units. Exact expressions or correctly rounded decimals are acceptable. All firms, measurements, economic coefficients and probability laws below are original synthetic teaching cases. The stated rates and models are assumptions, not current market facts.

  1. D1. An investment, its alternative and its funding — 25 points

    A company can buy a machine for 500 USD now, at time 0. It would use an already owned room for two years. Without the project, the room would earn rental receipts of 54 USD at year 1 and 58.32 USD at year 2; those receipts are forgone if the project proceeds. Machine operations generate net cash receipts before financing of 324 USD at year 1 and 349.92 USD at year 2. The machine is sold for 116.64 USD at year 2. The room remains owned in either alternative; its later use is identical. An unrecoverable 40 USD feasibility study was paid before time 0 and is already reflected in the opening accounts.

    All future cash amounts are nominal USD paid at the stated year-ends. Assume annual inflation of 8%, an annual effective nominal project opportunity rate of 13.4%, and no omitted taxes, costs or cash flows. Treat these cash flows as stipulated for the valuation exercise. For funding, a bank offers a loan of exactly 300 USD at time 0, with 30 USD interest at each year-end and all 300 USD principal repaid at year 2. Its financing cash flows are separate from the project-versus-rental comparison.

    Immediately before the new loan and purchase, the company's balance sheet contains cash 1,000 USD and the room at a book value of 300 USD, debt 400 USD and equity 900 USD. There are no other assets or obligations in this snapshot. At time 0, record the new machine at its purchase cost; no time has elapsed for depreciation. A binding cash policy requires at least 850 USD immediately after initiating the project.

    1. Construct the three dated incremental project-versus-rental cash flows before financing. Explain the treatment of the old study, the forgone rent and the room's book value.
    2. Derive the effective real discount rate. Compute NPV using both nominal cash flows and time-0 purchasing-power cash flows, and explain why the answers agree. State the conditional valuation decision.
    3. List the loan's cash flows separately. Add them to the incremental project cash flows to obtain the cash-flow sequence after this financing. Explain why loan proceeds are not operating revenue, principal repayment is not an operating expense, and this new sequence should not automatically be discounted at the project's unfinanced opportunity rate.
    4. Reconcile the company's time-0 balance sheet after receiving the loan and buying the machine. Show cash, other assets, debt, equity and the balance-sheet identity. Does borrowing itself create profit or raise book equity here?
    5. Check the 850 USD cash policy, quantify any funding shortfall, and give a feasible-to-evaluate next step without assuming that further finance is already available. Explain why positive NPV alone does not establish liquidity feasibility or an unconditional recommendation.

  2. D2. Workload equilibrium and constrained allocation — 25 points

    A delivery operation's normalized steady workload u is required to solve u = T(u), where

    T(u) = (1 + u²)/4,   u ∈ [0, 1].

    After determining its fixed point u*, management allocates continuous delivery blocks x and y, with x ≥ 0, y ≥ 0 and x + y ≤ c, where 0 ≤ c ≤ 4. The objective is

    F(x,y;u*) = (4 + u*)x + (2 + u*)y − (x² + y²)/2.

    The numerical quantities x, y and c count standardized delivery blocks per day; F is measured in hundreds of USD per day, with the displayed coefficients incorporating those units. Workload u is dimensionless and is held fixed when capacity c changes. No integer restrictions are imposed. Define V(c) as the maximum attainable F at capacity c.

    1. Show that T maps [0,1] into itself and has a Lipschitz constant q = 1/2 there. Prove existence and uniqueness of a fixed point in this interval, checking the conditions of any theorem used. Obtain its exact value and exclude the other algebraic root.
    2. Starting at u₀ = 0, calculate u₁, u₂ and u₃. Derive a bound on |u₃ − u*| from the latest increment and q. Does that bound certify an error below 0.001? Using the bound qⁿ|u₁ − u₀|/(1 − q), find the smallest n for which this particular a priori bound is below 0.001; do not confuse this with the smallest n having that actual error.
    3. Explain why the capacity constraint binds at an optimum for 0 < c ≤ 4. Substitute y = c − x, derive the one-variable derivative, and find the unique optimal allocations at c = 1 and c = 4. Check the relevant boundary rather than applying an interior formula outside its domain.
    4. Derive x*(c), y*(c) and V(c) for the entire interval 0 ≤ c ≤ 4, identifying the capacity at which the active boundary changes. Show that V and its first derivative agree across that transition.
    5. Give V′(4) from the left and its units. Approximate V(4) using u₃, and propagate your part (b) workload-error bound into a bound on this value error, in both the objective's units and USD per day. Does this arithmetic certificate establish that the economic coefficients are accurate?

  3. D3. Buying a signal and interpreting statistical evidence — 25 points

    A machine is in a fault state H with prior probability 1/5 and a sound state L otherwise. A risk-neutral operator can service it now or leave it alone. Service costs 40 USD in either state and prevents all fault damage. Leaving it alone costs 160 USD if H and zero if L. These are complete losses over the decision horizon; both actions are feasible.

    A diagnostic report is positive with probability 3/4 conditional on H and 1/4 conditional on L. It is negative otherwise. The report does not change the state or service effectiveness. If acquired, it is observed before the service decision. Later fee questions assume any report invoice must be paid regardless of the subsequent service action, and no liquidity constraint binds.

    1. Construct the four joint probabilities for state and report. Find the positive-report probability and both posterior fault probabilities. Identify which probability is a signal likelihood and which is a posterior.
    2. For a fault belief p, derive the service threshold, including the equality case. Find the best action and expected loss without a report, and the best action after each report.
    3. Calculate expected loss under the optimal free-report policy. Find the gross expected value of sample information and of perfect information, measured as reductions in expected loss. Verify their ordering and explain why a favorable report's conditional loss is not the ex ante value of acquiring the report.
    4. The vendor charges 28 USD only when the report is positive and zero otherwise, irrespective of whether service is chosen. Compute the expected invoice and total expected loss if the report is bought. Decide whether to buy and derive the largest positive-report-only fee that leaves the operator indifferent. Does the realized invoice change the optimal service action after observing a report under these assumptions?
    5. Separately, a preregistered pilot observes 36 iid normal cost savings with unknown fixed mean μ and known standard deviation 12 USD. Its sample mean is 5 USD. Construct a two-sided 95% confidence interval using z0.975 = 1.96, and derive its repeated-sampling coverage from the normal pivot. Evaluate the claim that this means a 95% posterior probability that μ lies in the realized interval or that μ exceeds a 4 USD cost threshold. Contrast with the explicit Bayesian probabilities in part (a).

Foundation Form A solutions and point allocation

Each part is worth 5 points, with 25 per domain and 100 overall. Award consistent arithmetic follow-through when the later method is correct. A wrong information set, counterfactual, unit, feasibility claim or probability interpretation does not earn its associated reasoning point. Equivalent valid arguments receive credit.

  1. D1 solutions

    1. Before financing, incremental flows at years (0,1,2) are (−500, 324 − 54, 349.92 + 116.64 − 58.32) = (−500, 270, 408.24) nominal USD. The study is sunk: neither present alternative recovers or changes its past payment. Rent is a future opportunity cost even though the room is owned. Its 300 USD book value is a stock in the accounts, not an additional dated cash outflow; the room is retained in both alternatives. Including that book value as a new cost would double-count an irrelevant common holding rather than value a stated forgone use.

      Scoring: three dated flows 2; sunk-study treatment 1; rental opportunity cost 1; book-value/common-asset distinction 1.

    2. Consistency requires (1 + rnom) = (1 + π)(1 + rreal), so rreal = 1.134/1.08 − 1 = 0.05. Deflating at each date gives 270/1.08 = 250 and 408.24/1.08² = 350 time-0-purchasing-power USD. Thus

      NPV = −500 + 270/1.134 + 408.24/1.134²
      = −500 + 250/1.05 + 350/1.05² = 500/9 ≈ 55.555556 time-0 USD.

      The equality follows period by period because 1.134ᵗ = 1.08ᵗ1.05ᵗ. Positive NPV favors the project over renting under these complete incremental flows and the stipulated opportunity rate, subject to feasibility and the stated model. Discounting nominal amounts at 5% would overstate this valuation.

      Scoring: real-rate derivation 1; correctly dated real flows 1; nominal NPV 1; real NPV and consistency explanation 1; conditional decision 1.

    3. The loan sequence is (+300, −30, −330) USD, the final amount containing 30 interest and 300 principal. Adding it yields (−200, 240, 78.24) USD after this financing. Proceeds create both cash and an obligation, not sales or operating profit. Principal repayment reduces cash and debt, not an operating expense; interest is a financing cost. The asset-level rate was specified for the unfinanced incremental comparison. Financing changes the cash-flow claim and can change its appropriate required return, so inserting debt cash flows into the old NPV formula without a coherent financing/claim valuation would answer an unstated question. No equity discount rate is supplied.

      Scoring: loan sequence 1; combined sequence 1; proceeds classification 1; principal/interest classification 1; discount-rate/claim consistency 1.

    4. Cash becomes 1,000 + 300 − 500 = 800 USD. Other assets are the room 300 and new machine 500, so total assets are 1,600. Debt is 400 + 300 = 700 and equity remains 900. The identity is 800 + 300 + 500 = 700 + 900. The loan increases assets and liabilities together; purchasing the machine swaps cash for an asset. Neither time-0 transaction creates profit or book equity here. The already expensed study is not deducted again.

      Scoring: cash 1; other assets/total 1; debt and equity 1; balanced identity 1; no profit/equity creation or double counting 1.

    5. Cash 800 is 50 USD below the required 850, so the specified funding arrangement fails the policy. A proposed next step is to seek and evaluate an additional 50 USD equity contribution, renegotiated funding, a lower time-0 cost or a delayed project; none is assumed already feasible. Any additional loan would require new debt-service and liability calculations, and additional equity is not sales revenue. Positive NPV compares discounted complete flows under a rate/model; it does not supply cash at a required date, authorize overriding a constraint or establish that real forecasts and alternatives equal the stipulated values. Under the currently supplied arrangement the project cannot proceed while honoring the policy.

      Scoring: policy failure 1; shortfall 1; conditional practical next step 1; liquidity versus value distinction 1; qualified recommendation/model scope 1.

  2. D2 solutions

    1. On [0,1], T is increasing and T([0,1]) = [1/4,1/2] ⊂ [0,1]. It is continuously differentiable, with T′(u) = u/2 between 0 and 1/2; the mean value theorem gives |T(u) − T(v)| ≤ (1/2)|u − v|. The interval is closed in complete ℝ, hence complete and nonempty, so the contraction theorem supplies a fixed point. Alternatively, the continuous function T(u) − u is positive at 0 and negative at 1, so the intermediate value theorem supplies existence. If u and v are fixed, |u − v| ≤ (1/2)|u − v| forces equality, proving uniqueness. The equation is u² − 4u + 1 = 0, with roots 2 ± √3; only u* = 2 − √3 ≈ 0.267949192 lies in [0,1].

      Scoring: self-map 1; global Lipschitz argument 1; valid existence conditions 1; uniqueness argument 1; root and excluded alternative 1.

    2. u₁ = 1/4, u₂ = 17/64 = 0.265625 and u₃ = 4385/16384 ≈ 0.267639160. Iteration increments contract: |uk+1 − uk| ≤ q|uk − uk−1|. Since the iterates converge to u*, telescoping the tail and summing its geometric bound gives |uₙ − u*| ≤ [q/(1 − q)]|uₙ − un−1|. At n = 3 this is 33/16384 ≈ 0.002014160, which does not certify an error below 0.001, although the actual error is smaller. The stated a priori bound is (1/2)ⁿ(1/4)/(1/2) = 2−n−1. It is 1/512 at n = 8 and 1/1024 at n = 9, so 9 is the first n passing that bound. This is a conservative certificate, not a claim that nine iterations are necessary in fact.

      Scoring: three iterates 1; geometric-tail derivation 1; latest-step bound 1; certification interpretation 1; smallest n for the specified a priori bound 1.

    3. The feasible triangle is nonempty and compact, and F is continuous, so a maximum exists. If x + y < c ≤ 4, then x < 4 and Fx = 4 + u* − x > 0: slightly increasing x is feasible and raises F. Thus capacity binds for positive c. With y = c − x and 0 ≤ x ≤ c, the derivative is 2 + c − 2x and the second derivative is −2. The stationary candidate is x = (c + 2)/2, but it must lie in [0,c]. For c = 1 that candidate is 1.5 > 1, the derivative is positive throughout [0,1], and the unique maximum is (x,y) = (1,0). For c = 4 the candidate (3,1) is feasible and is the unique global maximum by strict concavity. Both comparisons use the boundary and the full feasible interval.

      Scoring: existence/binding argument 1; derivative and curvature 1; c = 1 corner 1; c = 4 interior allocation 1; feasibility/global uniqueness justification 1.

    4. For 0 ≤ c ≤ 2 the stationary x would exceed c (or equal it at 2), so x* = c and y* = 0. For 2 ≤ c ≤ 4, x* = (c + 2)/2 and y* = (c − 2)/2. Substitution gives

      V(c) = (4 + u*)c − c²/2,   0 ≤ c ≤ 2;
      V(c) = 1 + (3 + u*)c − c²/4,   2 ≤ c ≤ 4.

      The zero-y boundary stops binding beyond c = 2. Both value formulas give 6 + 2u* at 2. Their derivatives are 4 + u* − c and 3 + u* − c/2, both equal to 2 + u* at 2. Thus V and V′ join continuously, although their second derivatives differ. At c = 0 the sole feasible allocation is (0,0) and V = 0, consistent with the first formula.

      Scoring: corner allocation range 1; interior allocation range/transition 1; first value formula 1; second value formula 1; value/derivative matching and zero-capacity boundary 1.

    5. The left marginal value at 4 is 3 + u* − 4/2 = 1 + u* = 3 − √3 ≈ 1.267949192 hundreds of USD per day per added block per day. It describes a local capacity change from within the stated range. V(4) = 9 + 4u* = 17 − 4√3 ≈ 10.071796770 hundreds of USD per day. Using u₃ gives 9 + 4u₃ = 41249/4096 ≈ 10.070556641. Since value depends linearly on u with coefficient 4 here, its error is at most 4(33/16384) = 33/4096 ≈ 0.008056641 hundreds of USD per day, or 0.805664063 USD per day. This controls the error introduced by the approximate workload within this stipulated model. It supplies no bound on misspecified demand, costs or other economic coefficients.

      Scoring: marginal value and one-sided scope 1; its units 1; approximate V(4) 1; propagated bound in both unit systems 1; arithmetic/model distinction 1.

  3. D3 solutions

    1. The joint masses are P(H,+) = (1/5)(3/4) = 3/20, P(H,−) = 1/20, P(L,+) = (4/5)(1/4) = 1/5 and P(L,−) = 3/5. Therefore P(+) = 7/20 and P(−) = 13/20. Bayes gives P(H | +) = (3/20)/(7/20) = 3/7 and P(H | −) = (1/20)/(13/20) = 1/13. The sensitivity 3/4 is P(+ | H), a report likelihood conditional on the state; it is not the posterior fault probability after a positive report.

      Scoring: four-cell joint law 1; report probabilities 1; positive posterior 1; negative posterior 1; likelihood/posterior direction 1.

    2. Service has loss 40; no service has expected loss 160p. Service is preferred when 40 < 160p, or p > 1/4; at equality both actions tie. Without a report p = 1/5, so leave the machine alone with expected loss 32 USD rather than 40. After a positive report p = 3/7 > 1/4, so service; after a negative report p = 1/13 < 1/4, so do not service. These minimize expected money losses under the stated risk-neutral objective.

      Scoring: expected losses/threshold 2; equality case 1; prior action/loss 1; both report actions 1.

    3. The free-report policy incurs 40 USD whenever positive and fault damage only in the joint event (H,−). Thus expected loss is (7/20)40 + (1/20)160 = 14 + 8 = 22 USD. Equivalently average conditional losses 40 and 160/13 using report probabilities. Gross EVSI = 32 − 22 = 10 USD. Perfect information services only in H, producing expected loss (1/5)40 = 8 USD and EVPI = 32 − 8 = 24 USD. Hence 0 ≤ 10 ≤ 24: a report can be ignored, while knowing the state can do at least as well. The conditional loss 40 after a positive report is neither an improvement over the optimal prior policy nor an average over possible reports; information value is the ex ante reduction relative to the best no-report action.

      Scoring: free-report expected loss 1; EVSI 1; perfect-information loss/EVPI 1; ordering and reason 1; ex ante versus conditional distinction 1.

    4. The expected invoice is (7/20)28 = 49/5 = 9.8 USD. Total expected loss is 22 + 9.8 = 31.8 USD, below the no-report 32 by 0.2, so buy under the stipulated objective and feasibility. If the positive-only fee is f, indifference requires 22 + (7/20)f = 32, giving f = 200/7 ≈ 28.571429 USD. Comparing the full conditional invoice 28 directly with EVSI 10 would ignore its probability of payment. Once the report is observed, the invoice is owed regardless of service, so it adds the same realized loss to both actions and does not change their ranking. A fee conditional on service, a binding liquidity constraint or a different risk objective would require a new calculation.

      Scoring: expected fee 1; total expected loss and purchase 1; indifference fee 1; correct contingent-fee comparison 1; unchanged conditional action and scope 1.

    5. SE = 12/√36 = 2 USD and the interval is 5 ± 1.96(2) = [1.08, 8.92] USD. Under the stipulated iid normal model, Z = (X̄ − μ)/(12/√36) is exactly standard normal for every fixed μ. Therefore Pμ(|Z| ≤ 1.96) ≈ 0.95, which is equivalent to the random interval [X̄ − 1.96·2, X̄ + 1.96·2] covering μ. The small approximation here comes from rounding the normal quantile to 1.96, not from a central-limit approximation. Once the data are fixed, this frequentist statement does not assign posterior mass to the realized interval or to μ > 4. The interval also contains 4. A posterior requires a prior and likelihood for μ; part (a) had an explicitly supplied state prior and report likelihood, which licensed its Bayesian probabilities.

      Scoring: SE and interval 2; pivot/coverage derivation 2; posterior and cost-threshold contrast 1.

  4. D4 solutions

    1. The source-history key is (firm, reference month, release date); the selected panel key is (firm, reference month). Validate uniqueness at the source key, retain releases no later than 15 March, then choose the greatest remaining release date separately for each required firm-month.       Scoring: both keys 1; filter-then-select rule 1; selected values 1; correctly vintaged change 1; duplicate-version explanation 1.

    2. x̄ = 1 and ȳ = 4. The centered x sum of squares is 1 + 0 + 1 + 1 + 0 + 1 = 4. The centered cross-product sum is 2 + 0 + 1 + 1 + 0 + 2 = 6. Hence b = 6/4 = 3/2 and a = 4 − 3/2 = 5/2. Fitted values in each month are (5/2,4,11/2), and residuals are (−1/2,0,−1/2,1/2,0,1/2). Their sum is zero; their x-weighted sum is −1 + 1 = 0. They establish sample residual orthogonality and, with distinct x values, a unique minimizing line; they do not identify a causal effect or prove future prediction accuracy.

      Scoring: means/centered sums 1; coefficients 1; residual vector 1; both normal equations 1; SSE units and projection scope 1.

    3. For April y = (3,5,7), line errors are (1/2,1,3/2), giving MSE (1/4 + 1 + 9/4)/3 = 7/6. Constant forecasts are all 4, with errors (−1,1,3), giving MSE (1 + 1 + 9)/3 = 11/3. April's labels are released on 10 May, so the 11 May selection can choose the line. For June y = (4,4,4), line errors are (3/2,0,−3/2), giving MSE 3/2; the constant's MSE is zero.       Scoring: April MSEs 1; selection and availability 1; June MSEs 1; score date and squared units 1; original-protocol score and reselection limit 1.

    4. The unconstrained latest selector uses A's January revision released on 20 March, five days after the claimed forecast origin. Its historical forecast is infeasible even though the reference month is January. A corrected algorithm validates the unique (firm,month,release) keys and dates; filters release ≤ cutoff; chooses the maximum eligible release within each (firm,month); and verifies the selected keys equal the required January–February three-firm grid, failing on duplicates or uncovered active records rather than inventing values. Preserve original bytes, cutoff, definitions, selection code and the resulting model/forecast log. The revision is available on 11 May, so using it in a genuinely new June forecast is not automatically look-ahead. It would, however, be a new declared fitting procedure, not the unchanged March-frozen rule specified here. Preserve both versions and origins, state when the new rule was chosen, and evaluate it on outcomes not yet used in its selection. Do not rewrite the original March forecast or silently replace its score.

      Scoring: precise unavailable-version error 1; cutoff before per-key maximum 1; duplicate/coverage checks 1; legitimate later use distinguished from historical leakage 1; preserved versions/protocol and evaluation boundary 1.

    5. The missing cell is large firms at dose zero. Small A supplies a zero-dose observation, but it is not by itself a valid no-automation counterfactual for large B/C. Extending the pooled line to zero for large firms adds a functional-form/transport assumption across size and dose; residual orthogonality cannot supply it. A causal interpretation also needs a well-defined intervention, consistency/noninterference and an assignment or adjustment argument establishing the relevant counterfactual comparisons. No such exchangeability has been supplied, and the stipulated target lacks observed zero-dose support among large firms. Repeated months give more rows under the same assignments, not the missing untreated large-firm outcomes. Persistent firm shocks can also make their rows dependent, so six rows do not automatically behave like six iid draws. More observations or exact computation cannot by themselves repair the identification gap.

      Scoring: missing target support cell 1; role of pooled extrapolation 1; residual orthogonality versus causal assumptions 1; repeated rows do not supply counterfactuals 1; dependence/independent-evidence limitation 1.
