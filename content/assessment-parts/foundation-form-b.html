Form B — Reconcile, certify and challenge the claim

100 points; 180 minutes; closed notes and solutions; basic nonprogrammable calculator. There are four essential domains, each worth 25 points through five parts worth 5 points each. Show the reasoning, state units and distinguish exact results from approximations. Exact fractions, powers and square-root expressions are acceptable. No code execution or external information is required. All businesses, records, populations and mechanisms below are original synthetic teaching constructions.

  1. D1 — Earnings, liquidity and financing, 25 points.

    Parts (d)–(e) concern a separate financing choice, not an extra transaction in the preceding month. A supplier permits an invoice to be settled for 900 USD now or 1000 USD exactly three months later. A bank will lend exactly 900 USD now, with no fees and no interim payments, quoting a nominal annual rate of 24% compounded monthly; the balance is repaid after three months.     5. Compare all three financing choices by equivalent costs at the three-month date, including the opportunity cost of internal cash. Choose the cheapest under the assumptions and explain why “internal cash carries no cost because no interest invoice arrives” is wrong. (5)
  2. D2 — An allocation with a verifiable certificate, 25 points.

    Two divisible activities x and y, measured in the same activity unit, share capacity b. The loss is f(x,y)=(x−4)²+2(y−3)² in squared activity units. Define V(b) as the minimum of f over x≥0, y≥0 and x+y≤b. Begin with b=4. Use the minimization convention L=f+λ(x+y−b), λ≥0, retaining x,y≥0 in the domain when defining the dual lower bound. The activities and loss are a stipulated mathematical model, not an estimated causal relationship.

    1. Show that the b=4 problem has an attained unique global minimizer. Identify the properties of the feasible set and of f that establish existence and uniqueness; a first-order equation by itself is insufficient. (5)
    2. Solve for the minimizing activities and a valid capacity multiplier. Verify feasibility, stationarity with the nonnegativity multipliers, complementary slackness and the attained loss. (5)
    3. Complete the squares in L at λ=4 to compute the dual value. Use weak duality to certify the candidate globally. For the feasible candidate (1,3), calculate the primal–dual gap and explain exactly what error it bounds. (5)
    4. Keep λ=4 and derive a lower bound for V(b) at another nonnegative capacity b. Find the exact optima and values at b=0 and b=8. Compare them with this bound and explain why the old multiplier is not an exact finite-change formula. (5)
    5. Suppose capacity instead follows the deterministic update bt+1=2+bt/2, with b0=0 and integer t≥0. Derive its equilibrium and exact path, establish stability and nonnegativity, and find the first date with |bt−4|≤1/8 activity unit. State what assumption makes this path calculation applicable. (5)
  3. D3 — Sampling, evidence and decisions, 25 points.

    A fixed population consists of four account adjustments, (0,2,5,9) USD. Select two distinct accounts uniformly without replacement; every unordered pair is equally likely. For parts (a)–(c), randomness comes from the selection design, not from treating these four fixed values as estimated parameters.

    1. List all six possible sample means. Derive the mean and variance of the sample mean by exact enumeration, with units. (5)
    2. Order the two selected accounts randomly and call their values X1,X2. Find each draw's variance and their covariance. Reconstruct the sample-mean variance and compare it with two independent draws with replacement. Explain the dependence mechanism. (5)
    3. A null model specifies exactly the four listed population values and this sampling design. The observed selected pair is (5,9). For the predeclared upper-tail statistic “sample mean,” find the exact p-value Pnull(mean≥observed mean). Would the rule p≤α reject at α=0.20 or α=0.05? Give the actual null rejection probability of the α=0.20 rule, and explain why the p-value is not a posterior probability of the null. (5)
    4. In a separate decision, final wealth is 9 or 25 USD with equal probability. Utility is u(w)=√[w/(1 USD)] on positive wealth. Calculate expected wealth, expected utility, certainty-equivalent wealth and risk premium. Compare the lottery with certain wealth 16 USD under this utility and under risk neutrality. (5)
    5. For another experiment, independent fair signs ε1,ε2 produce cumulative gains M0=0, M1=ε1, M2=ε1+ε2 USD. Stop at T=1 if ε1=1; otherwise stop at T=2. Enumerate the stopped gains and their expectation. Compare with a hindsight report that records max(M1,M2) after both signs are known. Explain the stopping-time distinction and whether bounded optional stopping has been contradicted. (5)

Form B complete solutions and scoring

Each part totals 5 points. Give method credit for a consistently carried arithmetic slip, but withhold interpretation points for a wrong target, information set or unit. Equivalent valid derivations receive credit. The written mastery gate is 85/100 and at least 19/25 in each domain; these are library design choices. Critical errors require remediation even when the numerical total passes.

  1. D1.

    (a) Profit is 160−100−30−20−6=4 USD. The old receivable collection exchanges a claim for cash; its sale was already recognized. Principal repayment reduces cash and a liability rather than consuming a current-period service, while the owner distribution reduces cash and equity after profit. Neither is an expense under the supplied rules. Scoring: 2 complete profit calculation; 1 old receivable; 1 principal; 1 distribution.

    (b) Closing cash is 200+60+80−40−70−30−40−6−14=140. Receivables are 120+100−80=140; inventory is 180+140−100=220; net equipment is 500−20=480. Supplier payables are 100+100−70=130; bank principal is 300−40=260; equity is 600+4−14=590, all USD. Assets total 140+140+220+480=980 USD, matching liabilities 130+260=390 plus equity590. Opening assets1000 similarly equal liabilities400 plus equity600. Scoring: 1 cash; 1 receivables/inventory; 1 equipment; 1 liabilities/equity; 1 closing identity.

    (c) Direct operating cash flow is 60+80−40−70−30−6=−6 USD. Investing flow is zero; financing flow is −40−14=−54 USD. For the independent bridge, receivables rise20, inventory rises40 and payables rise30, so operating cash flow is profit4 + depreciation20 −20 −40 +30=−6. Depreciation is added back because it reduced profit without current cash; an increase in receivables or inventory uses cash relative to recognized earnings; an increase in payables postpones cash payment. Total cash change is −60, agreeing with closing140 minus opening200. Scoring: 1 operating flow; 1 investing/financing; 2 correctly signed bridge; 1 cash reconciliation.

    (d) Forgoing the early payment leaves 900 available now in exchange for owing1000 later: the supplier financing rate is 1000/900−1=1/9≈11.1111% for three months. The bank monthly rate is 24%/12=2%; its three-month effective rate is (1.02)³−1=0.061208=6.1208%. Repayment is 900(1.02)³=955.0872 USD. The effective annual rate is (1.02)12−1≈26.82418%, not24%. Scoring: 1 supplier rate; 1 monthly rate; 1 quarterly rate/repayment; 1 effective annual rate; 1 denominator distinction.

    (e) At the three-month date the equivalent costs are 945 USD for internal cash, because900 invested at5% would become945; 955.0872 USD for the bank; and 1000 USD for supplier financing. Internal cash is cheapest under the stated equal-risk/equal-feasibility assumptions, followed by the bank and supplier. Internal cash has foregone earnings of45 USD even though no lender sends an interest invoice. These are comparable date-three-month resource costs, not three new expenses in the earlier accrual statement. A liquidity restriction, tax, different risk or infeasible return could change the comparison. Scoring: 1 each equivalent cost; 1 ranking; 1 opportunity-cost explanation and scope.

  2. D2.

    (a) The feasible set is a nonempty triangle: it contains(0,0), is closed as an intersection of closed halfspaces, and is bounded by0≤x,y≤4. In finite-dimensional Euclidean space it is compact. The continuous polynomial f therefore attains a minimum. Its Hessian is diag(2,4), and for every nonzero v, vᵀHv=2v₁²+4v₂²>0; f is strictly convex. The feasible set is convex, so two distinct minimizing points would have a feasible midpoint with a strictly smaller value. Thus the minimizer is unique. Scoring: 1 nonempty/closed/bounded set; 1 compactness/continuity attainment; 1 Hessian; 1 positive curvature; 1 uniqueness argument.

    (b) With nonnegativity multipliers α,β≥0, stationarity is 2(x−4)+λ−α=0 and 4(y−3)+λ−β=0. The candidate x=2,y=2,λ=4,α=β=0 satisfies both equations. It is feasible, its resource sum equals4, and λ(x+y−4)=αx=βy=0. The loss is4+2=6 squared activity units. A global certificate follows in part(c), complementing the strict-convexity argument. Scoring: 1 equations/signs; 1 quantities; 1 multipliers; 1 feasibility/complementarity; 1 loss.

    (c) At b=4 and λ=4,

    L(x,y,4)=(x−2)²+2(y−2)²+6.

    The infimum over the retained nonnegative domain is attained at(2,2), so the dual lower bound is d(4)=6. Weak duality makes6 a lower bound on every feasible objective; the candidate attains6, certifying the optimum. At(1,3), f=9, and the primal–dual gap is3 squared activity units. It bounds objective excess f−V(4), here exactly3. A gap in loss units is not automatically a bound of3 activity units on distance or a claim about model realism. Scoring: 1 square completion; 1 domain minimum/dual value; 1 equality certificate; 1 gap; 1 error interpretation.

    (d) With the same λ but a new b, L=(x−2)²+2(y−2)²+22−4b, whose retained-domain minimum is22−4b. Hence V(b)≥22−4b. At b=0 only(0,0) is feasible, with V(0)=34, whereas the bound is22. At b=8 the unrestricted target(4,3) is feasible and yields V(8)=0, whereas the old bound is−10. Nonnegativity of f provides a sharper lower bound there. A valid fixed-multiplier bound can be loose; it does not state V(b)=6−4(b−4). The binding constraints and optimal multipliers can change with capacity. Scoring: 1 new dual expression; 1 bound; 1 zero-capacity optimum/value; 1 large-capacity optimum/value; 1 boundary/sensitivity distinction.

    (e) The equilibrium solves b*=2+b*/2, giving b*=4. Subtracting it gives bt+1−4=(bt−4)/2, hence bt=4(1−2−t) from b₀=0. Nonnegative starting capacity remains nonnegative; this path rises toward4. Absolute deviations shrink by1/2 each update, giving stability and convergence under the fixed rule. The tolerance condition is4·2−t≤1/8, or2t≥32, so the first date ist=5; t=4 still has error1/4. The constant update rule and its parameters must remain applicable; the calculation is not evidence that an actual capacity series obeys them. Scoring: 1 equilibrium; 1 path derivation; 1 stability/domain; 1 first date; 1 model condition.

  3. D3.

    (a) Pairs(0,2),(0,5),(0,9),(2,5),(2,9),(5,9) give means1,5/2,9/2,7/2,11/2,7 USD, each with probability1/6. Their sum is24, so expectation is4 USD, equal to the population mean. Scoring: 2 complete means/equal probabilities; 1 expectation; 1 squared-deviation total; 1 variance/units.

    (b) Each ordered draw is uniform over the four values. Its variance is [(0−4)²+(2−4)²+(5−4)²+(9−4)²]/4=23/2 USD². For two distinct ordered draws,

    E[X₁X₂]=[(0+2+5+9)²−(0²+2²+5²+9²)]/(4·3)=146/12=73/6.

    Thus covariance is73/6−16=−23/6 USD². The average has variance[2(23/2)+2(−23/6)]/4=23/6 USD². With independent replacement draws it would instead be(23/2)/2=23/4 USD². Without replacement, a large first value removes itself from the possibilities for the second, producing negative covariance. Scoring: 1 marginal variance; 1 cross moment/covariance; 1 reconstructed variance; 1 replacement comparison; 1 dependence mechanism.

    (c) The observed mean is7, the largest of the six possibilities. Its upper-tail null probability is1/6≈0.166667. Reject at0.20, but not at0.05. Discreteness makes the test conservative relative to0.20. This p-value is a tail probability under the fixed null sampling law, not the chance the null is true after observing the data. Such a posterior would require additional model probabilities and a Bayesian specification. Scoring: 1 tail calculation; 1 decision at0.20; 1 decision at0.05; 1 actual size; 1 interpretation.
