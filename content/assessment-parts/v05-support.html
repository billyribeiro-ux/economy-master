Volume 05 · Microeconomics and strategic interaction

Assessment, market-intervention project and reference guide

Derive individual choices, solve the interaction they create, and defend the welfare and evidence claims that follow.

This package joins the twelve chapters through two original examinations, a reproducible market-intervention project, an oral defense and delayed transfer. An equilibrium calculation must state who chooses, what each person knows, what is feasible, and what happens after a deviation. A welfare conclusion additionally needs a criterion, a resource ledger and the relevant external effects. These are this library's assessment standards, not institutional examinations or qualifications.

Prerequisite diagnostic

Before starting the volume, allow 40 minutes with closed notes and a basic calculator. These eight questions use tools taught in Volumes 1–4; prior knowledge of the microeconomic models is unnecessary. Each question is four points: two for the calculation and two for the argument, units or interpretation. Aim for 28/32 and repair every failed distinction before proceeding. The numbers are synthetic.

  1. A charge per item rises from 8 USD to 10 USD. Find the proportional increase. A separate participation rate rises from 40% to 50%. State both its percentage-point change and its proportional increase.
  2. Maximize f(q) = 10q − q² on 0 ≤ q ≤ 3. Find the unconstrained stationary point, the feasible maximizer and its value. Explain why solving f′(q) = 0 alone does not answer the constrained question.
  3. Compute the area under g(x) = 6 − 2x between x = 0 and x = 2 by integration and by a trapezoid calculation. If x is items and g is USD per item, state the area's unit.
  4. Solve x + 2y = 10 and 3x + y = 15. Check both equations. Does this point satisfy the additional restrictions x ≥ 0, y ≥ 0 and x ≤ 3?
  5. A type H has prior probability 1/3. A positive signal has probability 3/4 conditional on H and 1/4 conditional on the other type. Find the positive-signal probability and the probability of H after a positive signal.
  6. An event has probability 1/4. Action A loses 8 USD if it occurs and zero otherwise. Action B loses a certain 3 USD. Compare expected losses. If a decision maker chooses B, does this alone prove an arithmetic mistake?
  7. A certain 121 USD arrives two years from today; the effective annual discount rate is 10%. Find its present value. Separately compare 10 USD today with 12 USD in one year at that rate. State the timing convention.
  8. Exactly two of four people receive an intervention by a uniform lottery. Under the sharp null that it changes no person's outcome, fixed outcomes are (1, 1, 0, 0). The two people with outcome 1 happened to receive treatment. Enumerate the six possible treated pairs, compute the treated-minus-control means, and find the two-sided randomization p-value using absolute differences at least as large as observed.
Diagnostic answers and repair routes
  1. The charge increase is (10 − 8)/8 = 1/4 = 25%. The participation-rate change is 10 percentage points and its proportional increase is (50 − 40)/40 = 25%. A difference of rates and a ratio to the original rate answer different questions. Review rates and units.
  2. f′ = 10 − 2q vanishes at 5, outside the interval. On [0, 3] the derivative is at least 4, so the function strictly increases and reaches 21 at q = 3. Alternatively, f = 25 − (q − 5)² makes the nearest feasible point evident. A boundary optimum need not have zero derivative. Review constrained optimization.
  3. The integral is [6x − x²] from 0 to 2, or 8 USD. The trapezoid has width 2 and endpoint heights 6 and 2, giving 2(6 + 2)/2 = 8 USD. Marginal USD per item multiplied by items gives USD. Review integration.
  4. The unique solution is x = 4, y = 3: 4 + 6 = 10 and 12 + 3 = 15. It violates x ≤ 3. Correctly solving equations does not establish feasibility under additional restrictions. Review linear systems and feasibility.
  5. The positive probability is (1/3)(3/4) + (2/3)(1/4) = 5/12. The joint H-and-positive probability is 1/4, so the posterior is (1/4)/(5/12) = 3/5. A signal likelihood is not its posterior. Review Bayes' rule.
  6. Expected monetary losses are 2 USD for A and 3 USD for B. A minimizes expected monetary loss. Choosing B can instead reflect dislike of loss uncertainty under a nonlinear utility criterion or other consequences omitted from the stated monetary loss. Without the decision maker's objective, the choice alone does not establish a calculation error. Review decision criteria and risk preferences.
  7. The two-year value is 121/(1.1)² = 100 USD today. The one-year payment has present value 12/1.1 = 120/11 ≈ 10.9091 USD, exceeding 10 today. Payments occur at the stated dates, discounting compounds once per year, and the comparison assumes these certain cash amounts can be valued at that common rate. Review time value.
  8. Label people 1–4 in the displayed order. Pairs 12, 13, 14, 23, 24, 34 produce differences 1, 0, 0, 0, 0, −1. Each pair has probability 1/6, so the absolute-tail p-value is 2/6 = 1/3, including equality. It is a probability over assignments under the stated sharp null, not the probability that the null is true. Review testing and identification.

Examination rules and essential domains

Each examination is 100 points and 180 minutes, closed notes and closed solutions, with a basic nonprogrammable calculator. Do not run code. Exact fractions and unevaluated logarithms are acceptable. Each domain has four five-point parts. Preserve Form A answers before opening its solutions; attempt Form B only after remediation, without rehearsing its solutions. The project is separately open-resource and executable.

Advance after at least 85/100 overall and 15/20 in every domain, a satisfactory project and oral transfer. These thresholds are this library's design choices. Award consistent arithmetic carry-through when a single slip propagates; do not award reasoning credit for a result with the wrong objective, information set or units. Equivalent methods and alternative defensible open answers receive credit under each rubric. A material infeasibility, omitted profitable deviation, transfer counted as a new resource, false causal claim or fabricated evidence requires correction regardless of the score. Report model implications as conditional on the model.

Oral defense and transfer

After submitting the project, give a 15-minute explanation without reading the benchmark, followed by questions. A reviewer chooses a permitted parameter change before the learner predicts and computes its consequences. For each of the following three tasks award up to four points: one for the target and assumptions, one for a correct argument, one for a feasible alternative or boundary, and one for a clear explanation with a check. Require at least 3/4 on each task and repair every critical error. A self-recorded explanation with comparison to the guide supports practice; it is not independent certification.

  1. Explain the marginal wedge. Starting with the project's demand, cost and damage functions, explain why a welfare-efficient quantity and an unregulated strategic quantity can differ. Derive the tax that implements the target when feasible; distinguish it from the marginal-damage number alone.
  2. Defend an instrument. Change one supplied damage, capacity or administration parameter. Explain whether the original quantity remains attainable, identify an active bound, and compare the best permitted policy with doing nothing. Reconcile private payments with real resources.
  3. Defend an empirical claim. Separate a simulated comparative static from an identified effect in an actual market. Propose a target population, outcome, assignment or identification condition, spillover treatment, and a way to measure or bound the relevant resource and distributional effects.
Oral evaluation guide
  1. A satisfactory derivation begins with buyer marginal value, each firm's marginal cost and total marginal damage. A Cournot firm's perceived marginal revenue also contains its own price effect. For an interior target the implementing tax offsets external damage net of that strategic output restriction; the feasible tax interval and firms' capacities must then be checked. In the supplied baseline the target is total output 32, the Cournot implementing tax is 16 and marginal damage is 32. A statement that every externality mechanically calls for a tax equal to marginal damage omits the other distortion. Full credit requires the actual first-order and boundary arguments, not these memorized numbers.
  2. A satisfactory answer uses the changed input and recomputes the attainable equilibrium, rather than treating a first-order candidate as automatically feasible. A binding tax ceiling, firm capacity or zero-output condition changes the response. Fixed administration costs can make no intervention preferable even when a positive policy improves gross surplus. Tax receipts redistribute money within the specified welfare population; administration, production and damage consume resources or reduce welfare. If a different population or distributional weighting is chosen, state it explicitly and recompute the criterion.
  3. A satisfactory proposal states the causal contrast and unit of assignment, explains why comparison groups identify it, and accounts for interference between buyers, firms or neighboring markets. It distinguishes demand or cost parameters from an intervention's reduced-form effect and does not infer total welfare from price alone. Credible randomization, a defensible quasi-experiment or a transparently assumed structural design can earn credit; an unqualified before/after comparison cannot. Include prespecified outcomes, information dates, attrition/noncompliance handling where relevant and a reproducible audit trail.

Delayed retention and transfer

Seven to fourteen days later, allow 75 minutes with closed notes and a calculator. These six original tasks are ten points each. Aim for 51/60 and at least 7.5/10 on every task, using half points where justified. All amounts are synthetic, and all monetary units are the same numeraire USD. State boundary conditions and explain a changed mechanism before computing. Each numbered allocation below sums to ten points.

  1. Complementary choice. A consumer has utility min{x, 2y}, nonnegative goods, income 18, and initial prices (1, 2). Only the x price rises, to 2. Derive both Marshallian bundles and utility levels (4). Find the least expenditure at new prices needed to retain the old utility and the compensation required (3). Find equivalent variation, defined here as the amount taken away at old prices that reproduces the new utility (3).
  2. A tax is not automatically corrective. Competitive inverse demand is 40 − Q and marginal private cost is 4 + Q, for the nonnegative output range used here. Gross benefit and production cost are zero at Q = 0, and there are no other costs. Total external damage is 2Q. A tax of 6 USD per unit is remitted by sellers; its receipts are returned without real administration costs within the same welfare population. Find untaxed and taxed quantities and buyer/seller prices (3). At the taxed outcome compute consumer surplus, producer surplus, tax revenue and damage, and reconcile total welfare (4). Find efficient output and compare its welfare with both outcomes (3).
  3. Inactive rivals. Two quantity-setting firms face P = max{44 − q₁ − q₂, 0}, constant marginal costs 8 and 20, and no fixed costs or capacity limits. Give best responses and the equilibrium quantities, price and profits (5). Raise firm 2's marginal cost to 28. Find the new equilibrium and verify both firms' global choices, including why a negative interior quantity cannot be retained (5).
  4. The omitted action. A risk-neutral worker has actions L, M, H with success probabilities 2/5, 7/10, 4/5 and effort costs 0, 2, 6. Outside utility is 1. Failure and success wages must be nonnegative; output alone is contractible. An intended action is selected at incentive ties, and participation occurs at equality. A proposed wage pair (0, 20) claims to implement H. Check all three utilities (2). Find the minimum expected wage implementing H, proving its optimality under all action and participation constraints (3). Success revenue is 60 and failure revenue zero: compare the principal's best contracts for L, M and H (3). State which action the principal chooses and whether optimizing wages for H alone solved that choice (2).
  5. Two allocation rules. In a sealed-bid second-price auction for one object, the reserve is 25; bids below reserve cannot win, and the highest eligible bidder pays the greater of the reserve and the second-highest bid. Values are private, utilities are value minus payment if winning and zero otherwise, bidders are risk-neutral, and ties use a fixed rule. Values are (30, 20, 12). State the truthful outcome and prove that truthful bidding is a weakly dominant choice by comparing a bidder's value with the threshold imposed by others (5). Separately, applicants A and B both rank X above Y; X ranks B above A and Y ranks A above B. All pairs are acceptable and each institution has one place. Run applicant-proposing deferred acceptance and check every unmatched pair for blocking (5).
  6. Small experiments. Exactly two of five people are assigned treatment uniformly. Observed binary outcomes, ordered with treated people first, are (1, 1, 0, 0, 0). Under the sharp null of no effect on anyone, let k count the two fixed ones placed in the treated pair. Derive the possible treated-minus-control differences and assignment counts for k = 0, 1, 2 (3). Compute the exact two-sided absolute-tail p-value (2). Without that null, give the logical bounds on the finite-population average treatment effect using only the observed outcomes and binary potential outcomes (3). Explain why neither the observed difference nor its randomization test alone proves a psychological mechanism or an optimal welfare intervention (2).
Retention solutions and scoring
  1. To reach utility u, the least-cost quantities are x = u and y = u/2 at positive prices: any excess of one complement alone is useless. Expenditure is (pₓ + pᵧ/2)u. Initially this is 2u, giving u = 9 and (9, 9/2). Afterwards it is 3u, giving u = 6 and (6, 3) (4: two for each bundle and level with the complementary-input argument). Maintaining old utility costs 3(9) = 27, so compensating variation is 27 − 18 = 9 (3). Old-price expenditure for new utility is 2(6) = 12, so the stated equivalent loss is 18 − 12 = 6 (3). Both measures are positive losses here; their amounts differ because they evaluate different utility targets at different prices.
  2. Without tax, 40 − Q = 4 + Q gives Q = 18 and a common price 22. With tax, 40 − Q = 4 + Q + 6 gives Q = 15, buyer price 25 and seller price 19 (3). Consumer surplus is 15²/2 = 225/2; producer surplus is also 225/2 because the variable-cost supply starts at 4. Revenue is 90 and damage is 30. Welfare is 225/2 + 225/2 + 90 − 30 = 285 (4: one each for the two surpluses together, receipts, damage and reconciliation). Independently, benefit less cost and damage is W(Q) = 34Q − Q², maximized at Q = 17 with W = 289. Untaxed welfare is W(18) = 288. The excessive tax yields 285, below both values (3). Tax revenue is a transfer in this specified population; counting it as a net new benefit after already using willingness to pay minus resource cost would count it twice.
  3. For positive costs, outputs that force zero price are dominated by producing zero. On the positive-price branch the best response is max{(44 − cᵢ − qⱼ)/2, 0}; concavity and the zero-price comparison make it global. Solving the two active responses gives (16, 4), price 24 and profits (256, 16) (5: two for responses/justification, two for quantities/price, one for profits). With c₂ = 28, the putative interior solution has q₂ = (44 − 56 + 8)/3 = −4/3 and is infeasible. At q₂ = 0, firm 1 chooses 18; given 18, firm 2's unconstrained response is (44 − 28 − 18)/2 = −1, so zero is optimal. Price is 26 and profits are (324, 0) (5: one for rejecting the interior candidate, two for both best responses, one for price/profits, one for globality). Setting q₂ to zero while keeping the old interior expression for q₁ does not solve mutual best responses.
  4. Utilities under (0, 20) are 8, 12 and 10 for L, M and H, so M is chosen (2). Write Δ = w₁ − w₀. Implementing H requires Δ ≥ 15 against L and Δ ≥ 40 against M. Expected H pay is w₀ + (4/5)Δ ≥ 32 because w₀ ≥ 0. The pair (0, 40) attains this bound, gives H utility 26 ≥ 1, ties M at 26 and exceeds L's 16; the stated tie rule implements H (3). L can be implemented by constant wages (1, 1), expected pay 1 and profit 24 − 1 = 23; participation gives the cost lower bound. M requires 20/3 ≤ Δ ≤ 40, hence expected pay w₀ + (7/10)Δ ≥ 14/3. Pair (0, 20/3) attains this, gives M and L utility 8/3, exceeds H's −2/3 and satisfies participation. M profit is 42 − 14/3 = 112/3. H profit is 48 − 32 = 16 (3: one for each implementable minimum-cost comparison). The principal chooses M, since 112/3 exceeds 23 and 16 (2 with the distinction between minimizing cost conditional on H and choosing the best induced action). A different tie rule may require a small bonus perturbation and changes whether the cost infimum is attained.
  5. The value-30 bidder wins and pays 25; its utility is 5 (2). For any bidder the opponents determine a threshold equal to the greater of reserve and their highest bid. If value exceeds it, winning is better; if below, losing is better; if equal, the bidder is indifferent. Truthful bidding makes the appropriate choice, subject to a payoff-irrelevant tie at equality. Own bid does not change the payment conditional on winning. Thus truth is weakly dominant; it need not be the only optimal bid in a particular profile (3). In matching, A and B apply to X, which holds B and rejects A. A next applies to Y; the final matching is A–Y, B–X (3). Unmatched A–X does not block because X prefers B; B–Y does not block because B prefers X. All assigned pairs are acceptable (2). Stability here is a property of preferences on both sides, unlike willingness to pay in the auction.
  6. There are ten equally likely treated pairs. For k = 0, 1, 2 the treated mean is k/2 and control mean is (2 − k)/3, so the difference is (5k − 4)/6: −2/3, 1/6 and 1. Counts are 3, 6 and 1, respectively (3). Only the last absolute difference reaches the observed 1, giving p = 1/10, including equality (2). Without the sharp null, each treated person's observed Y(1) = 1 implies an individual effect in [0, 1]; each control person's observed Y(0) = 0 also implies an effect in [0, 1]. All missing outcomes can simultaneously take either endpoint, so the sharp logical ATE bounds are [0, 1] (3: two for the missing-potential-outcome argument, one for attainability). They are not a sampling confidence interval. A treatment bundle can affect behavior through several channels; assignment identifies a specified contrast under its design, not a unique mechanism. Welfare additionally needs benefits, costs, spillovers and a criterion, and transport requires further assumptions (2).

Twelve-chapter capability index

  1. Preferences and consumer choice: derive demand from preferences and feasible budgets, including corners and revealed-choice checks.
  2. Demand, duality and welfare: hold the correct utility or income concept fixed and distinguish compensation from an observed price response.
  3. Production, costs and firm choice: move from technology to conditional costs and then to a global operating decision.
  4. Competitive equilibrium and surplus: solve clearing, incidence, entry and surplus under explicit market assumptions.
  5. General equilibrium and welfare: reconcile individual budgets, profit ownership, market clearing and the hypotheses behind welfare conclusions.
  6. Monopoly, oligopoly and pricing: derive the relevant strategic marginal revenue and check rival activity and market segmentation.
  7. Game theory and strategic behavior: distinguish domination, mutual best response, mixed support and sequential credibility.
  8. Information, signaling and selection: solve participation, updating and deviations under an explicit information structure.
  9. Contracts, incentives and organizations: implement and compare actions using all participation, incentive and liability constraints.
  10. Externalities and public goods: compare feasible corrective institutions with resource, financing and information constraints.
  11. Auctions, matching and market design: test incentive and stability claims under the allocation rule's actual assumptions.
  12. Behavioral economics and microeconomic evidence: separate preference models, identified effects, welfare criteria and evidence limits.

From individual incentives to aggregate outcomes

The common method is to specify feasible choices and information, derive individual optimality, solve the resulting interaction, and evaluate the allocation under a declared criterion. Changing any one of those layers can change the conclusion. A competitive price, a Cournot quantity, a credible promise, an incentive-compatible contract and a stable match each solve a different interaction problem. Their labels cannot substitute for the defining conditions.

Efficiency and evidence travel together but answer different questions. A model can establish a conditional welfare ranking without showing that its parameters describe an actual market. An experiment can establish a treatment contrast under its assignment design without measuring all relevant costs or identifying a unique mechanism. The project requires both a defensible model calculation and a precise statement of what empirical work would be needed next.
