Form B — Change the allocation mechanism

100 points; 180 minutes; closed notes and closed solutions; basic nonprogrammable calculator. There are five essential domains, each worth 20 points, with four parts worth 5 points each. Allow about 34 minutes per domain and 10 minutes to check your work. Show the target, reasoning and units. Exact fractions, radicals and logarithmic expressions are acceptable. No computer execution, external lookup or advanced theorem proof is required. Every economy, payoff and experimental record below is an original synthetic assessment input.

Unless stated otherwise, quantities and dollar amounts refer to one period, all choices are feasible with sufficient wealth, and parties maximize the stated objectives. Do not add an unstated tax, externality or administrative expense. Credit valid alternative arguments and correct subsequent reasoning using an earlier arithmetic error, while marking that error once. Ignoring a feasible deviation, counting a transfer as a net resource gain, violating a budget/participation constraint, or turning an assigned behavior contrast into an unsupported welfare conclusion is a critical conceptual error requiring remediation under the volume's advancement protocol.

  4. D4 — An unused signal and a costly audit, 20 points.

Form B complete solutions and scoring

Each part totals 5 points. Equivalent exact methods receive full credit; accepted alternative fee pairs and critique examples are described below.   1. D1.

    (a) Initially a dollar buys 3/2 utility units through x and 1 through y. Spending all 12 on x gives (x,y) = (6,0), U = 18. At new prices the ratios are 3/4 and 1, so choose (0,12), U = 12. For every initially feasible bundle, U = 3x+y ≤ (3/2)(2x+y) ≤ 18, with equality only when y = 0 and the budget binds. At new prices, U ≤ 4x+y ≤ 12, with equality only when x = 0 and the budget binds. These inequalities establish global corner optima. The old (6,0) now costs 24 > 12 and is infeasible. Scoring: 1 each bundle/utility pair; 2 global comparisons including corners; 1 old-bundle feasibility.

    (b) One utility unit costs px/3 through x or py through y. Thus e(p,u) = u min{px/3,py}. At strict inequality the cheaper good alone reaches the target. At px = 3py, every nonnegative bundle satisfying 3x+y = u minimizes cost; for u = 0 this is only the origin. New-price expenditure for initial utility 18 is 18, attained at (0,18), so CV = 18−12 = 6 dollars. Old-price expenditure for final utility 12 is 8, attained at (4,0), so EV = 12−8 = 4 dollars. The targets differ; the two positive numbers measure the stated welfare loss using different reference prices. Scoring: 1 expenditure derivation; 1 full tie set; 1 each variation; 1 matching target bundles and interpretation.

    (c) At w = 6,r = 4, cost is 6L+4K = 3(2L+K)+K ≥ 30. Equality requires K = 0 and 2L+K = 10, so the unique pair is (L,K) = (5,0) and cost is 30 dollars. At w = 8, cost is 4(2L+K), so the complete minimizing set is {(L,K) ≥ 0: 2L+K = 10}, all costing 40 dollars. It includes both corners and every combination between them. Positive prices exclude excess output/input expenditure. There is no unique interior tangency solution at the tie, and the first case has a corner. Scoring: 1 initial pair; 1 global cost bound; 1 tie set; 1 tie cost; 1 tangency limitation.

    (d) Conditional variable cost is 3q. Shutdown profit is −7. Operating profit is (p−3)q−19 for 0 < q ≤ 10, increasing in q at all three prices. The only positive-output candidate is therefore q = 10. At p = 4 it gives 40−30−12−7 = −9, below −7: choose q = 0, profit −7. At p = 5 it gives 50−30−12−7 = 1: choose q = 10, profit 1. At p = 21/5 the operating candidate gives −7 and ties shutdown, so Q(21/5) = {0,10}. Every strictly intermediate positive quantity earns less; the tie is not the whole interval. Scoring: 1 global positive-output comparison; 1 each output/profit pair; 1 both tie outputs; 1 exclusion of intermediate quantities and cancellation of the unavoidable bill.

    (a) At p = 19/2, buyers B1–B3 buy and sellers S1–S3 sell: Q = 3. Gross value is 18+14+10 = 42 and real cost is 2+5+9 = 16. Scoring: 1 participants/clearing; 1 each CS and PS; 1 total; 1 fourth-unit/global gains argument.

    (b) At 7, S1 and S2 supply two units; B1,B2,B3 would like to buy. The specified allocation serves B2 and B3, with value 24 and resource cost 7. Buyers pay 14, so CS = 10, PS = 7, total surplus = 17. Serving B1 and B2 instead gives value 32 and the same cost, hence surplus 25. Scoring: 1 supply/demand and implemented quantity; 1 CS/PS; 1 actual total; 1 best fixed-quantity total; 1 loss decomposition.

    (c) Completing the square gives firm profit pq−q²/2 = p²/2−(q−p)²/2, so qs = p, π = p²/2. Household income is m = 20+p²/2. With z positive, substituting its budget into utility gives derivative 12/(1+q)−p, so demand is 12/p−1 when positive and affordable. Market clearing requires p = 12/p−1, or (p−3)(p+4) = 0. The positive solution is p = 3, q = 3, π = 9/2 and m = 49/2. Output spending is 9, leaving z = 31/2 = 15.5, so the assumed numeraire interior is valid. The budget binds: 31/2+9 = 49/2. Numeraire resources also clear: household consumption 31/2 plus production input 9/2 equals endowment 20. The concave household objective and the firm's square verify individual optimality. Scoring: 1 firm supply/profit derivation; 1 household demand/clearing equation; 1 price/output; 1 profit/income/consumption; 1 budgets, resources and feasibility.

    (d) At the same p and q, income incorrectly fixed at 20 gives z = 20−9 = 11. Production still uses 9/2, so only 31/2 of the 20 numeraire units are allocated, leaving 9/2 unaccounted for. This is precisely the firm's profit, which belongs to the household under the specified ownership rule.

    With all resources assigned and no disposal of valuable numeraire, feasible utility is F(q) = 20−q²/2+12 ln(1+q), on 0 ≤ q ≤ √40. Its derivative is −q+12/(1+q), zero at 3, and F″ = −1−12/(1+q)² < 0. The interior feasible q = 3 is therefore the unique global maximizer, with z = 31/2. There is one household and the firm is its owned technology, with no unrepresented affected party: no feasible allocation can make that household better off. This is the Pareto claim here; it would require reconsideration if another party bore an omitted effect.     (b) Initially M and N propose to R, which holds M and rejects N; O proposes to S and is held. N next proposes to S, which replaces O by N. O then proposes to R, which replaces M by O.     (c) The observed contrast is 3/3−0/4 = 1, or 100 percentage points. Under the sharp null, K treated enrollees leave 3−K control enrollees, so the statistic is T(K) = K/3−(3−K)/4 = (7K−9)/12. At K = 0,1,2,3 the values are −3/4, −1/6, 5/12, 1, with the supplied counts 4,18,12,1. Only the last has |T| ≥ 1; therefore the specified exact two-sided p-value is 1/35 ≈ 0.02857. Reject the sharp null of zero effect for every volunteer at 0.05. This is not a posterior probability that that null is true, nor a test obtained merely by asserting that the average effect equals zero. Unequal arm sizes make this null statistic distribution asymmetric: there is no negative realization with absolute magnitude at least one, so doubling the positive-tail count would implement a different rule. Scoring: 1 observed contrast/units; 1 statistic formula and four values; 1 inclusive p-value; 1 decision/precise null; 1 asymmetric-tail explanation.

    (d) Under the stipulated dollar criterion, each extra enrollment changes surplus by 7−9 = −2. Using the observed rate contrast 1 as a plug-in effect and comparing assignment to the default with assignment to active choice gives (7−9)×1−1/2 = −5/2 dollars per person. The supplied criterion and parameters therefore give a negative plug-in welfare change, despite a positive enrollment contrast. The fixed default expense is charged per person assigned the default, not once per additional enrollee.

    Random assignment identifies an assignment-effect comparison under its design assumptions; it does not isolate inattention from changed action costs, perceived endorsement or other responses to the interface. One such alternative with an explanation earns credit. Volunteers are not a random sample of every population, so a universal claim needs a transport argument and relevant additional evidence. Finally, one randomization realization does not reveal all missing potential outcomes or the true average effect. The plug-in number also relies on stipulated benefit/cost values and the chosen welfare criterion; the enrollment test does not validate those values or prove the welfare effect equals −5/2. Scoring: 1 correct criterion/benefit-minus-cost margin; 1 per-person plug-in calculation; 1 mechanism alternative/identification limit; 1 population limit; 1 uncertainty and welfare-premise distinction.
