Volume 04 · Data, computation and empirical reasoning

Assessment, empirical project and reference guide

Turn a stated economic question into a traceable calculation, test the calculation, and defend what its evidence permits you to conclude.

This package assesses the twelve chapters through two distinct examinations, an open-resource empirical project, oral transfer and delayed retrieval. All assessment questions are original. A successful calculation includes its target, information set, units and assumptions. These are this library's assessment instruments and standards, not university examinations or institutional qualifications.

Prerequisite diagnostic

Before beginning this volume, allow 35 minutes with closed notes and a basic calculator. Programming is not a prerequisite: the volume teaches it. The following eight questions test the mathematics and probability already developed in Volumes 1–3. Each is four points: two for the result and two for its reasoning, definitions or units. Aim for 28/32; repair every failed domain distinction before proceeding.

  1. A rate rises from 2% to 3%. Give its change in percentage points and its relative percentage increase. State each denominator.
  2. Discount a certain 105 USD payment one year from today at an effective annual rate of 5%. Separately, an account grows by 21% over a year through two equal half-year compound rates. Find each half-year rate.
  3. A population has two groups with proportions 3/4 and 1/4 and fixed group means 4 and 10 USD. Find its mean. A sample contains the groups in proportions 9/10 and 1/10, with the same group means. Find the unweighted sample mean and explain why it targets a different mixture.
  4. An event has prior probability 1/5. A positive signal has probability 4/5 given the event and 1/10 without it. Find the positive-signal probability and the event probability conditional on that signal.
  5. Let Xi = μ + C + εi, where C has mean zero and variance 9, the εi are iid with mean zero and variance 4, and C is independent of them. Find the mean and variance of the average of 25 observations. Does increasing the number of ε terms eliminate the shared uncertainty?
  6. In an iid normal model with unknown mean, known standard deviation 3 USD and n = 9, the observed mean is 5 USD. Form a 95% interval using 1.96. Explain the procedure's coverage statement and distinguish it from an interval for a new observation.
  7. Decompose the vector (2, 4) as a multiple of (1, 1) plus a vector orthogonal to (1, 1). Give both pieces and check their inner product.
  8. An event has probability 0.3. Acting costs 2 USD if the event is absent and zero if present; not acting costs 8 USD if present and zero if absent. Compute both expected losses and choose an action. Must the decision threshold be 0.5?
Diagnostic solutions and repairs
  1. The change is 3 − 2 = 1 percentage point. Relative growth is (3 − 2)/2 = 1/2 = 50%, using the old rate as denominator. A percentage-point difference subtracts two rates measured on the same 100-per-unit scale; it does not divide by the old rate. Review rates, growth and units if the distinction is unclear.
  2. Present value is 105/1.05 = 100 USD. The half-year rate solves (1 + r)² = 1.21 with a positive gross accumulation factor, giving r = 0.10. Dividing the annual rate by two would give an incompatible compounding rule. Review the time-value and function material in Volumes 1–2.
  3. The population mean is (3/4)4 + (1/4)10 = 11/2 = 5.5 USD. The sample mean is (9/10)4 + (1/10)10 = 23/5 = 4.6 USD. Different group weights, despite identical within-group means, produce the difference. Reweighting needs the correct target proportions and a justification for using those group means. Review expectation and sampling designs.
  4. Positive probability is (1/5)(4/5) + (4/5)(1/10) = 6/25. Bayes gives (4/25)/(6/25) = 2/3. The likelihood 4/5 is not itself the posterior; the denominator includes positives without the event. Review conditional probability and Bayes.
  5. The average has mean μ and variance 9 + 4/25 = 229/25 = 9.16 in squared outcome units. Covariances from C survive averaging. Increasing the number of observations reduces the ε contribution, while the limiting shared variance remains 9. Review covariance and shared-shock limits.
  6. The standard error is 3/√9 = 1 USD, yielding [3.04, 6.96] USD. Across repeated samples from this model, approximately 95% of intervals constructed by this rule cover the fixed mean; using the supplied 1.96 is a rounded normal critical value. The interval concerns the mean, not the range containing 95% of individual outcomes. A new observation also contains its own outcome variation. Review confidence procedures.
  7. The component along (1, 1) is 3(1, 1) = (3, 3), leaving (−1, 1). Their inner product is 3(−1) + 3(1) = 0. The projection coefficient is [(2, 4)·(1, 1)]/[(1, 1)·(1, 1)] = 6/2. Review matrix geometry and projection.
  8. Acting loses 2(0.7) = 1.4 USD on average; not acting loses 8(0.3) = 2.4 USD. Act. More generally, act when 2(1 − p) < 8p, or p > 1/5, with indifference at equality. The consequences determine the threshold. Review decision losses and forecasting and scoring.

Examination rules and domain scoring

Each form is 100 points and 180 minutes, closed notes and closed solutions, with a basic nonprogrammable calculator. Do not run code during the examination. You may give exact fractions, unevaluated logarithms or square roots when appropriate; show the steps that identify the quantity. Project work below is separately open-resource and executable. Attempt Form A first and preserve your answers before reading its solutions. Use Form B after remediation as a distinct reassessment, without rehearsing its answers.

Each question has four five-point parts. Award reasoning and interpretation points only when the claimed result follows from the supplied information. Apply reasonable arithmetic carry-through: do not punish one arithmetic slip repeatedly when subsequent reasoning consistently uses it. Do not award interpretation credit for a numerically correct answer with the wrong target or units. Equivalent algorithms, algebra and defensible explanations receive credit under the stated criteria.

Advance after at least 85/100 overall and 15/20 in every domain, then satisfactory project and oral transfer. These thresholds are this library's design choices. A material look-ahead error, fabricated or silently changed source value, false causal claim, confused unit/denominator or unacknowledged missing population requires repair regardless of the total. An executable report and an examination measure different capabilities; success in one does not waive the other.

Oral transfer and review protocol

After submitting the project, ask a reviewer to choose one permitted input change and one invalid input. Predict the consequences before rerunning the code, then explain the observed results. An appropriate reviewer checks the explanation against the specified target, rather than rewarding a fluent narrative alone. If studying independently, record the explanation and compare it with the reference calculations; this is practice, not independent certification.

  1. Reconstruction: identify a single output's source row, release date, unit and transformation. Recompute it through a different arithmetic route. Explain what a matching file hash establishes and what it cannot establish.
  2. Transfer: change a forecast horizon, delay a label's release, or change an aggregation denominator. Identify exactly which calculations may change and which must remain unchanged. A changed test winner cannot retrospectively become the originally selected winner.
  3. Inference: separate a descriptive coefficient, Monte Carlo error, input-rounding sensitivity, sampling uncertainty and a causal effect. State which are actually supported by the report and what additional design or data would be needed for the others.

All three explanations must be coherent and free of the critical errors above. If a reviewer cannot reproduce a central result from the supplied files and instructions, the project is incomplete even when the printed benchmark number matches. Preserve both the failed attempt and the corrected version with a short explanation of the change.

Delayed retention and transfer

After seven to fourteen days, allow 60 minutes with closed notes and a calculator. These six tasks are ten points each. Aim for 51/60 and at least 7.5/10 on every task. Use half points when justified by the scoring criteria; repeat missed skills with changed inputs after remediation. All numbers below are original synthetic teaching values.

  1. Information time and provenance. A February estimate is first released on 3 March as 100 and revised on 12 March to 110. A forecast is issued on 10 March. Which value may enter that forecast (3)? Describe the record fields needed to retain both versions and reproduce the forecast later (4). Explain why changing both a local file and its stored hash does not establish that the new value is authentic (3).
  2. Calendar and target. A firm is active throughout January–March but reports nominal sales only in January, 100 USD, and March, 121 USD. Price indexes are 100 and 110. Compute real sales and the observed change (4); state whether March monthly growth is known (3). A separate account has cash stocks of 40 and 50 USD at those two month ends. Explain whether their sum gives any quarter flow or time-average balance (3).
  3. Projection. Fit an intercept and slope to x = (0, 1, 2) and y = (2, 2, 5) by least squares. Give coefficients and fitted values (4), residuals and their two orthogonality checks (3), SSE and centered R² (2). Does the slope identify a causal effect of x (1)?
  4. Replication unit. Four iid complete simulation replications from the same fixed simulator law with finite variance produce paired policy-profit differences (−2, 0, 1, 3) USD. Find the mean, unbiased sample variance and estimated standard error of the mean (6). Explain why reusing common simulated shocks within a pair can help comparison (2), and why treating many dependent periods inside each replication as independent replicates would invalidate the same precision calculation (2).
  5. Frozen and updating forecasts. Monthly outcomes are (5, 7, 6, 10, 11) units. An endpoint-drift rule uses the first and latest available observations, with slope (last − first)/(elapsed months). All outcomes are available at their month ends. From origin 3 forecast months 4 and 5 with the origin held fixed (3); compute the alternative month-5 forecast after the predeclared update at origin 4 (2). Find MSE over targets 4–5 for both protocols (3). Explain why choosing whichever protocol wins after these outcomes are observed changes the evaluation claim (2).
  6. Reconstruction scope. Two fictional published labor-force summaries give unemployed levels 354 then 426 thousand and labor force 10,000 thousand at both dates. Reconstruct their unemployment rates and their nearest-tenth displays (3); compare the difference of displayed rates with the rounded difference of reconstructed ratios (3). Explain why an input-rounding envelope is not a sampling interval, and why the labor-force estimate is not the survey sample size (4).
Retention answers and scoring
  1. Use 100: the revision is not yet released (3). Retain observation period, release timestamp/time zone, value and unit, source identifier/release version and retrieval information; the forecast also records its origin/cutoff and the rule selecting the latest version available then. The observation-period/release combination distinguishes versions instead of treating the newer row as permission to overwrite the old evidence (4 for the time fields, source/unit and reproducible selection). A hash checks bytes against a trusted reference. An altered file and newly computed hash can be mutually consistent while the value has no official source (3).
  2. Real amounts are 100(100)/100 = 100 and 100(121)/110 = 110 base-price USD; the observed two-month change is +10 USD or +10% (4). February is an active but unreported month, so March's monthly difference and growth are unknown; January is the previous observed row, not the previous month (3). The two cash stocks are measurements at instants. Their sum, 90 USD, is neither a quarter flow nor an identified time-average balance. Intermediate balances and cash-flow histories are not supplied (3).
  3. x̄ = 1, ȳ = 3, Sxx = 2 and Sxy = 3, so slope is 3/2 and intercept 3/2. Fitted values are (3/2, 3, 9/2) (4). Residuals are (1/2, −1, 1/2), summing to zero and satisfying Σxiei = 0 − 1 + 1 = 0 (3). SSE = 3/2; SST = 1 + 1 + 4 = 6; R² = 1 − (3/2)/6 = 3/4 (2). This descriptive projection has no supplied counterfactual design or exogeneity condition identifying a causal effect (1).
  4. The mean is 1/2 USD. Centered squares sum to 25/4 + 1/4 + 1/4 + 25/4 = 13 USD². The unbiased sample variance is 13/3 USD²; estimated standard error is √[(13/3)/4] = √(13/12) ≈ 1.040833 USD (6: two each). For a difference A − B, variance is Var(A) + Var(B) − 2Cov(A, B); positive within-pair covariance reduces comparison variance relative to independent same-marginal policies. A shared seed alone does not guarantee the covariance sign (2). The stated iid law and finite variance apply across the four complete replications. Serial observations inside one run do not automatically supply new independent replication units (2). Four runs also provide weak precision evidence; the standard-error estimate is not a guaranteed confidence bound.
  5. At origin 3 the endpoint slope is (6 − 5)/2 = 1/2, so fixed forecasts are 13/2 and 7 (3). At origin 4 the slope becomes (10 − 5)/3 = 5/3, giving the updated month-5 forecast 10 + 5/3 = 35/3 (2). Fixed errors are 7/2 and 4, so MSE = [(49/4) + 16]/2 = 113/8 = 14.125 units². Updating errors are 7/2 and −2/3, giving MSE = [(49/4) + 4/9]/2 = 457/72 ≈ 6.347222 units² (3). Comparing two prespecified protocols is legitimate. Selecting a new winner after seeing their final outcomes consumes that information; the same scores are no longer untouched evaluation of that selection procedure (2).
  6. Rates are 3.54% and 4.26%, displaying as 3.5% and 4.3% (3). Display subtraction gives 0.8 percentage point. Exact ratio subtraction gives 0.72 percentage point, which rounds last to 0.7. The operations differ because rounding is nonlinear; neither recovers undisplayed underlying estimates from rounded source levels (3). An envelope conditional on rounding bounds measures that assumed input perturbation only. Sampling inference needs a sampling design and variance method. Ten million estimated labor-force persons are the estimated population total, not ten million independent survey respondents (4: two for each distinction).

From reliable evidence to economic mechanisms

The central workflow is now complete at an introductory empirical level: define a question and target; inspect provenance and information time; validate records and units; choose a defensible transformation; describe the result; fit or simulate only what the question requires; evaluate the procedure against a suitable reference; and explain its limits. A technically exact answer to a different question remains a wrong answer to the original question.

Three distinctions travel into every later volume. Accounting and data identities constrain what a calculation can mean. Prediction concerns performance under an explicit information and evaluation scheme. Causal inference concerns counterfactual effects under a design or identification assumptions. A better fit, a smaller numerical error and a more plausible causal design solve different problems; no single statistic certifies all three.
