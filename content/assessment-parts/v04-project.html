Integrative project: six months, two archives and three kinds of claim

Task: produce a reproducible economic-data report that reconstructs official historical statistics, summarizes a time pattern, evaluates a fixed forecasting exercise and critiques a causal interpretation. This is open-resource work drawing on this volume's methods. Submit your own analysis and explanation; use the instructor benchmark after freezing your first report. The data are actual published aggregate estimates. The project design, code, questions and grading criteria are original.

Your client asks: “What happened to the U.S. unemployment rate from February through July 2024, does a fitted time trend explain it, and which simple forecast should we trust?” Replace this ambiguous request with three distinct targets: a descriptive reconstruction from archived figures, an intercept OLS projection on month index, and a retrospective prediction exercise. A regression summary cannot identify why unemployment changed, and two test forecasts cannot establish which rule to trust in general.

The 5 July 2024 BLS release, Table A-1 TOTAL, supplies February 2024. Its full header contains three NSA columns followed by SA June 2023 and February–June 2024; February is the fifth numeric column. The release describes June, even though its table also contains earlier months.

The 2 August 2024 BLS release, Table A-1 TOTAL, supplies March–July 2024. Its full header contains three NSA columns followed by SA July 2023 and March–July 2024. It has no February 2024 column. The earlier July 2023 comparison is not February 2024. This project therefore combines two archived releases and retains the source-release field in each row. The overlapping March–June levels and rates agree across the two inspected tables; this observation does not establish their availability or values at every earlier forecast origin.

The six records form a retrospectively assembled series, not a single-release extract or a contemporaneous forecast backtest. Source inspection occurred on 7 October 2026 for the 6 October 2026 edition boundary. Both complete headers and selected TOTAL rows were inspected through successful web retrieval. The local extract is explicitly a manual transcription; it is not original HTML bytes. Commas were removed from counts, one-decimal source rates retained, and no balancing values inserted. Local SHA-256 hashes detect changes against the manifest but do not authenticate BLS origin. Checking JSON against a transcription created from the same inspection catches transformation discrepancies, not every shared transcription mistake.

The numeric extract is attributed to BLS under its public-use statement, already inspected for Chapter 12: its publications are public domain except previously copyrighted photographs and illustrations. This package contains neither images nor the BLS emblem. Access required no account or payment. Original-source reconstruction can be checked online when available; the frozen computation is fully offline.

The population is the U.S. civilian noninstitutional population age 16 and over, covering the 50 states and District of Columbia. BLS CPS definitions distinguish employed, unemployed and outside-the-labor-force persons. The unemployed generally have no work, are available and have searched actively in the preceding four weeks; temporary layoff with expected recall has a search exception. Estimated persons are not payroll jobs or survey respondent counts. The household reference week generally contains the 12th. “Monthly” therefore does not mean a cumulative flow of unemployment through the month.

Fixed pedagogical forecast protocol

The historical results were known when this exercise was designed. This is a transparent teaching specification, not a retrospective claim of preregistration. Within the frozen vector, reveal outcomes chronologically: training February–April, validation May, final test June–July. These are period-index teaching origins. The archives themselves were released after some hypothetical forecast origins, so even correct prefix slicing does not establish a contemporaneous out-of-sample backtest. Such a study would need every relevant first-release/as-of vintage and a genuine information-availability calendar.

For history y1,…,yk, define a naive one-step forecast fN = yk and an endpoint-drift forecast fD = yk + (yk − y1)/(k − 1), for k ≥ 2. Drift extrapolates the average endpoint change; it is not the OLS line. Use squared error (actual − forecast)², measured in percentage points squared. Predict May using only February–April. Select the lower validation loss; an exact tie selects naive. Use exact decimal fractions to apply the tie rule without binary-rounding accidents.

Freeze the selected rule. Predict June using February–May, then reveal June and predict July using February–June. This is two updating one-step forecasts, not two forecasts made at the end of April or May. Keep the two rules' test scores for the predeclared comparison table, but report the selected rule's result as the protocol outcome. A competing rule's lower test loss is a diagnostic, not permission to rewrite the selection. Do not tune endpoints, weights, tolerance or the loss after seeing the test.

Uncertainty, reliability and causal limits

For a reconstructed percent ratio 100A/B with B > 0.5, assume the published numerator and denominator differ from their undisplayed values by at most 0.5 thousand. Monotonicity gives the outer envelope [100(A − 0.5)/(B + 0.5), 100(A + 0.5)/(B − 0.5)], truncated to [0,100] where needed. For a later-minus-earlier change, subtract the earlier upper endpoint from the later lower endpoint, and the earlier lower from the later upper. Related numerator and denominator values may make some corners unattainable; the rectangular calculation is conservative. It requires no assumption of independent random rounding.

The bracket answers sensitivity to displayed-level rounding under a specific assumption. Survey sampling error, nonresponse, measurement error and revision uncertainty are different. Neither the observed sample variance of these six dependent monthly estimates nor an iid binomial formula with the estimated labor force as n supplies the required survey standard error. A proper monthly-change analysis needs the survey estimation/design information and covariance across months. No confidence level attaches to this package's rounding bounds. The OLS residual orthogonality identities similarly supply no sampling standard error or causal identification.

For the causal critique, start by rejecting the claim that b is “the causal effect of a month.” A time index is not a randomized intervention. Many economic developments move together; trends, survey composition and measurement may affect the association. One possible future question concerns the effect of a specifically defined counseling offer to eligible job seekers in June on employment in July's reference week. That changes the unit and outcome from this aggregate regression. A randomized offer and complete follow-up could support an intention-to-treat comparison under consistency and a suitable interference scope. Simply controlling for July job-search effort may condition on a mediator; an observational design would need justified exchangeability and overlap. Other well-specified study proposals are acceptable if their assumptions and data requirements are explicit.

Reliability challenge: use a separate temporary copy. Change June labor force from 168,009 to 168,109 thousand without changing employed or unemployed. Explain why file integrity, cross-copy agreement, reconstructed displays and identities are complementary checks. Also test duplicate April, an invalid unit and a boolean level under normal and optimized Python. Preserve the original package and failing cases. Test a forecast implementation that accidentally uses the target in its history; show that changing July must not alter the selected rule or the June forecast, whereas changing June may legitimately change July's updating forecast.

Conditional uncertainty. Apply the endpoint formula to every ratio; the preserved JSON gives exact fractions for each endpoint. For July unemployment the envelope is approximately [4.252521084, 4.253140056]%. The February-to-July reconstructed change is bounded by [0.394984449, 0.396223738] percentage point under the half-thousand assumptions. A positive lower bound means input display rounding alone cannot reverse this reconstructed change. It does not establish statistical significance of a population change, and it is not a confidence interval. Neither a six-month residual standard deviation nor the estimated labor-force population solves the missing survey-variance problem.

Failure cases. The invented June labor force 168,109 makes L − E − U = +99 thousand and P − L − N = −100 thousand. Its reconstructed unemployment rate is about 4.051538%, still displayed as 4.1%; unemployment-display matching alone misses the corruption. The original manifest flags changed bytes, and the text/JSON comparison flags an altered copy unless both copies were changed. Regenerating both hashes does not authenticate a false input; the arithmetic identities still reveal this case. Duplicate April, wrong units and boolean counts must produce explicit failures in both execution modes. A forecast function using y through the target produces zero errors by construction and fails the stated history contract.

Interpretive memo criteria. A strong report states that the published unemployment rate rose 0.4 percentage point over the endpoints while the intervening March value fell below February. It records all three measure reconstructions, the descriptive slope, the validation-selected naive rule and its 0.025 pp² test MSE, and the smaller unselected drift score without rewriting selection. It states both source releases and the late availability of the archived snapshot, identifies the deterministic rounding limits, and declines sampling-significance, real-time forecasting or causal-effect claims. A future causal proposal is judged on a specific intervention, unit/timing/outcome, credible comparison and the explanation of identification threats; generic “control for everything” language is insufficient.
