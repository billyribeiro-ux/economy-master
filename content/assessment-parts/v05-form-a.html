Form A — Follow choices through institutions

Allow 180 minutes, closed notes and closed solutions, with a basic nonprogrammable calculator. Answer all five 20-point domains; each contains four parts worth 5 points each. A suggested allocation is 32 minutes per domain and 20 minutes for checking. Show the optimization or deviation argument as well as the number. Exact fractions and correctly rounded decimals receive equal credit. All preferences, technologies, populations, games and interventions below are original synthetic cases. No external evidence, code execution or additional theorem is required.

Unless specified otherwise, quantities are nonnegative, prices are dollars per unit, and monetary payoffs are dollars for the one period in question. Parameters carry the units needed by their equations. Distinct submodels within a question are separate economies: do not transfer incomes, endowments, information or welfare assumptions between them.

  1. D1. Reoptimization and the cost of output — 20 points

    A consumer has income m = 18 dollars and preferences represented by U(x,y) = x²y on nonnegative bundles. Initial prices are (px,py) = (1,1). They change to (8,1), holding nominal income fixed. A separate price-taking producer can make q units using labor L ≥ 0 with q ≤ √L. Labor costs 2 dollars per unit and materials cost 3q dollars; there are no other costs, commitments, capacity limits or externalities.

    1. Derive this consumer's Marshallian demands for positive prices and income. Establish global optimality, including the budget boundaries. Compute both chosen bundles and represented utility levels before and after the price change.
    2. At the new prices, find the minimum expenditure and bundle attaining the old utility. Compute the income addition that exactly compensates the price change. Compare it with the additional income required to buy the original bundle; explain the difference.
    3. Derive the producer's conditional labor demand, total cost C(q), marginal cost and average cost for q > 0. Explain why the cost-minimizing input constraint binds and why output cost is strictly convex here.
    4. At output price 11, find globally optimal q and L, revenue, cost and profit. Give a global certificate, rather than only setting price equal to marginal cost. State the optimal output when the output price is between zero and 3, inclusive.

  2. D2. A tax ledger and a complete exchange economy — 20 points

    In a competitive product market, inverse demand is pb = 50 − 2Q and inverse supply is ps = 5 + Q, on 0 ≤ Q ≤ 25. These are marginal willingness to pay and marginal real cost. Preferences are quasilinear, with sufficient numeraire, and there are no externalities, other distortions or collection costs. A specific tax of 9 per unit is fully enforced. The revenue recipient is included in total surplus.

    Separately, two consumers trade two goods x and y in a pure exchange economy. A has UA = xAyA and endowment (6,0); B has UB = xB²yB and endowment (0,6). There is no production, tax, externality or missing commodity in this exchange model. Both consumers take prices as given and may trade fractions. Normalize py = 1 and write px = p > 0.

    1. Find the product market's untaxed quantity and price, then its taxed quantity, buyer price and retained seller price. Allocate the per-unit tax burden between buyers and sellers relative to the untaxed price. Explain what would happen if legal remittance moved from sellers to buyers with all stated trading and enforcement assumptions unchanged.
    2. Compute consumer surplus, producer surplus, public revenue and their sum before and after the tax. Compute the deadweight loss and explain why neither the tax revenue nor the entire loss of consumer surplus is that loss.
    3. For the exchange economy, write each consumer's wealth and demand as functions of p, then solve the competitive relative price and allocation. Check both goods' resource constraints and both individual budgets.
    4. At the trial price p = 2, calculate aggregate excess demands and their value. Explain why Walras' law does not make that price an equilibrium. For the equilibrium in part (c), give the expenditure contradiction that rules out a feasible Pareto improvement and state the relevant preference and market assumptions; do not infer that A and B receive equal utility or equal bundles.

  3. D3. Active firms and credible continuation plans — 20 points

    Two firms choose quantities simultaneously, q₁,q₂ ≥ 0. Price is P(Q) = max{62 − Q,0}, where Q = q₁ + q₂. Costs are c₁q₁ and c₂q₂, with c₁ = 8 and initially c₂ = 20, no fixed costs and no capacity limit. Each firm takes its rival's quantity as fixed when evaluating a deviation.

Out ends the game. Payoffs, already net of all costs, are ordered (E,I); all actions and payoffs are common knowledge. No commitment device removes any action.

    2. Change only c₂ to 40. Re-solve the equilibrium, rejecting any infeasible interior candidate explicitly. Check both best responses and give price and profits. Explain why automatically retaining two positive producers would be an error.

  4. D4. Who trades, and which effort does a wage induce? — 20 points

    A mass one of sellers each owns one asset of privately known type. Low-quality assets have seller reservation value 5 and buyer value 9; high-quality assets have seller reservation value 13 and buyer value 21. Initially the high-quality share is 1/4. At least two risk-neutral buyers can make posted price offers for all assets that accept, with no capacity, inspection or transaction cost. Sellers accept the highest offer if it reaches their reservation value, including equality; ties between buyers split sellers independently of type. A candidate competitive outcome must survive profitable alternative offers, not only break even locally.

    In a separate employment model, a risk-neutral principal offers nonnegative outcome wages (wF,wS) to a risk-neutral worker. The worker accepts or takes outside utility 3 dollars, then privately chooses effort L or H, then verifiable failure or success occurs. Effort costs are 0 and 8 dollars; success probabilities are 1/3 and 2/3. The principal receives revenue 12 on failure and 54 on success, and can instead decline to hire for payoff zero. The schedule is enforceable, no other signal or transfer is available, and all probabilities are known. For weak implementation, assume acceptance and the recommended action at a tie.

    1. At high-quality share 1/4, determine a competitive trading price and participating quality. Verify that no buyer can gain with a higher or lower price offer. Compute realized total gains from trade and the gains under verifiable quality.
    2. Change only the high-quality share to 1/2. Determine a pooling competitive price and verify it. Test whether the former low-only price remains an equilibrium by giving a concrete profitable deviation if one exists. Compute realized total gains at the pooling outcome and distinguish a changed price from a changed composition.
    3. For the employment model, derive IC and IR for H and find a cheapest feasible H wage schedule with a global lower-bound argument. Compute expected wage, both effort utilities and the worker's rent. Identify binding constraints and the implementation tie.
    4. Compare the principal's best H, L and no-hiring profits, first at success revenue 54 and then when only success revenue falls to 48. For the original revenue case, also compute a verifiable-effort H benchmark and explain the profit difference from hidden H. Does that difference compensate the worker for aversion to wage risk?

  5. D5. Correcting, allocating and evaluating — 20 points

    A competitive market has marginal benefit MB = 30 − Q, private marginal cost PMC = 6 + Q and external damage H(Q) = Q²/2, on 0 ≤ Q ≤ 30. Gross benefit and production cost have zero constants. Use unweighted dollar surplus W = B − C − H; preferences are quasilinear with enough wealth, and there are no other distortions. One unit of output causes one measured unit of emission. A tax can be enforced with a fixed real administration cost of 10. Alternatively, a competitive tradable-permit cap can be enforced with fixed real administration cost 4. Permit rights are assigned free, in fixed amounts unrelated to later output, to passive owners outside the producer sector. Any required public funding is a nondistortionary lump-sum transfer; neither fixed administrative cost changes private marginal choices.

    Separately, a sealed-bid second-price auction sells one object, with no reserve, to three private-value bidders with values (47,34,19). Utility is value minus payment for the winner and zero for a loser, bids are nonnegative, no budget binds, and a fixed priority 1 then 2 then 3 resolves ties. The seller's retention value is zero and there are no external costs.

    Finally, a person can redeem a voucher for a utility flow of 10 at date 1 or retain it for a certain flow of 18 at date 2. There is no other consumption, uncertainty, liquidity constraint or new information. At any date t, preferences are Ut = ut + βΣs>tδs−tus, with β = 1/2 and δ = 4/5. At date 0 an understood, irreversible lock can force retention until date 2; it costs one unit of utility immediately through a real service cost. Without a lock, the date-1 self chooses freely. A sophisticated person predicts actual future preferences; a fully naive person predicts that the date-1 self will use β̂ = 1, although actual β remains 1/2.

    1. Find unregulated Q and gross surplus net of damage. Find the globally efficient quantity before administrative cost and a per-unit corrective tax implementing it, with buyer and retained seller prices. Use a global argument for the efficient target.
    2. At that target, compare the tax with an equal-quantity tradable-permit cap. Give tax receipts or permit rents, the resource surplus net of administration, and any public surplus or funding requirement. Which instrument is preferred by the stated criterion, including the no-intervention option? Explain why free permit allocation does not eliminate its opportunity cost or create an extra social benefit equal to the rent.
    3. Compute the truthful second-price allocation, payment, buyer surplus and total gains. Prove that truthful bidding is weakly dominant using an arbitrary highest rival bid h and the three cases h < v, h > v and h = v. Explain with this value profile why truthfulness need not be the only best reply.
    4. For the voucher, calculate the date-0 ranking of redemption versus retention and the actual date-1 choice. Determine whether sophisticated and fully naive people buy the one-unit lock. Evaluate the resulting sophisticated commitment using both date-0 utility and the explicitly stipulated undiscounted-flow criterion W = Σus, including the service cost. Explain why these calculations neither make every temporal self better off nor establish an empirical effect of a real reminder.

Scoring and critical errors. Each part has the five-point allocation below. Award follow-through credit for a clearly stated earlier arithmetic error when the subsequent method is correct; do not award points for an interpretation that contradicts the relevant constraint, information set or units. Essential-domain mastery requires more than a high aggregate score: the volume's common advancement protocol applies. An unresolved critical error prevents advancement until corrected in a transfer check. Here those errors include treating an infeasible or merely stationary choice as a verified optimum; omitting endowment wealth or treating a transfer as a resource gain/loss; accepting a profitable deviation or an incredible off-path plan as equilibrium; claiming IR alone ensures effort, confusing risk compensation with a risk-neutral rent, or overlooking participation selection; and asserting that revenue, a chosen behavior or one temporal self's preference alone establishes welfare or a causal field effect. A computational slip corrected with sound reasoning is not automatically a critical error.

Form A complete solutions and per-part scoring

  1. D1 solutions

    1. For m > 0 and positive prices an interior affordable bundle has positive utility, while either axis gives zero. Utility increases in each good at a positive bundle, so the budget binds. Substitute y = (m − pxx)/py, with 0 ≤ x ≤ m/px. The objective is x²(m − pxx)/py; its derivative is x(2m − 3pxx)/py. It rises until x = 2m/(3px) and falls afterward, and both endpoints give zero. The unique global demand is x = 2m/(3px), y = m/(3py). Initially the bundle is (12,6), giving U = 864. At the new prices it is (3/2,6), giving U = 27/2. These utility numbers represent rankings; their difference is not a dollar welfare loss.

      Scoring: budget binding/interior versus axes 1; demands with derivative or equivalent global argument 2; both numerical bundles 1; both utility levels and their ordinal interpretation 1.

    2. The old target is x²y = 864. Minimum expenditure at positive prices attains the target exactly, since excess goods could be reduced to lower cost. Put y = 864/x² for x > 0. At the new prices minimize g(x) = 8x + 864/x². Its derivative is 8 − 1728/x³, which vanishes at x = 6; g″(x) = 5184/x⁴ > 0, and expenditure diverges at both ends of this positive domain. Thus this is the unique global minimum. It gives y = 24 and expenditure 72. The required income addition, or positive-loss compensating variation, is 72 − 18 = 54 dollars. Buying the old bundle now costs 8·12 + 6 = 102, needing an addition of 84. Reoptimization saves 30 dollars relative to mechanically maintaining the old basket, while preserving its utility.

      Scoring: target and expenditure problem 1; minimizing bundle/global justification 1; expenditure 1; exact compensation 1; old-basket addition and reoptimization difference 1.

    3. The requirement is L ≥ q². Since labor has strictly positive price, any L > q² wastes expenditure at fixed output, so conditional demand is Lc(q) = q², including L = 0 at q = 0. Total cost is C(q) = 2q² + 3q dollars. MC = 4q + 3 dollars per output unit and AC = 2q + 3 for q > 0. C″(q) = 4 > 0 establishes strict convexity in output: marginal labor requirements rise with q. AC is undefined at zero; C(0) = 0 is well defined.

      Scoring: conditional labor and binding explanation 1; total cost 1; marginal cost 1; average cost/domain 1; strict convexity with relevant derivative 1.

    4. Profit is 11q − (2q² + 3q) = 8 − 2(q − 2)². Hence the unique global optimum over q ≥ 0 is q = 2, not just a stationary candidate. Labor is 4; revenue is 22; cost is 2·4 + 3·2 = 14; profit is 8. For any output price r ∈ [0,3], profit is (r − 3)q − 2q² ≤ 0, with equality only at q = 0. Thus zero output is uniquely optimal, including r = 3. All costs are avoidable in the supplied technology.

      Scoring: global square certificate/output 2; labor 1; revenue/cost/profit ledger 1; complete [0,3] boundary argument 1.

  2. D2 solutions

    1. Without a tax, 50 − 2Q = 5 + Q gives Q = 15 and price 20. With the tax, 50 − 2Q = 5 + Q + 9 gives Q = 12, buyer price 26 and seller retained price 17. Buyers pay 6 more and sellers receive 3 less per unit, accounting for the full 9-dollar wedge. These are economic incidence amounts relative to the old price, not the identity of the remitting party. Moving legal remittance leaves the same wedge, quantities and prices under the supplied enforcement, trading, financing and no-other-distortion assumptions.

      Scoring: untaxed equilibrium 1; taxed quantity 1; two taxed prices 1; incidence 1; correctly qualified remittance invariance 1.

    2. Before tax, CS = (1/2)(50 − 20)15 = 225; PS = (1/2)(20 − 5)15 = 225/2; public revenue is zero. Their sum is 675/2. After tax, CS = (1/2)(50 − 26)12 = 144; PS = (1/2)(17 − 5)12 = 72; revenue is 9·12 = 108; total is 324. Deadweight loss is 675/2 − 324 = 27/2 = 13.5 dollars, also (1/2)9(15 − 12). The consumer-surplus decline is 81 and the producer-surplus decline is 81/2; together they equal 243/2, of which 108 is transferred to the public recipient and only 27/2 is lost. Revenue is already in the ledger and must not be subtracted again.

      Scoring: untaxed CS/PS/total 1; taxed CS/PS 1; revenue and taxed total 1; DWL 1; transfer-versus-loss reconciliation 1.

    3. Wealth comes from the value of endowments: mA = 6p and mB = 6. Equal expenditure shares for A give (xA,yA) = (3,3p); B's two-thirds/one-third shares give (xB,yB) = (4/p,2). These are the global product-utility choices at positive wealth and prices, as obtained by budget substitution or the same share derivation as D1. Market clearing for x requires 3 + 4/p = 6, so p = 4/3. The allocation is A:(3,4), B:(3,2). Each good sums to 6. A spends (4/3)3 + 4 = 8 = 6p; B spends (4/3)3 + 2 = 6. Endowments determine wealth even though neither initial bundle consumes both goods.

      Scoring: two wealth functions 1; demands 1; relative price 1; allocation and both resources 1; both budgets 1.

    4. At p = 2, A demands (3,6) and B demands (2,2). Aggregate excess demand is z = (5 − 6,8 − 6) = (−1,2). Its price value is 2(−1) + 1(2) = 0, but neither market clears. More generally p(4/p − 3) + (3p − 4) = 0. Walras' law is an expenditure identity, not a statement that each excess demand vanishes.

      At equilibrium each consumer globally maximizes own-consumption preferences over its endowment-valued budget. Local nonsatiation implies that a weakly preferred bundle cannot cost less than that wealth: otherwise some budget slack could finance a strict improvement, contradicting optimization. A strictly preferred bundle must cost strictly more, since any affordable strict improvement already contradicts optimization. A putative feasible Pareto improvement would therefore cost in total more than combined wealth, 8 + 6 = 14. Feasibility and the positive equilibrium prices instead bound its cost by the value of aggregate resources, (4/3)6 + 6 = 14, a contradiction. The argument uses the stated complete exchange opportunities, own-consumption preferences, global budget optimization, local nonsatiation and feasibility; no distributional equality follows. The utilities are ordinal and not interpersonal welfare units.

      Scoring: excess demands and zero value 1; identity-versus-clearing distinction 1; individual weak/strict expenditure implications 1; aggregate contradiction 1; relevant assumptions and no equality inference 1.

  3. D3 solutions

    1. On the positive-price branch, πᵢ = (62 − cᵢ − qⱼ)qᵢ − qᵢ². Completing the square when its linear coefficient is positive, and checking zero otherwise, gives BRᵢ(qⱼ) = max{0,(62 − cᵢ − qⱼ)/2}. Beyond the demand intercept, any positive output earns −cᵢqᵢ < 0, whereas zero earns zero, so that region cannot beat the stated response. With both active, 2q₁ + q₂ = 54 and q₁ + 2q₂ = 42 give (q₁,q₂) = (22,10). Both are positive and satisfy their responses. Total output is 32 and price is 30. Profits are (30 − 8)22 = 484 and (30 − 20)10 = 100.

      Scoring: both response formulas with zero branches 1; global/zero-price justification 1; equilibrium quantities 1; price 1; both profits 1.

    2. The two-active equations become 2q₁ + q₂ = 54 and q₁ + 2q₂ = 22, yielding q₁ = 86/3 and q₂ = −10/3. The negative quantity violates feasibility. If firm 2 is inactive, firm 1's best response is 27. At q₁ = 27, firm 2's response is max{0,(62 − 40 − 27)/2} = 0, so (27,0) is an equilibrium. The opposite single-active candidate would be q₂ = 11, but firm 1 would respond with (54 − 11)/2 > 0; both inactive also fail firm 1's response. Thus no other active set works. Price is 35; profits are (35 − 8)27 = 729 and zero. Interior first-order equations are conditional on positive outputs and cannot override their nonnegative domain.

      Scoring: infeasible interior candidate 1; correct active set and quantity 1; both response checks/other branches 1; price/profits 1; boundary explanation 1.

    3. Writing A for Accommodate and F for Fight, I's plans are (A,A), (A,F), (F,A) and (F,F). After Standard, I chooses A because 7 > 3; after Specialized, I chooses F because 9 > 6. The entrant then compares Out's 0, Standard's 5 and Specialized's −1, choosing Standard. The unique subgame-perfect profile is E:Standard and I:(A,F), with path Standard then Accommodate and payoff (5,7). Fighting after Specialized is part of the full plan even though that history is not reached. All backward comparisons are strict.

      Scoring: four plans 1; Standard response 1; Specialized response 1; entrant comparison 1; full profile/path/payoff with off-path instruction 1.

    4. The pure Nash profiles are (Standard,(A,A)), (Standard,(A,F)) and (Out,(F,F)). With Standard played, I must accommodate there; its other response has no effect on its realized payoff. Against either plan starting with A, E obtains 5 from Standard, exceeding both its outside payoff and its possible Specialized payoff of 2 or −1. With Out played, I is indifferent among all complete plans, and Out is E's best response only when both entry payoffs are negative, which requires (F,F). Specialized cannot be an equilibrium: if I accommodates, it would gain by fighting; if I fights, E would rather stay out.

      Only (Standard,(A,F)) is subgame perfect. The first Standard equilibrium wrongly prescribes A after Specialized, where I prefers F. The Out equilibrium prescribes F after Standard even though I would prefer A if that history occurred. Ordinary Nash evaluates unilateral changes against the fixed complete plans from the start: I cannot improve its realized payoff when E stays out. Subgame perfection also tests that unreached continuation.

      Scoring: two Standard Nash profiles 1; Out Nash profile 1; completeness/no Specialized equilibrium 1; unique subgame-perfect survivor 1; on-path versus conditional threat explanation 1.

  4. D4 solutions

    1. At price 9, only low types sell, buyers value each acquired asset at 9 and break even. Lower offers attract no sellers away from the existing price. An offer strictly between 9 and 13 still attracts only low types and loses money. An offer of at least 13 attracts all types, whose average buyer value is (3/4)9 + (1/4)21 = 12; it therefore loses money as well. The low-only outcome survives all price deviations. Realized gains per initial seller are (3/4)(9 − 5) = 3 dollars. With quality verifiable, both types can trade and total gains are 3 + (1/4)(21 − 13) = 5. Hidden quality prevents two dollars of gains on high-type trades; the transaction price does not itself consume those gains.

      Scoring: price and participation 1; lower/intermediate deviation checks 1; all-type mean and high-offer check 1; realized gains 1; verifiable benchmark and lost-trade interpretation 1.

    2. The all-type buyer value is now (1/2)9 + (1/2)21 = 15. At p = 15 all sellers participate, buyers break even, a higher offer loses money and a lower offer attracts no one away from 15. Pooling at 15 is sustainable. The candidate low-only p = 9 is not: a buyer can offer 14, attract both types and earn 15 − 14 = 1 per acquired asset. Realized gains at pooling are (1/2)4 + (1/2)8 = 6, the verifiable-quality benchmark for the new population. The high-type population share changed from 1/4 to 1/2, and participation also changed from low-only to both types. One cannot attribute the difference in total gains solely to a price increase holding the old composition fixed.

      Scoring: pooling value/price 1; participation and deviations 1; concrete profitable deviation from low-only 1; new total gains 1; population/participation versus price distinction 1.

    3. Let Δw = wS − wF. H utility is wF + (2/3)Δw − 8 and L utility is wF + (1/3)Δw. Thus IC requires Δw/3 ≥ 8, or Δw ≥ 24. IR requires expected H wage wF + (2/3)Δw ≥ 11. Nonnegative wF and IC separately require expected wage at least (2/3)24 = 16. Wages (0,24) attain that stronger bound and meet IR, proving global cost minimization. Expected wage is 16; H utility is 8 and L utility is also 8; rent above the outside payoff is 5. IC and the failure-wage floor bind, while IR is slack. H is only weakly implemented under the stated recommended-action tie rule. A slightly larger success wage would make H strictly preferable at a higher expected cost.

      Scoring: IC 1; IR 1; global lower bound and attaining wages 1; expected wage/utilities/rent 1; binding constraints and weak-implementation tie 1.

    4. At original revenues, expected H revenue is (2/3)54 + (1/3)12 = 40; expected L revenue is (1/3)54 + (2/3)12 = 26. H profit is 40 − 16 = 24. L can be implemented with a flat wage of 3, at its IR lower bound, yielding 26 − 3 = 23; no hiring yields zero. Choose H under the stated tie rule. When success revenue becomes 48, effort probabilities and wage constraints stay unchanged. Expected revenues become 36 for H and 24 for L, so profits become 20 and 21. Choose L despite its lower gross revenue.

      With verifiable effort and original revenues, paying 11 for H and zero for L gives utilities 3 and 0, induces H and yields profit 40 − 11 = 29. Its five-dollar advantage over hidden H equals the hidden-action rent 8 − 3 = 5. The worker is risk neutral, so this is not compensation for risk aversion. A higher expected revenue does not justify H unless it covers its higher incentive-compatible wage cost.

      Scoring: initial H/L/no-hiring comparison 1; cheapest L contract justification 1; changed-revenue comparison/choice 1; verifiable H wage/profit 1; rent rather than risk-premium explanation 1.

  5. D5 solutions

    1. Unregulated trade equates 30 − Q = 6 + Q, giving Q = 12 and price 18. Integrating the specified margins gives B = 30Q − Q²/2 and C = 6Q + Q²/2, so W = 24Q − 3Q²/2 = 96 − (3/2)(Q − 8)². Consequently W(12) = 72 and the unique global target before administration is Q* = 8 with W = 96. Marginal damage is Q, so a tax of 8 per unit implements the target. Buyer price is 30 − 8 = 22 and retained seller price is 6 + 8 = 14. The fixed administration costs affect whether to introduce an instrument, but not its best positive target here.

      Scoring: unregulated quantity 1; benefit/cost/damage surplus expression 1; initial surplus 1; global target/certificate 1; corrective tax and two prices 1.

    2. At Q = 8 the tax raises 8·8 = 64. Its administration uses 10, leaving public funds of 54 available for lump-sum return. CS is (1/2)(30 − 22)8 = 32; PS is (1/2)(14 − 6)8 = 32; damage is 8²/2 = 32. Thus the full tax surplus is 32 + 32 + 64 − 32 − 10 = 86, fourteen above no intervention's 72. The 54 is a net fiscal balance, not an additional social gain on top of that ledger.

      A cap of 8 has permit price 22 − 14 = 8. Passive permit owners receive rent 64; producers face that permit opportunity cost and retain operating PS of 32. The cap's resource surplus is 96 − 4 = 92, twenty above no intervention and six above the tax. The public sector collects no permit-sale revenue in this free-allocation arrangement and needs 4 of lump-sum funding. Including the taxpayer's payment and the public receipt cancels the transfer, while the administration resources remain a cost. Choose the cap under the stipulated unweighted criterion and feasible financing. Free allocation changes who receives the scarcity value; using a permit still sacrifices the sale opportunity. Neither the 64 of permit rents nor the 64 of tax receipts is an extra resource benefit.

      Scoring: tax receipts/net public funds 1; tax surplus and no-intervention comparison 1; permit price/rents and opportunity cost 1; cap surplus/funding and instrument ranking 1; correct transfer/resource accounting 1.

    3. Bidder 1 wins and pays 34. Its surplus is 47 − 34 = 13, other bidder surplus is zero, seller revenue is 34 and total gains are 47. For any fixed rival bids, let h be their maximum. If h < v, truthfully bidding v wins at h and obtains v − h > 0; any bid losing instead is worse, and another winning bid gives the same payoff. If h > v, truthfully bidding v loses for zero; a deviation that wins pays h > v and loses utility, while a losing deviation ties zero. If h = v, winning or losing yields zero under the fixed tie rule. Thus no unilateral bid improves on truthfulness for any rival bids: it is weakly dominant. In the supplied profile, bidder 1 could bid 40 and still win at 34, so truthfulness is not the only best response. By contrast, bidder 2 bidding 50 would win and pay 47, losing 13; its own higher bid does not become the price it pays.

      Scoring: allocation/payment/surplus ledger 1; h < v case 1; h > v case 1; equality/tie and weak-dominance conclusion 1; concrete nonunique best reply 1.

    4. At date 0, redemption at date 1 has value (1/2)(4/5)10 = 4; retention to date 2 has value (1/2)(4/5)²18 = 144/25 = 5.76. The date-0 plan favors retention. At date 1, actual redemption gives 10 now, whereas retention gives (1/2)(4/5)18 = 36/5 = 7.2, so the actual self redeems. A sophisticated person anticipates redemption without a lock, worth 4 at date 0. The lock yields −1 + 144/25 = 119/25 = 4.76, so it is purchased, improving date-0 utility by 19/25.

      A fully naive person predicts that the date-1 self compares 10 with (4/5)18 = 72/5 = 14.4 and retains freely. This person expects 144/25 without the lock, versus 119/25 with it, so declines; the actual date-1 self nevertheless redeems. Under the explicitly chosen flow criterion, actual sophisticated commitment yields W = −1 + 18 = 17 rather than the no-lock W = 10, a gain of 7. This criterion differs from the date-0 discounted ranking, although both favor the lock here. At date 1, with the fee sunk, the person would prefer release and redemption: 10 > 7.2. Not every temporal self benefits. A reminder that leaves the same feasible choices and payoffs does not necessarily implement this lock; the model supplies neither random assignment nor evidence of a real reminder's attention, behavior or welfare effects.

      Scoring: both date-0 values and date-1 reversal 1; sophisticated purchase and gain 1; naive forecast/purchase/actual behavior 1; stated flow criterion and gain 1; temporal-self and empirical-reminder limitations 1.
