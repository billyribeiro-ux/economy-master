Remediation and reassessment

Maintain an error ledger containing the exact prompt, your inference, the violated assumption, a corrected derivation and one new problem. Rework the error from a blank page before rereading the solution. A copied correct answer is a learning aid, not a passing attempt.

  • Feasibility or incidence: write individual cash and real-resource accounts, including owners, outsiders, government and every relevant state. Return to chapters1,3,5,9–12.
  • Optimization or equilibrium: specify the full action set, all boundaries, the timing of information, the decision rule and the global proof. Return to chapters2,3,6,8–11.
  • Causal or archival inference: define the estimand, observation law, missingness, interference, selection and the exact restriction that excludes a rival explanation. Return to chapters2,4,5,7,8,10–12and Volume12.
  • Distribution or welfare: distinguish personal incomes, prices, amenities, transfer recipients and real costs; state the social weights and population boundary. Return to chapters1,4,5,8,9,11.
  • Implementation: identify authority as a fact requiring verification, specify capacity, cash reserves, consent and participation, and show a funded revision rule. Return to chapters6and12and the project.

For a failed domain, explain the repair aloud and solve a changed feasible problem before taking the alternate form. Preserve disagreements between defensible welfare criteria. External expert judgment remains necessary for real research and policy advice.

Concept and outcome index

  1. Public economics and taxation: Analyze tax and spending decisions with efficiency and distribution in view.
  2. Labor economics and human capital: Evaluate wage, employment and education mechanisms using credible designs.
  3. Industrial organization and competition: Assess market conduct and competitive policy using models and evidence.
  4. Development, poverty and structural change: Compare poverty-reduction mechanisms and their institutional constraints.
  5. International trade and global production: Analyze firm and worker responses to trade and production networks.
  6. Political economy and institutions: Analyze collective decisions and institutional incentives with defensible identification.
  7. Economic history and historical inference: Use historical evidence to distinguish mechanisms and avoid anachronistic causal claims.
  8. Behavioral and experimental economics: Design and critique evidence about nonstandard preferences and beliefs.
  9. Environmental, energy and climate economics: Evaluate externalities and long-horizon choices under uncertainty.
  10. Health, education and demographic economics: Assess service provision and demographic change through incentives and evidence.
  11. Urban economics, housing and inequality: Analyze spatial decisions and distribution with equilibrium feedback.
  12. Policy synthesis and institutional feasibility: Defend an implementable policy with evidence, uncertainty and distributional consequences.

Each written form maps all twelve chapter outcomes to bounded tasks. This does not mean that twenty subparts test every issue in every field. The project adds implementation, and the oral and retention tasks test changed assumptions. Full field expertise requires deeper literature, evidence and repeated expert evaluation.

Volume synthesis

A field supplies substantive mechanisms and institutions; it does not suspend the common requirements of choice, equilibrium, accounting and identification. Labor, education and health policies change behavior. Market structure and trade redistribute gains. Historical records have selection and dependence. Environmental and spatial effects cross the boundary of a private transaction. Political approval and administrative capacity can prevent a positive-surplus proposal from being implemented.

Carry three outputs into the next stage: an explicit model with global reasoning and named payers; an evidence map separating measured, assumed and unidentified objects; and a decision rule tied to resources, uncertainty and revision. The integrated policy/investment case brings these together with valuation. Specialization and original research requires a scoped question and new defensible contribution, followed by teaching and leadership portfolios whose learner-specific achievements cannot be supplied by a textbook.
