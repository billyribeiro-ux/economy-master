2. Reconstruct the bond explanation

At date 0, P₀=144/(6/5)²=100. At date 1 only one year remains, so P₁=144/(11/10)=1440/11. The gain is P₁−P₀=340/11 currency. Divide by the original 100: H=17/55, or 340/11%, approximately 30.91%. The tuple(price 0,price 1,gain,return) is (100, 1440/11, 340/11, 17/55).

Explanation: “You pay 100 now for a 144 payment two years away. A year later, the buyer discounts that same 144 for only one remaining year at the new 10% yield. That buyer’s price is 1440/11, so your sale gain is 340/11 on the 100 you invested. There is no coupon. The quoted purchase yield described a maturity calculation; your early-sale return also depends on the new price.” Both representations assume the specified dates, no default, no tax or trading costs and a sale at the supplied price. A maturity payoff is not the date 1 market price.

3. Repair the guaranteed-coupon analogy

At date 1, H(y)=144/[100(1+y)]−1 for y>−1. H=0 iff 1+y=144/100, so y=11/25=44%. Since the denominator is positive, H is positive below 44% and negative above it. At 60%, sale price 144/(8/5)=90 and H=−1/10=−10%. The boundary and changed result are (11/25, 90,-1/10).

Replacement explanation: “The contract promises the stipulated maturity payment, conditional here on no default. A market quote for selling that payment early changes when the discount rate changes. The original yield is a way to translate purchase price into the maturity payoff, not a separate annual coupon.” Holding to date 2 gives total return(144−100)/100=11/25=44%, whose compound annual rate is 20% because (6/5)²=36/25. At an unchanged date 1 yield 20%, price 120 and holding return 20% happen to agree. Equality in that special case does not validate a guaranteed-return rule.
