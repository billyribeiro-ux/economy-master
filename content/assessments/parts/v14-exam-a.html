Form A — construct, solve and certify

Administration. 100 points: five domains of 20 points, each with four 5-point parts. Allow 300 minutes; this is an author-designed, uncalibrated timing recommendation, not evidence of equivalence to another examination. Work individually. Parts 1–16 are closed notes with a basic calculator; Parts 17–20 may use the volume and standard-library computation, with equations and reasoning retained. Exact expressions earn full credit; numerical answers require sufficient precision to establish signs and feasibility. Read each model's timing: these are separate synthetic economies, not one calibration.

Advancement. Target at least 85 overall and 15 in each domain, followed by a new-parameter transfer problem and a teach-back. A critical error in funding, probability domains, identification, or a claimed global certificate requires remediation even if the numerical threshold is met. These are this library's design criteria. All twenty solutions below supply additive rubrics; award method credit for a correctly propagated arithmetic slip, but not for assuming away the constraint being tested.

Coverage is explicit: Parts 1–2 use Chapters 1 and 3; 3–4 use Chapter 2; 5 uses Chapter 4; 6 uses Chapter 5; 7–8 use Chapter 6; 9–10 use Chapter 7; 11–12 use Chapter 8; 13–14 use Chapter 9; 15–16 use Chapter 10; 17 uses Chapters 11–12; 18–19 use Chapter 11; 20 uses Chapter 12. Proof and source scopes are inherited from those chapters; all configurations and questions here are original.

Form A — full solutions and additive scoring

Form A solutions

1. A global finite household certificate

The candidate has c=(2,4,4,6). Its transition Q is given in Solution 2. Define V=(I−.9Q)⁻¹log c: the inverse exists as Σ(.9Q)ʲ, with bounded entries. Within each income state the policy selects the same next bond at both inherited wealth levels, so continuation terms cancel: V(1,2)−V(−1,2)=log2 and V(1,6)−V(−1,6)=log(3/2). The gain in continuation value from raising b′ is GL=.9[(3/4)log2+(1/4)log(3/2)] and GH=.9[(1/4)log2+(3/4)log(3/2)].

At (−1,2), b′=1 gives zero consumption and is infeasible. At (−1,6), choosing high bonds rather than low loses log(6/4) now and gains GH; at (1,2), choosing low rather than high gains log(4/2) now and loses GL; at (1,6), choosing high loses log(8/6) and gains GH. The three strict advantages are (0.024181955491,0.134043184358,0.141964991147). Thus the candidate policy's value is a fixed point of the full Bellman operator.

The finite feasible action sets have bounded log rewards. Comparing the same action in two maxima gives contraction modulus .9; geometric iteration yields a unique fixed point. Iterating its Bellman inequality along any adapted randomized/history-dependent feasible plan and taking expectations bounds that plan's discounted reward by V plus a bounded terminal term tending to zero. Equality holds for the candidate policy. This supplies global verification, not merely local Euler conditions.

1 point: feasible consumption and policy-value construction; 2: all three strict comparisons and the infeasible alternative; 2: bounded Bellman/history verification.

2. Joint mass and clearing

Q=((3/4,1/4,0,0),(0,0,1/4,3/4),(3/4,1/4,0,0),(0,0,1/4,3/4)). Direct multiplication verifies the invariant law (3/8,1/8,1/8,3/8). Every entry of Q² is at least 1/16. Hence Q² has a common component of weight 1/4 equal to the uniform law; subtracting it leaves a stochastic remainder. The L1 distance between two laws contracts by at most 3/4 every two steps. Completeness gives a unique two-step invariant law; commuting Q with Q² makes it one-step invariant too. All initial distributions converge to it.

Mean inherited bonds and next bonds are both zero. Endowment and consumption means are (4,4); summing c+b′=b+e proves goods clearing with no unexplained external asset. E[be]=−3/4−3/4+1/4+9/4=1, while E[b]=0. The marginals each assign half the mass to either level, yet their product would give zero covariance. Persistence creates wealth-income dependence even when aggregate net credit vanishes.

2: Q and invariant law; 1: positive-power uniqueness argument; 1: bonds and goods; 1: covariance and marginal limitation.

3. Exact owned-capital allocation

Use V(k,z)=B log k+H(z). Conditional on output Y=zkᵅn^(1−α), the investment-dependent objective log(Y−k′)+βB log k′ is strictly concave with negative-infinite endpoints. Its maximizer saves share βB/(1+βB). Matching log k gives B=α(1+βB), so s=αβ. Labor then maximizes (1+βB)(1−α)log n+χlog(1−n), also strictly concave with negative-infinite endpoints. Consequently n=(1−α)/[(1−α)+χ(1−s)]. Here (s,n,B)=(1/4,1/2,4/9), c=3Y/4 and k′=Y/4.

Competitive factors are w=(1−α)Y/n=4Y/3 and r=αY/k=Y/(3k); full depreciation makes r the gross capital payoff. Wage plus rental payments exhaust output, so CRS profits are zero and must not be added again. The labor condition χc/(1−n)=4Y/3=w holds. Since c′=(3/4)Y′, βE[r′/c′]=β·4/(9k′)=1/(3k′)=4/(3Y)=1/c, proving Euler consistency independently of next productivity.

Global optimality uses more than those first-order conditions. Under bounded positive productivity the candidate log-capital recursion is stable, its discounted utility is finite, and βᵀk′/c=βᵀs/(1−s)→0. Concavity of log consumption/leisure and production lets the dated supporting-tangent terms telescope against every feasible nonnegative-capital alternative. Capital and positive utility parts are bounded above; negative-infinite alternatives do not threaten the comparison. The terminal bound vanishes, so the candidate attains the global optimum on the stated positive-state domain.

2: both Bellman choices and coefficients; 1: factor/ownership accounts; 1: labor and Euler equations; 1: explicit global verification conditions.

4. Timing and stationary moments

Let xₜ=log kₜ, L=log2 and εₜ=log zₜ∈{−L,L}. Then xₜ₊₁=xₜ/3−8L/3+εₜ. Productivity at t is independent of inherited kₜ, whose shocks stop at t−1. The stable recursion has mean μx=−4L and variance 9/8 times L². One construction is xₜ=−4L+Σj≥0(1/3)ʲεₜ₋₁₋ⱼ; absolute convergence follows from bounded shocks. Independence kills cross terms in the variance sum. The geometric mean of capital is 1/16, not generally its arithmetic mean.

Because xₜ₊₁=log s+log Yₜ, log output has the same stationary variance and AR coefficient as x; its one-lag correlation is 1/3. Initial conditions contribute a term shrinking as (1/3)ᵗ, so these are long-run moments even if the initial distribution is not stationary. They are consequences of synthetic iid productivity, log preferences and full depreciation, not an estimated explanation of observed business cycles.

1: dated recursion; 2: stationary construction/mean/variance; 1: geometric mean and correlation; 1: timing and empirical limitation.

5. Entry closes the labor market

Write θ=v/u, f=√θ and q=1/√θ. Stationary values satisfy rU=b+f(E−U), (r+s)J=y−w, (r+s)(E−U)=w−rU, and κ=qJ with V=0. Pairwise Nash bargaining maximizes X^(1/2)J^(1/2) at fixed positive sum S=X+J, hence X=J=S/2. Subtraction yields S=(y−b)/(r+s+f/2). Combining with entry gives (1/4)√θ+(1/4)θ=1/2. Its left side is continuous, strictly increasing from zero to infinity, so there is exactly one positive root, θ=1.

It follows that (θ,f,q,w,J,U,E,u*)=(1,1,1,5/4,1/2,4,9/2,1/5). In particular κ=qJ=1/2, y−w=1/4=(r+s)J, rU=1=b+f/2, and u*=s/(s+f). Vacancy mass is 1/5 and real recruiting expenditure is 1/10 per time unit. Zero vacancy value means zero net present value, not free recruiting. Owners pay those bills and receive firm surplus; wages cancel from consolidated surplus.

2: value/bargaining/entry derivation; 1: root uniqueness; 1: numerical values; 1: flow and funding interpretation.

6. A bounded rational-expectations solution

Eliminate the Phillips equation to obtain Eₜ(xₜ₊₁,πₜ₊₁)ᵀ=A(xₜ,πₜ)ᵀ+(νₜ,0)ᵀ with A=((2,0),(−1,2)). Seek x=aν,π=bν. Then a/2=2a+1 and b/2=−a+2b, giving (a,b,i/ν)=(-2/3,-4/9,1/9). Direct substitution checks all three original equations, and bounded ν makes this particular solution bounded.

For two bounded solutions their difference h satisfies Eₜhₜ₊₁=Ahₜ. Repeated conditional expectation gives hₜ=A⁻ⁿEₜhₜ₊ₙ. Write A=2I+N with N²=0; then A⁻ⁿ=2⁻ⁿ(I−nN/2), whose norm tends to zero. Uniform boundedness of h forces hₜ=0 almost surely. This also excludes extra-signal bounded sunspots within the stated filtration and shock-law class; it is stronger than checking a guessed linear family. It proves a unique solution of a local linear model about the specified fiscal/monetary baseline, not global nonlinear equilibrium, optimal policy, or a floor-constrained solution.

1: reduced system; 2: particular solution and substitution; 1: conditional bounded uniqueness proof; 1: information and local-domain limits.

7. A financed terminal surplus

Date 0 gives B₀=6−1=5. Date 1 gives B₁=(5/4)5−2=17/4. Date 2 with B₂=0 requires s₂=(5/4)(17/4)=85/16. Multiply each budget by R⁻ᵗ and add: outstanding principal terms cancel, leaving D=s₀+s₁/R+s₂/R²+B₂/R². With the imposed terminal zero, 6=1+8/5+17/5. The equation is a feasibility/accounting condition. Preferences, incidence, distortionary tax costs and alternative allocations have not been supplied, so it cannot establish welfare optimality or universal sustainability outside this finite horizon.

8. Terminal default and creditor price

Strictly increasing log utility selects repayment exactly when y−b≥3y/4, namely b≤y/4; this guarantees positive repayment consumption. With repayment at equality, q(b)=4/5 for 0≤b≤1/2; q(b)=2/5 for 1/2<b≤5/4; and q(b)=0 for b>5/4. At zero face this quotes the marginal unit schedule but proceeds remain zero. The price is expected paid fraction divided by lenders' required gross return; it is not the same as the total proceeds q(b)b.

At b=1, date-0 sovereign funding is 2/5. In income2 it defaults, consumes3/2, destroys1/2 and pays zero. In income5 it consumes4 and pays1, with no destruction. The two lender payoffs have mean1/2, equal to (5/4)(2/5). Thus each state's goods account and the lender's expected return close. Backward induction is terminal here; adding future exclusion or re-entry would change the default comparison. No date-0 optimal issuance assertion follows from a prescribed face.

10. A sharp risk-price bound

From E[mR]=1 and Rf=1/E[m], E[R]−Rf=−Cov(m,R)/E[m]. Cauchy–Schwarz gives the requested inequality. In this case Var(m)=27/100 and Var(R)=25/27, so (Sharpe,sd(m)/E[m])=(0.577350269190,0.577350269190). Equality holds because the centered return is a negative constant multiple of the centered SDF; two nonconstant opposite-ordered two-state random variables are perfectly negatively correlated.

A positive SDF standard deviation only permits a nonzero premium; it does not force covariance with each payoff. A return orthogonal to the centered SDF has zero expected excess return even when the bound is positive. The positive-variance denominator and finite moments are essential to this displayed ratio.
