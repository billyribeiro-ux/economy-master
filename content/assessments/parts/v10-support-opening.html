Volume 10 assessment — fixed income, derivatives and quantitative finance

Defend a price, finance a hedge and state exactly what the evidence supports.

This package assesses the twelve chapters through a prerequisite diagnostic, two distinct written forms, an integrated pricing-and-risk project, oral transfer and delayed retention. All cases are original synthetic teaching material. A calculation is conditional on its contract, trading rights, information and model; its precision does not establish that those assumptions describe a real market. Complete the diagnostic before studying the volume and the remaining components after the chapters. The project requires an inspectable computation and decision record as well as correct numbers.

Prerequisite diagnostic

Allow 40 minutes without notes, with a basic calculator. There are eight five-point items. Readiness requires at least 34/40 and at least four points on each of items 1, 4 and 7. These are prerequisite algebra, probability, valuation and cash-flow skills from earlier volumes. They do not assume that the new stochastic calculus has already been learned. Write your reasoning before opening the answers.

  1. Price the dated right. A claim pays 12 CU in one year and 112 CU in two years. Prices today of one certain CU at those dates are 0.97 and 0.92. Find the claim's value if those payments can be bought separately with identical credit and settlement rights. Explain why multiplying the sum of both payments by the two-year discount factor changes the contract.
  2. A derivative and a finite change. Let P(y)=100e−2y, with y an annual rate in decimal units. Derive P′ and P″. At y=0, estimate the price change for an increase of 0.01 using first order, then compare with the exact change. State the units of the rate movement and the direction of the second-order correction.
  3. Independent shocks. Independent variables X and Y have mean zero and variances 0.04 and 0.09. Calculate E(3X−2Y) and Var(3X−2Y). Repeat the variance calculation if their covariance is 0.03. Does giving two shocks different names establish independence?
  4. Match two state payoffs. A stock costs 90 CU and pays 60 or 120 CU at a single terminal date. A claim paying one CU in both states costs 0.95. Signed holdings and borrowing are permitted. Replicate a payoff of zero in the first state and 30 in the second, price it, and find the two positive state prices. Explain what those prices establish about arbitrage and what they do not establish about physical frequencies.
  5. Sampling and selection. Under an explicitly justified IID model, 100 observations give a sample mean of −0.10 CU and sample standard deviation 2 CU. Compute the estimated standard error and an approximate 95% interval using 1.96. Interpret inclusion of zero. Would the same interval automatically retain its interpretation if this mean were selected as the best of 200 rules fitted to those observations?
  6. Curvature is a term with units. For f(S)=S²/50, evaluate delta and gamma at S=20. Find the exact change when S rises by 3 and decompose it into delta and one-half-gamma terms. What is the remainder in this quadratic example? Distinguish a second derivative from its contribution to a finite change.
  7. Finance a rebalance. A portfolio holds three shares worth 40 CU each and a bank balance of −100 CU. Over one period the account factor is 1.02 and the stock becomes 35, with no dividend. Find incoming wealth. Sell one share and retain all receipts in the bank. Give the new stock holding, bank balance and total wealth. If instead someone forces total wealth to five CU, identify the external cash transfer required.
  8. Assign the right evidence. A program agrees with an independent formula on a small benchmark and reproduces its calibration data exactly. Its sponsor calls that proof that the next year's hedge will be profitable. Identify what the two checks establish, two additional kinds of evidence needed for the proposed use, and one reason that a matching file hash does not settle the financial claim.
Diagnostic answers and five-point scoring
  1. The separately purchasable payments cost 12(0.97)+112(0.92)=11.64+103.04=114.68 CU. Discounting 124 entirely at the second date gives 114.08, lower by 0.60: it moves the first 12-CU receipt one year later. Equal-price reasoning requires identical dated rights and feasible matching trades. Credit: dated decomposition 1; arithmetic 2; timing explanation 1; rights qualification 1. Review capital budgeting and no-arbitrage pricing.
  2. P′(y)=−200e−2y and P″(y)=400e−2y. At zero, the linear change is −200(0.01)=−2 CU; exact change is 100(e−0.02−1)≈−1.980132669 CU. A 0.01 decimal-rate move is one percentage point, or 100 basis points. The positive second-order term ½(400)(0.01)²=0.02 offsets part of the linear loss. Credit: derivatives 2; linear/exact changes 2; scale and curvature interpretation 1. Review differential calculus.
  3. The mean is zero. Under independence the variance is 9(0.04)+4(0.09)=0.72. With covariance 0.03 it becomes 0.72+2(3)(−2)(0.03)=0.36. Independence implies zero covariance here, but labels supply neither property. Credit: mean 1; variance without covariance 1; signed covariance term 1; new variance 1; assumption explanation 1. Review expectation, covariance and transformations.
  4. Let Δ be shares and A terminal bank cash. Solve 60Δ+A=0 and 120Δ+A=30: Δ=0.5, A=−30. Cost is 45−30(0.95)=16.5 CU. State prices solve π₁+π₂=0.95 and 60π₁+120π₂=90, giving (0.40,0.55). Their positivity prices every attainable nonnegative nonzero payoff positively and rules out arbitrage under the stated finite trading rights. Dividing them by 0.95 gives pricing probabilities, not estimates of physical frequencies. Credit: two-state match 2; cost 1; state prices 1; interpretation 1. Review linear systems and state prices.
  5. Estimated SE is 2/√100=0.20 CU. The interval is −0.10±0.392=[−0.492,0.292] CU. Inclusion of zero is not proof of zero mean, and the interval permits economically nonzero effects. Data-dependent rule selection changes the inferential claim; use appropriate selection-aware inference or freeze the rule and collect new suitable evaluation data. Credit: SE 1; interval 2; nonrejection interpretation 1; selection qualification 1. Review inference and validation.
  6. Delta is 2S/50=0.8; gamma is 2/50=0.04 per CU. Exact change is (23²−20²)/50=2.58 CU. Delta contributes 2.4 and ½gamma contributes 0.18; the remainder is zero because the function is quadratic. Gamma itself is not the 0.18-CU contribution; it must be multiplied by half the squared move. Credit: two derivatives 2; exact change 1; decomposition/remainder 1; units and interpretation 1. Review Taylor expansion.
  7. Initial wealth is 120−100=20. Incoming shares are worth 105 and debt is 102, so wealth is 3 CU. Selling one share puts 35 in cash: new holdings are two shares and bank −67 CU, giving 70−67=3. Forcing wealth to five requires an external contribution of 2 CU; it is not a self-financed improvement. Credit: incoming wealth 1; holdings 1; bank 1; conservation 1; external transfer 1. Review dynamic portfolios and implementation cash.
  8. The benchmark supports conditional implementation correctness on that test; exact calibration supports reproduction of selected inputs under its fitting criterion. Neither alone proves identification, an adequate contract/model, or profitable future funded hedging. Additional evidence could include verified cash/dividend/funding rights, independent numerical and boundary checks, alternative models fitting the data, and frozen out-of-sample hedge outcomes including costs and failed trades. A hash binds bytes, not economic correctness. Credit: two evidence distinctions 2; two relevant additional requirements 2; hash limitation 1. Different examples earn credit when they address the requested use. Review provenance, validation and governance.

Administration, essential domains and advancement

Each written form totals 100 points, divided among five 20-point questions. The suggested allowance is 240 minutes with a nonprogrammable calculator and blank paper, without notes, internet or code. Prompts must supply the numerical information needed, including any normal-CDF values; proof and interpretation remain the learner's work. These are authored assessment conditions, not timings validated through student trials. Complete Form A first. Use the structurally distinct Form B only after documented remediation; the forms have not been statistically equated.

Essential domainChapter coverage in each formEvidence assessed
D1. Cash-flow and rate valuation1–3Date receipts, construct a curve, measure rate sensitivity, and distinguish physical rate beliefs from pricing restrictions.
D2. Credit and funded contracts4–5Separate default/recovery assumptions, financing, forward or swap rights, collateral and actual payment dates.
D3. Replication and continuous valuation6–8Match finite payoffs or feasible exercise rules; derive stochastic terms and a pricing result within its admissible assumptions.
D4. Sensitivities and computational evidence9–10Keep Greek units and funding explicit; distinguish approximation, sampling, inversion and hedge errors.
D5. Price identification and controlled use11–12Identify unspanned or contractual exposure, prove appropriate bounds, and connect validation evidence to an enforceable use decision.

Pass a written form with at least 85/100 and 15/20 in every essential domain, then pass the project and oral transfer. A passing reassessment uses the same criterion. These thresholds are library design choices, not institutional examination rules or professional certification. Preserve intermediate precision, and accept equivalent rounding that does not change a sign, admissibility boundary or policy decision.

Critical errors require repair even when the total passes. Examples are paying an earlier obligation with unavailable later cash; substituting physical frequencies for pricing probabilities without justification; using future information in an exercise or trading decision; treating a local mark reset as self-financing; claiming that numerical precision identifies an incomplete-market price; or treating a sampling interval as a bound on contract/model error. A learner must explain the error, redo the affected reasoning and pass a fresh transfer before advancing.

Scoring and remediation. Follow each form's explicit subpart allocations. Give method and justified follow-through credit after a carried arithmetic slip; penalize the same slip once within its calculation. Separately score a missed financial implication, such as negative cash or an inadmissible state price. Open answers may choose different controls or restricted uses when the evidence, assumptions and consequences support them. Record every domain score and any critical error. Revisit the mapped chapters, reconstruct a worked example without its solution, solve a new case with a changed information set or constraint, and teach back why the original argument failed. Open-resource project work has a different purpose from timed formula recall.
