Volume 13 assessment — graduate microeconomics and economic theory

State the model, prove the claim, and defend its economic meaning.

This package combines a prerequisite diagnostic, two distinct written examinations, an original theoretical research project, oral transfers and delayed retention. The problems and numerical economies are instructional constructions. Their assumptions make the answers determinate; applying a model requires separate evidence about preferences, information, resources and institutions. Take the diagnostic before studying the volume. Attempt the remaining components after all twelve chapters, keeping your first attempts, revisions and proof notes.

Prerequisite diagnostic

Use paper and a basic calculator, without notes or code. A proposed allowance is 75 minutes; it has not been calibrated on learners. Each question carries five points. The library's readiness criterion is 34/40, with at least four points each on questions 2, 7 and 8 and no unresolved feasibility or proof error. The solutions identify study routes. A formula recalled without its domain is incomplete evidence of readiness.

  1. Rationalize a choice rule. A person selects a from {a,b}, b from {b,c}, and c from {a,c}. Can a strict, complete and transitive preference rationalize these choices? Give the exact contradiction and change one choice to obtain a rationalizable rule.
  2. Respect a binding constraint. Maximize 6x−x²/2 over 0≤x≤4. Give the unique global choice, its value and the multiplier on the upper bound. If that bound becomes B near four, derive the value derivative with respect to B. Explain what is wrong with returning the unconstrained stationary point.
  3. Keep wealth and utility separate. With utility √w, a lottery pays wealth four or sixteen with equal probability. Compute expected wealth, expected utility, the certainty equivalent and the risk premium. Does √E[w] equal E[√w] here?
  4. Clear a small economy. Two price-taking consumers have utility xy over nonnegative quantities. Their endowments are (2,0) and (0,2), with no production. Normalize prices to (r,1), r>0. Derive both demand bundles, every normalized equilibrium and the value of aggregate excess demand at an arbitrary positive r.
  5. Solve the entire small game. A zero-sum game has row-player payoff matrix ((2,−1),(−2,1)); the column player receives its negative. Let p be the probability of the first row and q that of the first column. Find every independently mixed Nash equilibrium and its value, checking pure and boundary possibilities.
  6. Test an off-path threat. An entrant chooses Out, giving payoffs (0,3), or In. After In, an incumbent chooses Fight, giving (−1,0), or Accommodate, giving (2,2). Payoffs are in entrant–incumbent order. Find every pure subgame-perfect equilibrium. Is (Out,Fight) a normal-form Nash equilibrium, and is its threat credible?
  7. Check incentives and resources together. A buyer has type θ=1 or 3, each with probability one half, utility θq−t and outside utility zero. A seller offers L=(q,t)=(1,1) and H=(2,7/2); production costs q²/2 in real numeraire. The buyer starts with five cash units and the seller with two. Production is paid first, delivery and buyer payment follow, and enforcement is given. Check both incentive and participation constraints, funding, profits and expected total surplus. State the participation convention needed at a tie.
  8. Turn a residual into a bound. On [0,1], let T(x)=1/2+x/4. Verify invariance and contraction, establish the unique fixed point, and certify the distance of x̂=7/10 using its true residual. Compare the certificate with the actual distance.
Diagnostic solutions and scoring

  1. The first two choices imply a≻b and b≻c; transitivity requires a≻c, contradicting the third choice c≻a. Changing the last choice to a permits the ranking a≻b≻c, which explains all three menus. Completeness alone would allow a cycle; transitivity rules it out. Credit: the three comparisons 1; the transitive implication 1; contradiction 1; repaired choice 1; an explicit rationalizing ranking 1. Review preferences and revealed choice and the finite-choice foundations in Volume 5.

  2. The derivative 6−x is positive throughout [0,4], so the global optimum is 4 and its value is 16. For L=6x−x²/2+λ(B−x), stationarity at B=4 gives λ=2; the lower bound is slack. For B near four, V(B)=6B−B²/2 and V′(B)=6−B, also two at four. The stationary point six is outside the feasible interval. Monotonicity, or strict concavity together with the constrained conditions, proves global optimality. Credit: feasible maximizer 1; value 1; multiplier/sign convention 1; value derivative 1; global and feasibility justification 1. Review constrained optimization and duality and envelope reasoning.

  3. Expected wealth is 10, whereas expected utility is (2+4)/2=3. Solve √CE=3 to get CE=9, and the risk premium is 1 wealth unit. Strict concavity gives √10>3 for this nondegenerate lottery. Computing utility at average wealth would value a different, certain outcome. Credit: each of the four quantities 1; correct nonlinear-expectation comparison 1. Review risk and information.

  4. For positive income m, an interior bundle beats either axis, so the budget binds. Substituting y=m−rx gives xy=mx−rx², uniquely maximized at x=m/(2r), y=m/2. Thus each spends half its income on each good. Incomes are 2r and 2, giving A's demand (1,r) and B's (1/r,1). Aggregate excess demand is z=(1/r−1,r−1), so rzₓ+zᵧ=0 for every r>0. Clearing requires the unique normalized price r=1; both consume (1,1). Prices in other normalizations are proportional. At r=2, for example, z=(−1/2,1) is nonzero despite value balance. Credit: incomes and optimality 1; both demands 1; excess demands 1; unique normalized equilibrium 1; Walras law versus clearing 1. Review demand and general equilibrium.

  5. The first and second rows give expected payoffs 3q−1 and 1−3q. Indifference requires q=1/3. The column player's two choices leave expected row payoffs 4p−2 and 1−2p; minimizing makes it indifferent only at p=1/2. Both payoffs then equal 0. No pure cell has two best replies: after either pure column the row player strictly chooses the favorable row, and the column player then switches. A pure row similarly forces the unfavorable column and then a row change, excluding mixtures with one player on the boundary. Thus this interior pair is the entire equilibrium set. Credit: each indifference equation 1; both probabilities 1; value 1; complete support exclusion 1. Review static games.

  6. After In, accommodation gives the incumbent two instead of zero, so it is the unique optimal continuation. The entrant then prefers entry's two to outside's zero. The unique pure subgame-perfect profile is (In,Accommodate). In normal form, however, Out is a best reply to Fight, and after Out the incumbent's chosen continuation does not affect its payoff. Therefore (Out,Fight) is Nash but fails the proper subgame test: the incumbent would abandon the threat if entry occurred. Credit: continuation comparison 1; entry comparison 1; complete subgame-perfect strategy 1; normal-form Nash reasoning 1; credibility distinction 1. Review dynamic games.

  7. The low type receives zero from L and −3/2 from H; the high type receives two from L and 5/2 from H. Both assigned menu choices are strictly preferred to the other contract. The low type is indifferent between L and rejection, so actual low-type trade additionally uses an accept-at-indifference convention; absent that convention, rejection is another best response. The high type strictly participates.

    Production costs are (1/2,2), both funded by the seller's initial two; payments (1,7/2) are below the buyer's five. Seller profits are (1/2,3/2). Under the stated participation convention their expectation is 1; expected buyer surplus is 5/4. Real surplus is θq−q²/2, giving (1/2,4) by type and expectation 9/4, equal to expected profit plus buyer surplus. Payments redistribute cash; production consumes it. Credit: both IC comparisons 1; both IR conditions and tie 1; prefunding/payment feasibility 1; profit calculations 1; consolidated expected surplus 1. Review screening and contract constraints.

  8. The map sends [0,1] into [1/2,3/4] and contracts distances by one quarter. Iteration increments shrink geometrically, so their tails are summable; the resulting Cauchy sequence has a limit in [0,1], fixed by continuity. Two fixed points would have distance at most one quarter of itself, hence coincide. Solving x=1/2+x/4 gives x*=2/3. At x̂=7/10, the residual is 1/40. The triangle inequality gives distance ≤ residual/(1−1/4)=1/30, exactly the actual distance here. Credit: domain 1; contraction 1; existence/uniqueness and fixed point 1; residual 1; justified bound and comparison 1. Review fixed-point tools and computational certificates.

Administration and essential domains

Each written form has five 20-point domains, totaling 100. Use blank paper and a nonprogrammable calculator, without notes, internet or code. Exact fractions and symbolic expressions are acceptable. Follow the working-time allowance stated in each form; these are author-created study allowances, not calibrated examination timings. The reassessment uses distinct problem structures and has not been statistically equated to the first form. The research project is separately open-resource.

The written criterion is 85/100 overall and at least 15/20 in each domain, followed by the project, a fresh transfer and the oral assessment. These thresholds belong to this library. The form-specific coverage maps and project indicate which capabilities are sampled; no single examination checks every theorem or establishes original research quality.

Remediate critical errors before advancing, regardless of the aggregate score. Examples include an infeasible allocation or unfunded payment; an incentive constraint checked only at a selected action when a global claim is made; a noncredible punishment; a stationary point labeled globally optimal without justification; a singular or boundary solution silently discarded; an interpersonal welfare sum inferred from ordinal rankings alone; a finite numerical grid called a continuum proof; or exact fit treated as identification of a policy counterfactual.

Use each question's explicit point allocation. Give justified follow-through credit after a carried arithmetic error, while separately grading whether the learner recognizes its substantive effect. A proof can use a different valid route. A counterexample must satisfy the asserted theorem's assumptions; changing an assumption instead defines a new model. In open judgments, score the precise claim, evidence, assumptions, feasible alternatives and revision conditions. Record domain scores and unresolved errors alongside the overall total.
