Oral transfer and teach-back

Allow 20 minutes after the written examination and project. The examiner may display the numerical facts below, but the learner should explain the mechanism without notes. Four prompts are worth five points each; require at least 17/20 and no unresolved critical error. Per prompt award two points for correct economic and quantitative reasoning, one for the relevant constraint or information date, one for an explicit assumption or boundary, and one for a clear answer that distinguishes a calculation from an evidence claim. Follow-up changes should be disclosed prospectively rather than introduced into an earlier information set.

  1. A liability changes the action menu. At a decision node, liquid wealth is 10 CU. Safe wealth has terminal gross return one. A risky investment has gross returns 1.6 and 0.8 with equal conditional probabilities. A terminal liability of 9 must be paid. Available risky fractions are 0, 0.1, 0.5 and 1; there is no intermediate trade or borrowing. The objective is expected log of terminal surplus, which must be strictly positive in each possible state. Which action is best? How does the choice change if a guaranteed additional CU arrives at the terminal date before the liability? Explain why terminal income may alter this node's choice but cannot be used to pay an earlier bill.
  2. Equal expected payoff is not equal price. Physical state probabilities are (0.75, 0.25), and a strictly positive stochastic discount factor is (0.8, 1.4). Price claims X = (1, 0) and Y = (0, 3). They have the same physical expected payoff. Explain the price difference and identify the safe bond's price and the distinction between physical and normalized pricing probabilities.
  3. Design evidence that could disappoint you. A manager presents the best strategy found among 100 historical rules, a positive net information ratio, and a benchmark chosen after inspecting its worst quarter. Describe a prospective evaluation that could credibly reject the manager's skill claim. Address the information cutoff, strategy search, original opportunity set, fees and benchmark, and the uncertainty remaining after a successful test.
  4. Infeasibility is a possible result. Cash is 20 CU, a capital call of 18 CU and a grant payment of five CU are both due today. The cash floor afterward is four. A locked private asset has NAV 60 but cannot be sold or pledged today. A liquid bond can be sold at 0.95 CU per unit, without other costs, but the authorized sale cap is seven units. No credit facility exists. Can the specified plan be authorized as feasible? Quantify the gap after the largest permitted sale and describe what evidence an actual repair would require.
Oral benchmarks and transfer boundaries
  1. Without the extra income, surplus for risky fraction w is (1 + 6w, 1 − 2w). Fractions 0.5 and 1 violate strict positivity. Fractions zero and 0.1 have surplus products 1 and 1.28, respectively, so 0.1 maximizes 0.5 ln(product) over the given menu. With one extra CU arriving before payment, surplus becomes (2 + 6w, 2 − 2w). Products for 0, 0.1 and 0.5 are 4, 4.68 and 5; fraction one has zero bad-state surplus and is inadmissible. Now 0.5 is optimal within this menu. A node-specific continuation decision conditions on information then observed; this finite menu does not establish the optimum over all real fractions. Terminal resources enter terminal surplus, not an earlier cash account. This is a transfer of liability-aware contingent choice, with the log domain enforced before comparing objectives.
  2. State prices are q = p m = (0.60, 0.35). X costs 0.60 CU and Y costs 1.05 CU, although both have physical expected payoff 0.75 CU. Y delivers in the state with the larger discount factor, where marginal valuation is higher in this representation. A unit safe bond costs 0.60 + 0.35 = 0.95. Normalized pricing probabilities are (12/19, 7/19), not physical probabilities (3/4, 1/4); discounting a physical expected payoff at the safe rate would not price both claims. A positive SDF gives this linear valuation for the specified claims; it does not by itself identify an investor's utility or prove a unique SDF in a general incomplete market. Review state prices and discount factors.
  3. Freeze the strategy and all formation rules using a documented cutoff; retain the 100-rule search history and an independently dated evaluation period. Define a feasible benchmark before seeing evaluation returns, including exposure/currency/cash conventions. Preserve the original eligible cohort, failed funds and delisting recoveries; record availability dates and revisions. Reconcile gross returns to realized fees and execution costs, and specify an uncertainty analysis appropriate to the sampling dependence and selection claim. A weak or negative fresh net result is allowed to overturn the claim; redefining the strategy or benchmark after the result starts a new experiment. A positive evaluation remains conditional on risk controls, sampling assumptions, persistence and implementability. It does not establish causality or guaranteed future alpha. Other defensible designs earn credit when they make these same claims falsifiable. Review factor exposures, joint hypotheses and search and performance evaluation.
  4. The required sale proceeds are 18 + 5 + 4 − 20 = 7 CU. Seven units generate only 6.65, leaving cash 3.65 and a reserve gap 0.35. The locked NAV supplies no today's cash. Without the cap, a sale of 7/0.95 = 140/19 ≈ 7.3684 units would suffice; it is not permitted under the stated policy. A repair needs documented authority and timely resources: for example an actual pre-deadline cash contribution of 0.35, or an authorized, executable change in the sale cap. A hoped-for waiver, renegotiated grant or loan cannot be booked as though it already exists. Preserve the failure and escalation record. Review illiquidity and policy implementation.

After the oral, write a short correction memo: the original claim, the failed assumption or calculation, the changed decision and one fresh test. A polished explanation without a valid cash or probability calculation does not repair the underlying domain error.

Delayed retention assessment

Two to four weeks later, allow 90 minutes, closed-book with a basic calculator. These six new items are worth ten points each, for 60 overall. Require at least 51/60 and at least eight on every item. They deliberately join chapter boundaries; a correct isolated formula is insufficient. Use the same carry-forward and critical-error rules as the written examinations. Do not use these cases as additional observations in the project's holdout sample.

  1. Returns and a constrained risk minimum. An account starts at 1,000 CU, earns 8% in year 1, then immediately receives an external contribution of 200. Year 2 earns −5% before a fixed 16-CU ending fee, paid from the account. Compute ending wealth, the net two-year time-weighted return and the annual money-weighted IRR for this contribution history; explain why the last two differ. In a separate prospective two-asset allocation, variances are 0.04 and 0.01 and covariance is 0.005. Fully invest without shorts, with asset-A weight at least 0.20. Derive the minimum-variance weight and portfolio variance; identify the unconstrained minimum too.
  2. A bound is not an equilibrium forecast. There are three possible terminal states with physical probabilities (0.20, 0.50, 0.30). A unit safe bond costs 0.95 CU. Asset A costs one CU and pays (0, 1, 2). Signed frictionless trading is allowed. Parameterize all strictly positive state-price vectors matching both prices and derive the open arbitrage-free price interval for claim X = (1, 0, 1). Exhibit the arbitrage if X instead costs the lower endpoint. Separately, a proposed CAPM representation assigns A beta 1.4 and the market an expected excess return of 5%. Compare its required return with A's physical expected return using the safe rate implied by the bond. Explain whether a discrepancy alone supplies an arbitrage.
  3. An omitted exposure and an apparent winner. Four equally weighted observations have traded factor excess returns F = (−2, −1, 1, 2)% and G = (1.5, −0.5, −0.5, 1.5)%. A strategy's excess returns are Y = (0, −2.5, 0.5, 6)%. Treat these as a finite descriptive dataset. Fit Y on an intercept and F, then explain what changes when G is included. A benchmark of 1.5F was fixed before evaluation. Compute the gross and net information ratios using population moments over these four observations; the strategy pays a constant fee of 0.4 percentage points per observation and the benchmark does not. Explain why discovering this strategy as the best of 100 rules on these same observations does not demonstrate persistent skill.
  4. Nontraded income changes the hedge. Initial liquid wealth is 10 CU. Investing x CU in a risky asset produces terminal gross returns 1.4 in boom and 0.8 in slump; the remainder earns gross one. Each state has probability one-half. Nontraded terminal income is (2, 6) CU and a fixed five-CU liability is paid after income arrives. Maximize E[S] − (λ/2) Var(S), where S is terminal surplus, λ = 1 CU−1, and 0 ≤ x ≤ 10. Derive the optimum, state surpluses and variance. Recompute the optimum with no nontraded income and explain the direction of the change. Do not treat the nontraded income as money available to buy assets initially.
  5. Appraisal risk and payable cash. A pool has 50 CU cash before day 10. A capital call of 30 and a grant payment of 12 are due that day; its closing cash floor is ten. A private holding has reported NAV 100 but cannot be sold or pledged before month 12. In a joint stress an additional eight-CU margin payment is due on day 10. A separate liquid bond can be sold on day 10 for 0.98 CU per unit, without fees, up to twelve divisible units. Calculate the base and joint-stress funding gaps and the minimum stress sale. If capacity falls to ten units, is the stress plan feasible? Separately, an appraisal series obeys at = 0.6at−1 + 0.4ut; the prior appraisal return is 2% and the current one −2%. Recover the latent current return. Under stationary iid latent returns, derive the appraisal-to-latent variance ratio and state why this stylized unsmoothing does not create a liquid sale price.
  6. Measure the original target and fund the policy. The decision is to sell 200 shares at a decision benchmark of 40 CU per share. Only 120 sell, at average price 39.50; explicit fees total six CU. The remaining 80 shares are marked at 39 at the horizon. There are no other positions, flows or initial cash. Compute whole-target implementation shortfall and its filled and unfilled components. The arrival midpoint for the fills was 39.80; compute the signed effective spread per filled share, excluding fees. A prospective cash floor of 5,000 CU must now be met. Additional shares can be sold immediately at 39, with a fee of 0.05 per share, in whole shares and with capacity for all 80. Find the minimum additional sale, resulting cash and whether that repair completes the original mandate. Explain how to report the repair without rewriting the decision benchmark.
Delayed solutions, full reasoning and ten-point scoring
  1. The first valuation is 1,080; after the contribution, 1,280 enters year 2. Gross ending assets are 1,280 × 0.95 = 1,216; the fee leaves 1,200 CU. The net second-period factor is 1,200/1,280 = 0.9375. Linking the two flow-separated factors gives 1.08 × 0.9375 − 1 = 0.0125, or 1.25% net over two years. The annual money-weighted equation is −1,000 − 200/(1+i) + 1,200/(1+i)² = 0. Its economically admissible root is i = 0; the other gross-factor root is −1.2, outside 1+i > 0. More money was invested in the losing second period, so a positive linked return can coexist with zero total cash profit and zero IRR. Compare annual rates consistently: annualizing the TWR gives √1.0125 − 1, still positive, whereas the annual IRR is zero; the two-year TWR and annual IRR have different reporting horizons. For the separate allocation, v(w) = 0.04w² + 0.01(1−w)² + 0.01w(1−w) = 0.01 − 0.01w + 0.04w². Thus v′ = −0.01 + 0.08w, with unconstrained minimum w = 1/8 and variance 0.009375. On [0.20,1], variance is increasing from the lower endpoint; the constrained minimum is w = 0.20, variance 0.0096. Credit: dated ending wealth and fee treatment 2; net TWR 1; IRR and difference 2; covariance quadratic 2; both optima with justification 2; constrained variance 1. Repair returns, covariance and constraints.
  2. The restrictions are q₁ + q₂ + q₃ = 0.95 and q₂ + 2q₃ = 1. Write q = (t, 0.90−2t, 0.05+t), where strict positivity is equivalent to 0 < t < 0.45. X therefore has price q₁ + q₃ = 0.05 + 2t, ranging over (0.05,0.95). Every interior price supplies a strictly positive pricing vector for the extended market. At price 0.05, buy X, short one A and buy one bond. Initial cost is 0.05 − 1 + 0.95 = 0, with payoff (2,0,0): an arbitrage because the first state is possible. The bond implies rf = 1/0.95 − 1 = 1/19 ≈ 5.2632%. A's physical expected payoff is 0.5 + 0.3 × 2 = 1.1, so its expected return is 10%. Proposed CAPM required return is 1/19 + 1.4(0.05) ≈ 12.2632%, a discrepancy of −2.2632 percentage points. That rejects joint compatibility of the supplied moments/beta and proposed CAPM restrictions, not the existence of positive state prices. A factor-model discrepancy is not itself a statewise arbitrage. Credit: pricing family and positivity 3; interval and endpoint trade 3; safe and expected returns 2; CAPM comparison and implication 2. Repair equilibrium restrictions and incomplete-market pricing.
  3. In percentage-point units, E[F] = 0, Var(F) = 2.5, E[G] = 0.5, Var(G) = 1 and Cov(F,G) = 0. The exact identity is Y = 1.5F + 2G. The one-factor projection has slope 1.5 and intercept one percentage point, with residual 2(G−0.5). Adding G gives slopes 1.5 and 2, zero intercept and zero residual in this dataset. Relative to the fixed 1.5F benchmark, gross active returns are (3,−1,−1,3)%, mean 1% and population tracking error 2%; gross IR is 0.5. The constant fee changes the mean to 0.6% without changing the descriptive tracking error, yielding net IR 0.3. Relative to the full two-factor fitted identity, the net intercept is −0.4 percentage points. An omitted exposure with a nonzero sample mean can explain a one-factor intercept; a selected four-observation fit cannot identify future skill or validate the pricing of either factor. Freeze a feasible future benchmark, preserve the search and original cohort, and evaluate fresh net outcomes with stated uncertainty and dependence assumptions. Credit: one-factor moments/coefficients 2; exact two-factor identity and interpretation 2; gross IR 2; net IR and fee effect 2; selection/evidence distinction 2. Repair factor construction, search and joint hypotheses and net performance.
  4. Terminal surplus is S = (7 + 0.4x, 11 − 0.2x) CU. Hence E[S] = 9 + 0.1x and Var(S) = (−2 + 0.3x)² CU². With the specified units for λ, the objective's derivative is 0.7 − 0.09x; its second derivative is −0.09, so the interior maximizer is x = 70/9 ≈ 7.7778 CU. Surplus is (91/9,85/9) CU, mean 88/9 and variance 1/9 CU². With no nontraded income, surplus is (5 + 0.4x, 5 − 0.2x), mean 5 + 0.1x and variance 0.09x²; the optimum falls to x = 10/9 ≈ 1.1111. The original countercyclical income insures the low risky-return state, allowing larger liquid risky exposure under this surplus objective. Both optima lie within the actual ten-CU initial budget; neither capitalizes the nontraded receipt into cash available today. Credit: dated surplus 2; mean/variance/objective 2; first optimum and concavity 2; state outcomes/variance 1; no-income optimum 2; hedge and budget explanation 1. Repair strategic allocation.
  5. Base closing cash before a sale is 50 − 30 − 12 = 8, two below the floor. With the extra margin payment it is zero, ten below the floor. The minimum stress sale is 10/0.98 = 500/49 ≈ 10.2041 units, within capacity twelve, leaving exactly ten CU cash. With capacity ten the largest proceeds are 9.80; the reserve still fails by 0.20. The locked NAV cannot cure either date-10 gap. Unsmoothing gives ut = (−0.02 − 0.6 × 0.02)/0.4 = −0.08, or −8%. If latent returns are iid with finite variance and the filter is stationary, Var(a) = 0.6² Var(a) + 0.4² Var(u), so Var(a)/Var(u) = 0.16/0.64 = 1/4; reported volatility is half latent volatility in that model. Serially correlated latent returns, changing appraisal coefficients or a nonstationary start invalidate that variance shortcut. Unsmoothing changes a statistical estimate, not redemption terms or executable bids. Credit: two gaps 2; minimum sale and cash reconciliation 2; restricted-capacity conclusion 1; recovered return 2; variance derivation 2; liquidity/model boundary 1. Repair alternative assets and execution capacity.
  6. The paper target would produce 200 × 40 = 8,000 CU. Actual wealth at the horizon is 120 × 39.50 − 6 + 80 × 39 = 7,854; shortfall is 146 CU. Filled cost is 120(40−39.50) + 6 = 66; opportunity cost on the remaining shares is 80(40−39) = 80. With sell sign s = −1, effective spread excluding fees is 2s(P−M) = 2(−1)(39.50−39.80) = 0.60 CU per filled share. Cash before repair is 4,734 CU. Each additional share supplies 39 − 0.05 = 38.95, so the required integer sale is ceil[(5,000−4,734)/38.95] = 7. Six would leave 4,967.70; seven leave 5,006.65 and 73 shares. That sale repairs the cash floor, not the original all-200-share completion target. Its additional 0.35-CU fee also reduces wealth if the same 39 mark is retained. Keep the original decision price, original 200-share target, partial-fill shortfall, new instruction time, extra fee and residual risk visible. An authorized prospective change can alter the future mandate but cannot erase its earlier execution history. Credit: total and two-component shortfall 4; signed spread 2; minimum integer repair/cash 2; remaining target and honest reporting 2. Repair signed trading costs and policy reporting.

From investment theory to accountable decisions

Investment analysis links three different questions. What does a security pay, and when? Which prices or expected returns are compatible with stated trading and equilibrium assumptions? Which feasible policy should this investor choose, given liabilities, information, costs and governance? A replication answers a pricing question under its trading assumptions. A sample regression describes a dataset. An allocation solves a stated objective. None alone establishes the other conclusions.

The volume's project makes those connections inspectable through a frozen research history, a funded policy and a prospective comparator. The transferable habit is to preserve the chain from information to decision to execution to evaluation, including the cases in which the answer is infeasible or inconclusive. Advanced instruments in Volume 10, institutional balance sheets in Volume 11 and advanced inference in Volume 12 extend that chain; these links identify successor specifications and do not imply unwritten material is available. The source routes in the twelve accepted chapters support the methods used here; the assessment's cases, data, scoring and transfer questions are author-created.
