Question 1. What equal-price choices do not identify — 20 points

Consumption is (x,y) in the nonnegative orthant. Two complete observations are unique choices (3,3) at prices (1,1), income 6, and (5,5) at the same prices, income 10. Candidate indices are U(x,y)=sqrt(xy) and L(x,y)=min(x,y). For the conditional duality and information tasks explicitly maintain U, and for lotteries explicitly maintain expected U. Positive prices and nonnegative income are required. No finite observations are asserted to identify these maintained assumptions.

The separate consumption-prize decision has states low/high with prior (1/2,1/2). A safe action delivers (2,2) in either state; a risky action delivers (0,0) in low and (4,4) in high. An outside provider already owns four units of each good and delivers the selected bundle after the state is realized, retaining unused goods. The action is chosen after any observed signal. With columns minus/plus and rows low/high, experiment P=((3/4,1/4),(1/4,3/4)). Experiment Q is generated by flipping P’s signal independently with probability 1/4. There is no acquisition charge and no fee is subtracted from utility. The expenditure comparison asks how much wealth would be needed, not for an unfinanced compensating payment.

For reference, the two observed incomes are (6,10). The utility models are U(x,y)=√(xy); L(x,y)=min(x,y). In the changed-price calculation use (4,1) and income 10; the compensated target is 5.

  1. (a) 4 points. Show that both indices exactly rationalize the two complete observations. Give their demanded bundles at prices (4,1), income 10, and explain the resulting identification limit.
  2. (b) 6 points. Conditional on U, derive Marshallian demand and expenditure/Hicksian demand globally for p1,p2>0, m≥0 and target u≥0. At p=(4,1), target u=5, compute the compensated price-derivative matrix, verify its symmetry, negative semidefiniteness and price-null direction. Compare the required expenditure with baseline income 10.
  3. (c) 4 points. Derive Q, signal probabilities and posteriors under both experiments. Find every optimal action at each signal and the optimal expected-U values with no signal, P and Q.
  4. (d) 6 points. Prove the one-way finite Blackwell value inequality whenever Q=PG with row-stochastic G and identical feasible actions and payoff law. Explain why deterministic choice equivalence of U and U² does not authorize replacing expected U by expected U² here; use the no-signal actions as a witness.

Question 2. Redistribute goods before prices adjust — 20 points

Two households A,B and two divisible goods. Utilities are uA=xA^(1/3)yA^(2/3) and uB=xB^(2/3)yB^(1/3) on the nonnegative orthant. Original endowments are A:(6,0), B:(0,6); no production, outside resources, borrowing or money creation. At date 0 an announced compulsory transfer t in [0,6] moves t units of B’s second good to A. The resulting endowments are (6,t),(0,6−t). At date 1 both trade simultaneously in a competitive exchange, with p=(r,1). The transfer is physically owned and funded by B before trading; its acceptability is not inferred from the welfare theorem.

The first-good Cobb–Douglas shares in A,B order are (1/3,2/3) and the original endowment matrix is ((6,0),(0,6)). The requested transfer checks are (0,3,6). Utility is uA=xA^(1/3)yA^(2/3); uB=xB^(2/3)yB^(1/3).

  1. (a) 6 points. Prove household global demand, derive every competitive equilibrium for all t in [0,6], and exclude zero-price equilibria. Include the zero-income endpoint rather than citing a sufficient existence theorem whose hypotheses are not checked.
  2. (b) 5 points. Compute all incomes and allocations at t=0 and t=3. Verify both commodity balances and each budget. Holding r and total income fixed instead, derive the change in aggregate first-good demand when one unit of income is moved from B to A. Explain why a representative demand depending only on total income fails.
  3. (c) 5 points. Give a complete first-welfare argument for these equilibria, including the t=6 endpoint. Does efficiency mean the transfer from t=0 to t=3 is a Pareto improvement? Establish both households’ directions of change without adding ordinal utilities across households.
  4. (d) 4 points. Audit: “Walras law gives market clearing; the transfer leaves total resources unchanged, so prices and aggregate demand cannot change.” Give the exact counterexample here and a minimal qualified replacement for each statement.

Question 3. Who supplies a public service after the move order changes? — 20 points

Two players each own two units of a consumable input. Each chooses V (volunteer and consume its two units) or N (retain them). If at least one volunteers, each receives service benefit 3 in common payoff units; there is no service otherwise. Net incremental utility is 3*1{at least one V}−2*1{own V}. Benefits are not cash available to finance a prior action. No side payments occur. First analyze simultaneous independent mixed strategies. Then analyze a private recommendation law, and finally a different perfect-information protocol in which player 1 moves first, player 2 observes that action and moves once, with the identical terminal payoff law.

Equivalently, let 3 be the service benefit and 2 the input cost. With actions encoded as V=1,N=0, ui=3·1{a1+a2≥1}−2ai. The recommendation probabilities are (1/2,1/2) on (V,N),(N,V).

  1. (a) 4 points. Construct the simultaneous payoff matrix and find every independent mixed Nash equilibrium, including pure cases. Compute provision probability and expected payoffs at the interior equilibrium.
  2. (b) 4 points. A mediator privately recommends (V,N) or (N,V), each with probability 1/2. Verify every recommendation-contingent deviation, calculate utilities and input use, and test independent mixing with the same marginal probabilities.
  3. (c) 6 points. In the sequential protocol write player 2’s four complete contingent plans, list the entire strategic-form mixed Nash set and give the complete subgame-perfect outcome. Let p be player 1’s probability of V; describe any off-path mixing precisely.
  4. (d) 6 points. Prove directly which strategic-form equilibria are trembling-hand perfect. Use the fully mixed best-reply characterization already proved in C06; do not claim strategic-form perfection and sequential equilibrium coincide in all games. Compare this conclusion with the simultaneous CE result.

Question 4. Sell a promised service whose quality must first be induced — 20 points

One buyer has private value θ in {2,6}; probability of 6 is π in (0,1), initially π=1/3. It values one successful service at θ, failure at zero, is risk neutral, owns 6 units of currency and has outside option zero incremental utility. A risk-neutral intermediary owns and commits an escrow of 2 before announcing a direct menu. No borrowing or additional wage authority is allowed. The menu assigns a deterministic operating mode and a nonrefundable upfront price T to each type report; there are no lotteries over modes. Every quoted price satisfies 0≤T≤6 and is paid from buyer to intermediary before the operating contract is executed. Quality is the induced probability of success, not a separately enforceable hidden effort.

For any active job a separate risk-neutral worker has no private type, reservation utility zero and effort choices H,L, chosen after accepting its output-contingent wage. H gives success probability 3/4 and utility cost 1; L gives probability 1/4 and cost 0. Public success/failure is verifiable; wages wS,wF are enforceable and constrained to [0,2] by the committed escrow. Effort cost is disutility, not a second cash invoice. Wage payments occur after output; the intermediary retains unused escrow and the buyer price. Mode 0 supplies no job/no service and costs zero. Modes L,H recommend the respective effort and use the least-cost implementing wage schedule. At weak effort IC the worker follows its recommendation; participation and buyer-truth ties favor the stated target. These are explicit selected-outcome conventions, not unique implementation. Worker incentives must be solved before the buyer screening problem.

The worker success probabilities in H,L order are (3/4,1/4), effort costs (1,0), and wage bounds (0,2). Initial buyer/intermediary/worker cash is (6,2,0). Buyer utility is θq−T. The two prior transfers are (1/4,1/2).

  1. (a) 5 points. Derive all H-versus-L incentive and participation restrictions and globally minimize expected wages for each active mode under the escrow constraint. State exactly which delivery probabilities and expected wage costs result.
  2. (b) 6 points. For an arbitrary direct pair of the three modes, derive necessary and sufficient buyer IC/IR conditions. Show why one may restrict a profit optimum to qL≤qH and payments TL=2qL, TH=6qH−4qL. Do not import a continuum derivative formula or assume local IC is enough.
  3. (c) 5 points. At π=1/3 compare every feasible ordered mode pair and find all profit-optimal menus. Derive the complete optimal policy for π in (0,1), including the equality boundary. Provide the winners and profits at π=1/4 and 1/2.
  4. (d) 4 points. At the baseline prior reconcile intermediary profit, buyer utility, worker utility and total real surplus for both optimal menus, and give actual statewise cash accounts. If the worker must strictly prefer the recommended effort, with the escrow and other tie conventions unchanged, identify the change in feasible modes and the new baseline optimum.

Two applicants A,B and institutions X,Y, each with one seat. All applicant reports may be any strict ordered subset of {X,Y}; omitted names are unacceptable. These values are stipulated comparable benefit units only for the requested conditional sum, not inferred from ordinal ranks.

In the changed environment A’s actual payoff at a complete allocation mu is its intrinsic placement value plus λ*1{B is assigned X}, λ≥0. B and institution payoffs remain as above. This is an allocation-dependent externality, not a cash payment, a new seat or a permission for pair deviations. First hold B’s report fixed at [Y,X] while A can report any ordered subset. Historical data come from an older administrative rule that imposed M1=(A–X,B–Y) without any reporting stage; they record its assignments and all realized payoffs. “DA is proposer-optimal, so reporting intrinsic placement rankings is dominant even with allocation spillovers. Stability proves the new rule maximizes total value and benefits every participant. Its success under the old administrative assignment identifies the post-reform outcome.”

In X,Y,outside order, intrinsic applicant values are ((3,2,0),(2,3,0)). Institution values, rows X,Y and columns A,B, are ((1,2),(2,1)).   1. (a) 5 points. In the ordinary partner-only model enumerate every stable matching and A’s outcome for each of its five ordered-subset reports, holding B fixed. Give A’s full attainable menu and explain which result in C11, beyond proposer optimality alone, supports truthfulness.

  3. (c) 4 points. Use λ=0 and λ=2 as two specified worlds. Show whether the old administrative observations distinguish them, and compare the counterfactual imposed allocation M2 in each. Question 1: What equal-price choices do not identify

  1. At equal prices, xy is uniquely maximized at x=y=m/2 by (x−y)^2≥0 with budget exhaustion. For L, a target k requires x≥k and y≥k, so cost at least 2k; the unique maximum is also (m/2,m/2).

    At (4,1), U demands (5/4,5), while L demands (2,2), since attaining k costs at least 5k.

    Both are continuous weak-order representations, but they are not ordinally equivalent: U ranks (5/4,5) above (2,2), with values 5/2>2, whereas L reverses them, 5/4<2. The two observations leave the new-price choice unidentified. This is an exact two-rationalizer witness, not a claim that either index is the true preference.

    4 points: 2 for equal price global rationalization; 2 for new demands and nonidentification witness.

  2. For m>0, a positive bundle beats any axis bundle; budget exhaustion and maximizing the strictly concave logarithm give d=(m/(2p1),m/(2p2)). At m=0 the only bundle is zero. Weighted AM–GM gives p1x+p2y≥2 sqrt(p1p2xy)≥2u sqrt(p1p2), with equality at h=(u sqrt(p2/p1),u sqrt(p1/p2)); the formula also gives zero at u=0. Thus e=2u sqrt(p1p2).

    At p=(4,1),u=5, e=20 and h=(5/2,10); baseline (1,1),m=10 delivered U=5, so additional required wealth is 10.

    The Hessian of e is S=((-5/16,5/4),(5/4,-5)). It is symmetric; z′Sz=−(5/16)(z1−4z2)^2≤0 and S(4,1)′=0. This is a verified global dual model, not a conclusion from checking a few demand signs.

    6 points: 3 for global demands and expenditure including zero; 2 for matrix and three restrictions; 1 for required wealth interpretation.

  3. The garbling matrix is G=((3/4,1/4),(1/4,3/4)); Q=PG=((5/8,3/8),(3/8,5/8)). Both signals have probability 1/2.

    Under P, posterior high probabilities after minus/plus are 1/4,3/4; under Q they are 3/8,5/8. Safe U is 2 and risky conditional expected U is four times the posterior. Therefore risky is uniquely optimal after plus and safe uniquely optimal after minus in both experiments.

    Without information both actions, and every mixture between them, tie at value 2. The informed values are V(P)=(2+3)/2=5/2 and V(Q)=(2+5/2)/2=9/4. Gross gains over no information are 1/2 and 1/4.

    4 points: 2 for garbling and posteriors; 2 for all action ties and values.

  4. For any Q-contingent randomized action rule D, the P observer can first draw the simulated Q signal using G and then use D. Statewise action distributions are PGD=QD, so every prior expected payoff is identical. Maximizing under P yields V(P)≥V(Q); a finite action maximum exists, and randomized actions cannot exceed the best pure action conditional on a signal because payoff is linear in mixing.

    A source price, a changed action menu or unavailable randomization would need a new comparison.

    Since U≥0, squaring is strictly increasing on its attained range and preserves every deterministic comparison. Yet the safe/risky expected values under U are (2,2), while under U² they are (4,8). The two lottery orders differ. Positive affine transformations preserve an expected-utility order; arbitrary increasing transformations of sure-outcome labels do not. No converse Blackwell theorem or empirical identification of risk attitudes is requested.

    6 points: 4 for statewise simulation proof and quantifiers; 2 for exact cardinal counterexample.

Question 2: Redistribute goods before prices adjust

  1. At positive income, Cobb–Douglas demand follows from interior positivity, budget exhaustion and strict concavity of the log objective. Wealths are mA=6r+t,mB=6−t. A demands (mA/(3r),2mA/3) and B demands (2mB/(3r),mB/3); at zero income the only feasible demand is zero.

    First-good demand is 2+(4−t/3)/r, so clearing supply 6 uniquely gives r=1−t/12, positive throughout [0,6]. Thus mA=6+t/2,mB=6−t; allocations are A:(2(12+t)/(12−t),4+t/3), B:(8(6−t)/(12−t),2−t/3).

    At t=6, r=1/2 and allocations are (6,6),(0,0).

    At a nonzero price vector with one free good, someone owns positive wealth in the priced good because aggregate endowments of each good are 6; that household can buy a positive quantity of the priced good and raise the free coordinate without bound. There is no utility maximum, so no zero-price equilibrium. The positive relative-price solution is therefore complete. No general convergence claim is implied.

    6 points: 3 for global demands and price family; 3 for boundary and zero price exclusion.

  2. At t=0, r=1, incomes (6,6), and allocations A:(2,4), B:(4,2).

    At t=3, r=3/4, incomes (15/2,3), and allocations A:(10/3,5), B:(8/3,1). They sum to (6,6), and expenditures at r=3/4 are 15/2 and 3.

    At a fixed price, an income change (delta,−delta) changes aggregate good-one demand by delta/(3r)−2delta/(3r)=−delta/(3r), as long as both incomes stay in their nonnegative feasible domains. Interior Engel slopes differ, so the common-slope requirement for product-domain income aggregation fails. The endogenous price adjustment is distinct from that fixed-price derivative; holding the old incomes fixed would solve a different economy.

    5 points: 3 for baseline and transfer accounts; 2 for aggregation derivative and domain.

  3. Prices are strictly positive and every household optimizes globally and exhausts its budget. Any bundle weakly preferred to its equilibrium choice must cost at least its income: at positive income, otherwise local nonsatiation produces a strictly better affordable bundle; at zero income the bound is automatic. A strictly preferred bundle costs strictly more by optimality.

    Thus a feasible Pareto improvement would cost strictly more in aggregate than the value of (6,6), contradicting feasibility and budget sums. This works at t=6 as well; zero household wealth does not invalidate the direct argument.

    At t=3, both A coordinates are larger and both B coordinates smaller than at t=0. Equivalently individual utility cubes change A:32→250/3 and B:32→64/9. A gains and B loses. Both equilibria can be efficient while the compulsory redistribution is not a Pareto improvement.

    5 points: 3 for complete expenditure contradiction; 2 for individual comparison and welfare limit.

  4. Walras law gives r*z1+z2=0 after the actual owned-budget accounts, not z1=z2=0 by itself. With r>0 and second-good price 1, clearing one market then forces the other to clear.

    Total physical resources remain (6,6), but heterogeneous wealth effects change demand: r falls from 1 to 3/4 after t=3. A valid redistribution-invariance theorem would require the common affine income slopes on the stated product domain; these demands do not have them. Existence is established here by the direct global solution, while adjustment convergence or fairness would require distinct assumptions.

    4 points: 2 for Walras identity vs vector clearing; 2 for counterexample and precise repair.

Question 3: Who supplies a public service after the move order changes?

  1. With row/column order (V,N), the matrix is (((1,1),(1,3)),((3,1),(0,0))). V always gives 1; N gives 3 times the opponent’s probability of V.

    Pure equilibria are (V,N),(N,V). If exactly one player mixed while the other were pure, the mixer would face a strict pure best reply, so that is impossible. If both mix, both indifference equations give probability of V equal to 1/3.

    These exhaust all supports. Interior expected payoffs are (1,1); service probability is 1−(2/3)^2=5/9 and total welfare is 2.

    4 points: 3 for complete support argument; 1 for probability and payoffs.

  2. A recommended volunteer knows the other was told N, so obeying gives 1 versus 0 from N. A recommended nonvolunteer knows the other was told V, so obeying gives 3 versus 1 from V. These check both actions at each positive-probability recommendation; linearity handles every randomized contingent deviation.

    Hence this is CE. Each expected payoff is 2, service is certain, exactly two input units are consumed and total welfare is 4.

    Independent marginals 1/2 are not Nash: N gives 3/2>1, so a player would not mix. Correlation and the information conveyed by the recommendation matter; identical marginals are not the same game distribution.

    4 points: 2 for all conditional obedience checks; 2 for resource accounts and independence contrast.

  3. List player 2 plans by (action after V, action after N): VV,VN,NV,NN. Row V gives payoff vectors (1,1),(1,1),(1,3),(1,3); row N gives (3,1),(0,0),(3,1),(0,0).

    If 0<p<1 both nodes are reached with positive probability, making NV the unique best plan. Then player 1 strictly chooses N, a contradiction.

    At p=0, player 2 must choose V after N but may mix arbitrarily after V; any distribution over VV,NV is permitted. Player 1 gets 3 from N and only 1 from deviating to V.

    At p=1, player 2 must choose N after V; it can mix NV with probability r and NN with probability 1−r. Player 1 sticks with V iff 1≥3r, so exactly 0≤r≤1/3 is permitted.

    These two families are the entire strategic-form Nash set.

    Sequential rationality in each proper subgame requires N after V (3>1) and V after N (1>0); player 1 then chooses N (3>1). Unique SPE is (N,NV), with outcome (N,V) and payoffs (3,1). Off-path plans, not only the realized service, distinguish the assertions.

    6 points: 1 for complete plan table; 3 for both Nash families and exhaustion; 2 for all subgames and unique SPE.

  4. Against any fully mixed first-player strategy, both histories have positive probability, and NV strictly beats every other player-2 pure plan.

    Thus any perfect limiting strategy of player 2 must put all mass on NV. Player 1 then strictly prefers N, so only (N,NV) can be perfect.

    For attainment, take 0<ε<1/3, give player 1 V probability ε, and give player 2 probabilities (ε,ε,1−3ε,ε) on VV,VN,NV,NN. Both profiles are fully mixed and converge to (N,NV). NV is uniquely best against the first-player tremble.

    Against the second-player tremble, N gives 3−6ε>1, the payoff from V. The limiting pure strategies are therefore best replies along this sequence, proving perfection.

    Equality with SPE is a fact proved for this particular game. Neither the sequential protocol nor this refinement preserves the symmetric CE payoff (2,2); changing information and move order changes burden sharing, even though both outcomes provide the service efficiently.

    6 points: 3 for necessity under all fully mixed trembles; 2 for explicit attaining sequence; 1 for scope and distributional comparison.

Question 5: A placement externality changes the incentive theorem’s domain

  1. There are seven feasible partial matchings: empty, four single pairs and two full assignments.

    Empty and every single-pair matching leave an unmatched mutually acceptable pair, so are blocked. Both M1=(AX,BY) and M2=(AY,BX) are stable: M1 gives applicants their tops, M2 institutions their tops.
