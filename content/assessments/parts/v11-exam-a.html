Question 1. A selected loan still needs funding — 20 points

An applicant is type G with probability 2/3 and type B with probability 1/3. A test passes G with probability 3/4 and B with probability 1/4. Testing costs 2/5 CU for every applicant tested. A loan advances 20 CU and promises 24 at date 2. G has 24 available for service; B has only eight, all of which is paid at date 2, with no collateral or other recovery. The unpaid contractual claim remains recorded. There is no discounting, interest on idle cash or other lending cost. The stated screening policy tests each applicant and lends only on a pass.

For the actual one-applicant ledger, the bank initially has cash six, existing loans 54 and securities 20, against deposits 60, senior borrowing ten and equity ten. These are values before the test payment. The applicant passes. Pay the test fee in cash, recognize it as expense, then originate the new 20-CU loan by crediting the borrower's deposit. The borrower instructs a transfer of twelve to a different bank. An independent investor has seven CU of available cash and has legally committed up to seven of new cash lending to this bank before that transfer. Draw only what is required. The advance is a new senior liability, repayable at date 3 without interest. There is no other cash source. Other assets and claims do not change during these entries.

  1. (a) Find the pass probability, posterior G probability, conditional receipt on a passed loan, and expected gain per tested applicant. Compare screening with lending to every applicant without testing and with rejecting everyone without testing. Explain why the conditional loan margin is not the applicant-level gain.
  2. (b) Show the bank's complete balance sheet after each of the four events: test payment, loan origination, investor advance and external transfer. Derive the smallest investor advance and verify that the investor can supply it. Distinguish deposit creation, cash financing and income.
  3. (c) After the origination ledger, the borrower spends its remaining eight-CU deposit on inputs bought from another customer of the same bank; this changes deposit ownership but neither aggregate bank deposits nor cash. The passed applicant turns out to be B, whose eight of date-2 service cash now arrives from outside this bank; no other borrower assets or recovery exist. Find its service coverage ratio and unpaid contractual amount. Question 2. More borrowing is not always the smallest capital repair — 20 points

A portfolio's terminal loss is zero, four or 24 CU with respective probabilities 11/20, 3/10 and 3/20. Use the left quantile VaRα=inf{l:P(L≤l)≥α}. Expected shortfall averages exactly the worst 1−α of probability, taking only the required part of a boundary atom.

Separately, in one specified funding stress the treasury starts with five CU of unrestricted cash. Receipts of two and ten arrive before payments of thirteen and six at cutoffs 1 and 2. The terminal loss law above does not supply an additional cash flow to this ledger. The only repairs allowed are an unrestricted outside capital contribution x≥0 arriving before cutoff 1, with no repayment within these cutoffs, and one borrowing draw b≥0 arriving before cutoff 1. The loan must be repaid at cutoff 2 at 1.1b, after that date's ten-CU receipt and alongside the six-CU payment. Cash otherwise earns zero; excess cash may be carried forward.

The treasury owns twelve collateral units. Four are already pledged and cannot be released within the horizon. The other eight can support the new loan, at a price of 0.9 CU per unit and a 25% haircut. The lender has six CU of available cash and commits up to the smaller of that amount and eligible haircut value. Pledged units remain owned by the treasury and cannot also be sold or pledged elsewhere. The given receipts exclude them. The lender releases its security after full repayment; no asset sale or further rollover is available.

  1. (a) Find VaR0.75 and ES0.75. Specify exactly how much probability is taken from each loss. Explain why averaging all outcomes at or above the quantile would answer a different question.
  2. (b) Calculate the maximum funded draw and derive both closing-cash inequalities in x and b. Account for repayment of principal as well as interest at cutoff 2.
  3. (c) Choose x and b jointly to minimize the outside capital contribution while paying every obligation on time. Prove the minimum rather than testing only one guess. Show both cash balances and compare with drawing the maximum permitted b and supplying just enough x for cutoff 1.
  4. (d) Now the collateral facility can first deliver any draw at cutoff 2. All other contractual terms and receipts remain the same. Find the minimum early contribution and exhibit an actual feasible policy. Explain which part of the previous argument changes.

Question 4. Selection and currency access change different constraints — 20 points

Insurance case. A person has terminal wealth 100 CU without a loss and 64 with a loss. Utility is the square root of terminal wealth. Half of applicants are low risk with loss probability 1/10 and half are high risk with probability 2/5; type is private information. One fully performing insurance contract eliminates the 36-CU loss and charges premium ten. The premium is paid from the person's wealth before the terminal event, with zero interest, so full cover leaves certain wealth ninety. Each person may buy at most one policy or remain uninsured; there is no other contract. Indifference permits either choice. For each issued policy the seller has separately committed liquid capital 26 plus the received premium ten, available when the claim is due. There is no premium refund, expense or capital opportunity cost. Applicants' choices do not change their loss probabilities.

Independent exporter case. H and F are different currencies. An exporter receives twelve F in an onshore account before cutoff 1. Spot is two H per F. It may convert at most eight F before the deadline and may transfer at most twelve H out of the onshore account before that deadline. It has four unrestricted offshore H and owes 24 H offshore at cutoff 1. Conversion precedes transfer; assume the exporter makes the maximum permitted conversion and outward transfer. The remaining F and H stay owned in the onshore account. There is no other facility, derivative, guarantee or permissible transfer route. The insurance seller's capital and the applicant's wealth do not belong to this exporter.

  1. (a) Derive each type's maximum premium for certain full coverage by equating utility. Identify who buys at premium ten and distinguish participation from any claim about market-entry equilibrium.
  2. (b) Calculate the seller's expected profit per issued policy under actual participation. Compare with the calculation that mistakenly uses the original equal mixture. Prove that the stipulated seller can nevertheless pay either state of every issued policy.
  3. (c) For the exporter, show converted currency, outward transfer, onshore H/F holdings, offshore usable H and the funding gap, all measured before the offshore obligation is paid. Reconcile translated assets and explain why positive translated net worth need not imply timely payment.
  4. (d) Change only spot to three H per F. Recompute the locations, total translated assets and offshore gap. Explain what changed economically and what did not become executable. Name the specific contract or operational evidence needed before adding any new route to the funding plan.

Question 5. A financed intervention still needs a loss rule — 20 points

Begin with a fictional preliminary choice screen. A bank may choose direct funding, with expected net owner payoff four, or routed funding, with payoff five. Direct funding has reported and look-through exposure eight. Routed funding has reported exposure four but look-through exposure eighteen because a contingent liquidity commitment remains with the bank. A narrow rule permits reported exposure at most ten; a broad rule uses look-through exposure at most ten. The bank chooses the highest given owner payoff among permitted routes. The payoff values are stipulated ex ante net amounts, not additional cash in the following ledger. A newly anticipated continuing rescue policy could change those incentives and is not included in this fixed menu.

Now examine one realized stress of the routed plan. Bank A has two CU cash and eight securities, each carried at one before the stress, against a six-CU obligation due at cutoff 1 and equity four. There is no other initial asset or creditor. It can sell q units, 0≤q≤8, in one uniform-price auction with p(q)=1−q/16 CU per unit. All q units receive that price, so cash proceeds are q p(q), not the integral under the price schedule. A chooses the smallest quantity that covers its remaining deadline cash need. An outside buyer has four CU cash available before the auction and purchases the announced quantity at these stipulated terms. No other private borrowing, sale or rollover is available.

Bank B owns eight different collateral units priced by the same auction quotation. They secure a debt of six with zero haircut; its lender demands extra cash collateral max{6−8p(q),0} at the same cutoff after the auction. B has only one CU of free cash. B cannot sell units, obtain another loan or delay the call in this experiment. Analyze this first-round spillover; do not invent subsequent price feedback or an equilibrium selection rule.

A proposed intervention provider has one CU of its own unrestricted cash and can advance exactly one to A before the auction. No other provider cash is available. The advance promises 1.25 at cutoff 2, with recourse limited to A's retained securities; no sold unit may also secure it. A executes the funded advance, pledge and sale together. At cutoff 2 each retained unit produces cash v, with only two specified stress values, v=1 and v=1/5. The provider takes the smaller of 1.25 and collateral cash, the owner gets any remainder, and any loan deficiency is then discharged under the stipulated limited-recourse contract. These rights are fictional assumptions, not claims of actual authority.

The provider's hard risk limit permits at most 3/20 CU loss of its original principal in either specified terminal state. There is no authority to waive this limit. A separate private party may place δ≥0 cash in a controlled first-loss escrow before the intervention. At cutoff 2 that escrow pays the provider the smaller of δ and the remaining shortfall to 1.25 after collateral. Unused escrow is refunded to its private owner. It is additional external cash, is not A's securities, and cannot also finance the initial advance. At present only an unsigned promise to provide escrow exists.

  1. (a) Determine the private choice under each exposure rule. Explain the purpose of looking through the route and why the supplied owner payoffs alone cannot establish which rule maximizes social welfare.
  2. (b) Derive the smallest feasible sale quantity and its price needed for A to pay six without the intervention and with the funded one-CU advance. Establish that the minimum is unique within the permitted quantity interval and verify the buyer's cash capacity. In each case calculate B's margin call and uncovered cash need. Identify the four units that can secure the intervention when it is used.
  3. (c) Before any private escrow, calculate provider receipts, net gain or loss relative to its initial one CU, owner residual and contractual loan shortfall in each terminal state. Distinguish loss of principal from unpaid promised interest. Do all the receipts come from identified resources?
  4. (d) Derive the smallest actual escrow that meets the hard principal-loss limit in both states, applying the stated payout/refund rule. Can the proposal be executed now under that limit? Give a concise conditional decision naming the required funding and authority evidence, accountable owner and a revision trigger. Do not turn the unsigned promise into cash or infer legal authority from the numerical result.
