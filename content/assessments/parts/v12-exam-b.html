Form B — Information units, algorithms and policy support

This original closed-book reassessment contains five 20-point domains and sixteen scored parts, for 100 points. Allow 300 minutes: approximately 60, 65, 60, 65 and 50 minutes for Questions 1–5. This allowance has not been calibrated on examinees; it does not establish statistical equivalence with Form A. Use blank paper and a nonprogrammable calculator, without notes, code or internet. Exact fractions and symbolic square roots are sufficient. The only needed probability-table value is supplied.

State the target, information unit, maintained law and limits of each conclusion. The five cases concern separate synthetic populations. Their teaching laws are stipulated for the examination, not established by estimation. Show reasoning as well as answers. A correct calculation under an inapplicable sampling or assignment law is not a valid inference.

The author-created advancement threshold is at least 85/100 overall and at least 15/20 in every domain, with no unresolved critical identification, inference or integrity error, followed by an unfamiliar transfer and teach-back. Question 1 solutions

  1. The sample means are (RA,RB)=(1,3). Four independent pairs give Cov(R)=4I₂/4=I₂. Equivalently, in the eight-reading average each cluster contributes four ordered same-channel covariance terms, each equal to 4. Their sum is 4·4·4=64; division by 8² gives variance 1. All cross-channel covariances are zero. The false iid calculation keeps only eight diagonal terms, giving 8·4/8²=1/2. Thus the true and false variance multipliers of I₂ are (1,1/2). No degrees-of-freedom correction repairs an incorrect independence assumption.

    The observable population mean identifies π. If π≠0, β=E[B]/E[A] is uniquely determined. If π=0, the entire Gaussian observable law has mean (0,0) and covariance 4I₂ whatever β; repeating readings changes none of that. The identified set for β is ℝ and for θ=β² is [0,∞). A nonzero sample RA is not proof that population π is nonzero.

    Award 2 for the true covariance and cluster unit, 1 for both observed means, 1 for the false covariance, and 2 for identification at nonzero and zero π, including θ.

  2. The average R is Gaussian with mean (π,βπ) and covariance I₂. For any fixed candidate b, RB−bRA has mean (β−b)π and variance 1+b². Under β=b, therefore,

    Z(b)=(RB−bRA)/√(1+b²) ∼ N(0,1).

    This is an exact marginal contrast law, for every π, including zero. Its denominator is known and strictly positive. It does not condition on RA, estimate a cluster variance, or appeal to a ratio's asymptotic distribution. In this realization, acceptance is (3−b)²≤4(1+b²). Moving all terms to the right gives 3b²+6b−5≥0, with coefficient triple (3,6,−5). Its ordered roots are ((−3−2√6)/3,(−3+2√6)/3). The leading coefficient is positive, so the accepted set is

    Cβ=(−∞,−1−2√6/3] ∪ [−1+2√6/3,∞).

    Both endpoints are included; the finite interval between them is rejected. At b=0, Z=3, so reject. At b=3, Z=0, so accept. At b=−4, Z=7/√17 and Z²=49/17<4, so accept. The second acceptance branch would be lost by a search restricted to positive coefficients or by automatically reporting a bounded interval.

    For each fixed data-generating (π,β), Prπ,β{β∈Cβ}=Pr{|N(0,1)|≤2}=2Φ(2)−1, approximately 95.4499736%. This exact repeated-sample coverage is not exactly 95%, and it is not a posterior probability attached to this realized set.

    Award 3 for the contrast and exact null proof for every π, 3 for correct inversion with both branches and endpoints, and 2 for all three candidate decisions and the exact coverage/scope.

  3. Write b₊=−1+2√6/3 and b₋=−1−2√6/3. Because 2√6>3, b₊>0; also |b₋|>b₊. The positive branch squares to [b₊²,∞), and the negative branch squares to [b₋²,∞), a subset. Since b₊²=1−4√6/3+24/9=(11−4√6)/3,

    Cθ=[(11−4√6)/3,∞).

    This is the full image, not merely an outer bound: every θ in it is attained by b=√θ≥b₊, and no accepted b has a smaller square. For any parameter, the event {β∈Cβ} implies {β²∈Cθ}. Hence the projected set has coverage at least 2Φ(2)−1; equality is not asserted because either sign can cover the same θ.

    At π=0 the population identified set remains [0,∞). A random confidence set may exclude some observationally indistinguishable parameter values in a realization while maintaining its parameter-by-parameter coverage over repeated samples. Confidence coverage does not supply the missing population information.

    Award 3 for the full image and its attained lower endpoint, 2 for the event-inclusion coverage proof, and 1 for separating confidence from population identification.

Question 3 solutions

  1. The uniform vector is invariant because each column of P sums to one. With that initialization and a time-homogeneous transition law, every finite block has probability π(v₀)P(v₀,v₁)⋯P(vk−1,vk), unchanged by shifting its starting time. Hence V is strictly stationary. Its mean is 1 and variance is 1. For W=V−1∈{−1,1}, E[Wt+1|Wt]=(1−2p)Wt. Iterated conditioning gives E[Wt+h|Wt]=(1−2p)hWt, so Cov(Vt,Vt+h)=(1−2p)h. Negative lag correlations are possible when p>1/2; the given domain excludes the absorbing or deterministic endpoint chains.

    Independence from the random walk gives Var(Xt)=t and Var(Yt−aXt)=(1−a)²t+1. Thus a=1 is the only normalized coefficient with time-invariant finite variance, and then the difference is the stationary V itself. Both X and Y have variances growing with t, so stationary spread behavior does not make their levels stationary.

    All p in (0,1) give stationary mean 1, so that moment does not identify p. The turnover probability equals p in either current state, hence also unconditionally. The supplied population moment therefore identifies p=1/4. At this value, (E[V],Var(V),lag-one correlation) is (1,1,1/2). These are deductions from the given law, not statistical unit-root or cointegration test results.

    Award 3 for stationarity and the unique normalized stationary difference, 2 for mean, variance and lag covariance with conditioning, and 2 for distinguishing uninformative mean and identifying turnover.

  2. Measure Jt(v) in date-t currency units. At the final date J₂(v)=max{0,v−1}. For t=1,0, the agent may abstain, exercise now, or continue, so

    Jt(v)=max{0,v−1,(1/2)Σw∈{0,2}P(v,w)Jt+1(w)}.

    At any history reaching state v, the Markov assumption fixes that same future conditional transition law. Backward induction bounds every future history-dependent feasible strategy by Jt+1, so its continuation payoff is at most the third term. Immediate decisions satisfy the other bounds. Choosing a maximizing action now and the induction's maximizing future actions attains the bound. This proves the recursion is optimal over the permitted policy class; it does not assume that history-independent policies were the only competitors.

    With p=1/4, J₂=(0,1). At date 1 a low state has continuation value (1/2)(1/4)=1/8; a high state can exercise for 1, exceeding continuation (1/2)(3/4)=3/8. At date 0 the low continuation is (1/2)[(3/4)(1/8)+(1/4)1]=11/64. The high continuation is (1/2)[(1/4)(1/8)+(3/4)1]=25/64<1. Thus rows J₀,J₁,J₂, each in state order (0,2), are ((11/64,1),(1/8,1),(0,1)). Exercise at the high state; wait at the low state before the terminal date; abstain at the low terminal state.

    Starting low, the (V₁,V₂) paths (0,0),(0,2),(2,0),(2,2) have probabilities (9/16,3/16,1/16,3/16). The optimal discounted payoff is respectively 0, 1/4, 1/2 and 1/2. The expectation is 3/64+1/32+3/32=11/64, confirming the recursion. If the only allowed exercise date is 2, high-state probability is 3/16+3/16=3/8 and the value is (1/4)(3/8)=3/32. The optimal and terminal-only starting values are (11/64,3/32). The comparison keeps the transition law fixed and changes only the opportunity set.

    Award 2 for terminal condition and conditional recursion, 3 for all state-date values, 1 for the policy, and 2 for the history-dependent optimality argument and both starting-value calculations, including path confirmation.

  3. The first two nodes flip the initial low state at date 1, so the agent exercises then; their second coordinates cannot change the received payoff. The third node stays low at date 1 and flips high at date 2. The fourth stays low throughout. The discounted payoffs are (1/2,1/2,1/4,0). Their average is 5/16. Relative to the exact 11/64 expectation, the signed error is 5/16−11/64=9/64; the average and error pair is (5/16,9/64).

    These nodes assign equal weight to four coordinates, not the physical path probabilities above. Repeating each node any equal number of times leaves the same arithmetic average and error. An iid Monte Carlo variance formula requires a specified random draw law and independent repetitions; neither exists for these fixed nodes. The error is a known deterministic integration error in this finite example, not evidence of a wrong structural transition law or an estimated sampling confidence interval.

    Award 2 for all path payoffs, 1 for the average and signed error, and 2 for the repeated-node argument and absence of an iid precision claim.

Question 4 solutions

  1. For the ordered record the likelihood is θ²(1−θ), without a binomial-count factor. At the two prior support points its values are (3/64,9/64). On the separate continuous space, ℓ(θ)=2 log θ+log(1−θ), ℓ′=2/θ−1/(1−θ), and ℓ″=−2/θ²−1/(1−θ)²<0. The score vanishes at θ̂=2/3. Strict concavity and ℓ→−∞ at both boundaries prove the unique global maximum. The Bayesian support does not contain this continuous MLE; it solves a different optimization problem.

    Multiplying the likelihoods by the equal prior and normalizing gives posterior probabilities (1/4,3/4). A new conditionally iid Bernoulli success has predictive probability E[θ|data]=(1/4)(1/4)+(3/4)(3/4)=5/8. Expected launch utility is (1/4)(−4)+(3/4)(1)=−1/4, so decline for utility zero. The high parameter's posterior probability is larger, but its positive launch payoff does not compensate for the low parameter's loss. A decision requires the loss or utility function, not merely the most probable state.

    Award 1 for both likelihoods, 1 for the continuous MLE with global proof, 2 for posterior and prediction, and 2 for the utility calculation and decision interpretation.

  2. Let L and H denote the low and high parameter values. Symmetric proposals give acceptance probability min{1,π(new)/π(old)}. Thus L→H is accepted with probability 1, whereas H→L is accepted with probability 1/3. In L,H order, the retained matrix is ((0,1),(1/3,2/3)). The cross-edge probability flow is πLPLH=1/4=πHPHL; the diagonal balance is automatic. Summing these balanced flows gives πP=π, proving invariance.

    Deleting rejected repeats removes precisely the waiting time at H. Each accepted switch then alternates L,H,L,H,… (or the reverse), so empirical frequencies approach 1/2,1/2 and the empirical parameter mean approaches 1/2. This differs from the posterior mean 5/8. Both states are visited indefinitely almost surely: at H the probability of rejecting forever is limk→∞(2/3)k=0, and L always leaves immediately. For any finite prefix the imbalance in the jump counts is at most one.

    The jump sequence has period two. From a fixed starting state its marginal distribution alternates rather than converging to the uniform law, even though its empirical average converges. Invariance of a distribution, convergence of marginal distributions and convergence of empirical averages are different statements. Retained rejected states are part of the algorithm defining the desired posterior occupation frequencies.

    Award 2 for the acceptance probabilities and matrix, 1 for detailed balance/invariance, and 2 for the jump average, wrong target and periodicity distinction.

  3. Stationarity gives Pr(H₀=1)=3/4 and Var(H₀)=(3/4)(1/4)=3/16. Also E[H₀H₁]=Pr(H₀=H₁=1)=(3/4)(2/3)=1/2, so Cov(H₀,H₁)=1/2−(3/4)²=−1/16. Consequently Var((H₀+H₁)/2)=[2(3/16)+2(−1/16)]/4=1/16. The false iid variance is (3/16)/2=3/32. In the order single-state variance, lag covariance, correct mean variance, false iid mean variance, the results are (3/16,−1/16,1/16,3/32).

    The iid formula overstates variance here because this particular lag covariance is negative. This is an exact calculation for two retained states with the granted stationary start. It supplies neither a universal sign for MCMC dependence nor a long-chain central limit theorem, and it does not describe an arbitrary nonstationary initialization.
