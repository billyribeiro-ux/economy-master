Volume 12 assessment — advanced econometrics and causal inference

Identify the question, derive the estimator, and defend the evidence.

This package joins a prerequisite diagnostic, two graduate written forms, an open-resource replication-and-extension project, an oral defense and a delayed retention assessment. The numerical populations and research cases are original teaching constructions. Their stipulated laws make exact answers possible; an empirical application must supply evidence for those laws and assumptions. Complete the diagnostic before the volume and the remaining work after studying all twelve chapters. Keep the attempted work, corrections, code, data and grading record together.

Prerequisite diagnostic

Suggested conditions: 60 minutes, basic calculator, no notes or code. The eight questions carry five points each. Readiness requires 34/40 overall and at least four points on questions 1, 2 and 6. These are authored study criteria and an uncalibrated time allowance. The questions use algebra, optimization, probability and empirical reasoning from Volumes 2–5. A missed advanced term is not the issue: locate the precise missing prerequisite and repair it before proceeding.

  1. Separate a treatment effect from selection. In a stipulated population, X is zero or one with equal probability. Treatment D is binary, with P(D=1|X=0)=1/4 and P(D=1|X=1)=3/4. Potential outcomes are Y(d)=2X+3d, and the recorded outcome is Y=Y(D). Find P(D=1), E[X|D=1], E[X|D=0], the observed treated-minus-untreated mean difference and the population average treatment effect. Explain the difference between the last two answers.
  2. Identify what repeats independently. C, E₁ and E₂ are mutually independent fair signs, each taking −1 and 1 with equal probability. Two measurements from one unit are Y₁=5+C+E₁ and Y₂=5+C+E₂. Compute each variance, their covariance and the variance of their mean. What answer would incorrectly treating the two measurements as independent give? Distinct units have independent copies of the entire triple.
  3. Reconstruct least squares. Fit y=a+bx by ordinary least squares to (x,y)=(0,1),(1,2),(2,6). Compute a, b, the three residuals and their squared sum. Verify both normal equations. Does their satisfaction establish a causal effect of x?
  4. Prove the optimum. For b∈ℝ minimize q(b)=(b−2)²+2(b+1)². Derive the first and second derivatives, the minimizer and its objective value. Explain why solving the first-order equation is sufficient in this particular problem, and why a minimized discrepancy need not be zero.
  5. State the approximation. Independent identically distributed nonnegative observations have mean four and variance nine. Let Mₙ be their sample mean, and define Tₙ=1/Mₙ when Mₙ>0, with Tₙ=0 when Mₙ=0. State the probability limit of Tₙ and derive its root-n limiting variance from the central limit theorem and a first-order expansion. Give the population-based asymptotic standard error at n=100. Is this an exact finite-sample normal law?
  6. Account for the missing observation. Four fixed units have outcomes (3,5,missing,7). The missing outcome is known only to lie in [0,10]. Find the sharp set of possible four-unit means and the complete-case mean. Give endpoint completions. Does deleting the blank prove that the complete-case mean equals the full mean?
  7. Write the likelihood before optimizing. Eight independent Bernoulli observations contain five successes. For 0≤p≤1, write the likelihood up to a positive factor independent of p, derive the maximizing p and justify the boundary comparison. Does a fitted success probability alone identify a treatment effect?
  8. Count the reference events. Four fixed values are (1,3,5,7). A lottery selects exactly two labels, with all six subsets equally likely. For each selected pair compute selected mean minus unselected mean. If the observed selected pair is {3,4}, what fraction of lottery outcomes have an absolute difference at least as large as observed? State what is held fixed and what varies.
Diagnostic solutions and scoring
  1. The treatment probability is (1/2)(1/4)+(1/2)(3/4)=1/2. Bayes' rule gives E[X|D=1]=3/4 and E[X|D=0]=1/4. Therefore E[Y|D=1]=9/2 and E[Y|D=0]=1/2: the observed difference is 4. Every unit has Y(1)−Y(0)=3. The additional one is the difference in baseline outcomes between selected groups, 2(3/4−1/4). Credit: treatment probability 1; both conditional means 1; observed outcome difference 1; effect 1; selection explanation 1. Review conditioning and causal reasoning.
  2. Var(Y₁)=Var(Y₂)=2 and Cov(Y₁,Y₂)=Var(C)=1. Thus Var((Y₁+Y₂)/2)=(2+2+2)/4=3/2. The false independence calculation gives (2+2)/4=1. Averaging removes part of the individual noise but retains the common C. If independent units repeat, the unit mean is the independent observation; the two rows within each unit are dependent. Credit: marginal variances 1; covariance 1; correct mean variance 1; false calculation 1; repeated-unit interpretation 1. Review expectation and covariance.
  3. The means are x̄=1 and ȳ=3. The centered cross-product is five and the centered x sum of squares is two, so b=5/2 and a=1/2. Residuals are (1/2,−1,1/2), with squared sum 3/2. Their sum is zero and their x-weighted sum is −1+2(1/2)=0. These are projection identities for these observations; causality needs additional identification assumptions. Credit: slope 1; intercept 1; residuals and squared sum 1; both normal equations 1; causal boundary 1. Review projection geometry and regression.
  4. q′(b)=2(b−2)+4(b+1)=6b and q″(b)=6>0. The unique global minimizer is 0, with q(0)=6. Equivalently q(b)=3b²+6 proves the claim directly. The two desired equalities b=2 and b=−1 cannot hold together, so the best compromise has a positive discrepancy. A stationary point would need additional analysis without the positive curvature or equivalent global argument. Credit: first derivative 1; second derivative 1; minimizer 1; loss 1; sufficiency and incompatible targets 1. Review derivatives and optimization.
  5. The law of large numbers gives Mₙ→4 in probability, so Tₙ→1/4. Near four, 1/Mₙ−1/4=−(Mₙ−4)/16+oₚ(n−1/2). Consequently √n(Tₙ−1/4) converges in distribution to a centered normal with variance 9/256. At n=100 the asymptotic standard error is 3/(16√100)=3/160=0.01875. The zero extension does not change the limit because P(Mₙ=0)→0. This is a large-sample approximation, not an exact normal statement at 100. Credit: probability limit 1; derivative and expansion 1; limiting variance 1; standard error 1; approximation and extension scope 1. Review limit theorems and linear approximation.
  6. Writing the missing value as m, the mean is (15+m)/4. Its sharp set is [15/4,25/4]; m=0 and m=10 attain the endpoints, and every intermediate value is allowed. The complete-case mean is 5. It equals the full mean only when m=5. Deleting the blank supplies no such fact. Credit: mean formula 1; both bounds 1; attainability 1; complete-case value 1; missingness interpretation 1. Review tables and missing values.
  7. The likelihood is proportional to p⁵(1−p)³. For 0<p<1, the log-likelihood derivative is 5/p−3/(1−p), which vanishes at 5/8. Its second derivative is −5/p²−3/(1−p)²<0. The likelihood is zero at both boundaries and positive at the interior solution, proving the unique maximum. The model describes the outcome distribution; no treatment, counterfactual or assignment restriction has been supplied. Credit: likelihood 1; derivative 1; maximizer 1; curvature and boundaries 1; causal interpretation 1. Review estimation.
  8. In pair order {1,2},{1,3},{1,4},{2,3},{2,4},{3,4}, the differences are −4,−2,0,0,2,4. The observed difference is four; two of six absolute differences are at least four, giving 1/3. All four values stay fixed and the selected labels vary under the stated lottery. A different assignment law would need different weights; a different scientific null might not justify holding these values fixed. Credit: six differences 2; observed value 1; inclusive tail fraction 1; fixed-versus-random contract 1. Review finite probability and experimental reasoning.

Administration and essential domains

Each written form contains five 20-point cases, totaling 100 points. Suggested conditions are 300 minutes, a nonprogrammable calculator and blank paper, without notes, internet or code. Exact fractions, symbolic logarithms and square roots are acceptable; no external probability table is required unless supplied in the question. A possible allocation is 55 minutes per case and 25 minutes for reading and checking. Timing has not been calibrated on students. Form B reassesses the same domains with different structures; the forms have not been statistically equated.

D1. Identification and inference1–5Specify the population target; derive an estimator or influence expansion; establish relevance, rank and dependence conditions; distinguish likelihood, moments and causal restrictions.
D2. Causal design6–8State the assignment or counterfactual comparison; address timing, compliance, interference and support; derive the design-specific target and explain its limits.
D3. Dynamic and structural analysis9–10Respect persistence and forecast dependence; connect observable moments to primitive parameters; identify the invariant assumptions needed for a policy counterfactual.
D4. Modern methods11Distinguish posterior learning, prediction and causal estimation; analyze regularization, nuisance estimation, sample splitting or multiple testing under the stated law.
D5. Research audit12, integrating 1–11Reproduce the actual analysis, distinguish search from prespecification, track information and access, and defend or bound external validity.

A written pass requires 85/100 overall and at least 15/20 in every domain. Also complete the separately scored project, a fresh transfer and the oral defense. These are this library's criteria. A single form samples capabilities rather than examining every theorem; consult the form-specific coverage maps, chapter exercises and project requirements for the remaining evidence.

Critical errors require remediation regardless of the aggregate score. Examples include substituting association for an identified causal effect, using a denominator that has not been shown nonzero, treating dependent rows as independent units, invoking a regular limit at a weak or boundary parameter, confusing a sharp null with an average-effect null, conditioning a resampling distribution on an outcome-driven choice that should be repeated, assuming that good fit identifies a policy mechanism, hiding missing or inaccessible inputs, or relabeling an assumption bound as a confidence interval.

Use each subpart's explicit allocation. Award justified derivation and follow-through credit for a carried arithmetic slip without deducting repeatedly for the same mistake; separately evaluate whether the learner recognizes its substantive consequence. A claim that a method fails needs an argument or counterexample. Failure to meet a sufficient theorem condition means that the theorem is unavailable, which alone does not prove the conclusion false. For open judgments, grade the target, assumptions, evidence, alternatives and revision conditions. Keep domain scores and unresolved critical errors visible.
