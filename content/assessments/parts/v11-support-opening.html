Volume 11 assessment — banking, risk and international finance

Reconcile claims, fund the deadline and defend the decision.

This package tests the twelve chapters through a prerequisite diagnostic, two written examinations, an integrated institution project, oral transfer and delayed retention. All numerical institutions, rights and events are original teaching cases. Their exact answers follow from the stated contracts. A successful calculation does not establish that funding, collateral rights, legal powers or behavioral assumptions hold at an actual institution. Complete the diagnostic before the volume; use the examinations and project after studying all twelve chapters.

Prerequisite diagnostic

Allow 45 minutes with a basic calculator and no notes. Eight questions carry five points each. Readiness requires at least 34/40, with at least four points on each of questions 1, 5 and 7. These are authored study criteria, not empirically calibrated admission standards. The diagnostic tests accounting, conditional probability, valuation, cash timing and strategic reasoning taught in preceding volumes; it does not require the new bank-specific models. Attempt every question before opening the answers.

  1. Conserve the balance sheet. An institution has cash 12 CU, loans 58 and securities 30, financed by deposits 70, senior debt 20 and equity 10. Customers withdraw eight in cash and cancel eight of deposits. Then a new outside lender transfers five in cash for a new senior claim of five. Show the closing assets, liabilities and equity. Which transaction creates income?
  2. Condition before lending. An applicant is of type G with probability 3/4 and type B with probability 1/4. A test passes G with probability 4/5 and B with probability 1/5. A 100-CU loan returns 112 from G and 52 from B at the same future date; there is no discounting or other cost except a two-CU test cost paid for every applicant. The lender can lend only after a pass and reject after a fail. Find the pass probability, posterior G probability, expected receipt conditional on pass and expected net gain per tested applicant. Compare with lending without testing and with rejecting without testing.
  3. Value the dates. A certain claim pays ten CU at date 1 and 110 at date 2. The respective prices of one certain dated CU are 0.95 and 0.90. Find the claim's value and the value after the first payment is delayed to date 2. State the contractual and trading assumptions needed to add these prices. Does the higher original value itself deliver cash before either payment date?
  4. Read a discrete loss law. A loss is zero with probability 0.80, ten with probability 0.15 and fifty with probability 0.05. Find its expectation, the probability of a loss strictly greater than ten, and its 95% quantile, defined as the smallest loss l for which P(L≤l)≥0.95. Explain why a 95% quantile is not the maximum possible loss.
  5. Respect the prefix. Starting cash is five CU. At cutoff 1, four arrives before ten is due. At cutoff 2, eight arrives before six is due. No borrowing, sale or payment delay is otherwise allowed. What single unrestricted contribution received before cutoff 1 is minimally sufficient to pay both obligations? Show both closing cash balances with that contribution. Could an identical contribution first received at cutoff 2 repair the missed first deadline?
  6. Keep two probability questions separate. There are two terminal states. A claim pays four CU in the first and zero in the second. Physical state probabilities are (3/4,1/4). Positive state prices are (1/4,3/4), so one certain terminal CU costs one today. Compute the physical expected payoff and the no-arbitrage value under those state prices. Explain why the numbers differ and what would be needed to treat either probability vector as an empirical frequency estimate.
  7. Locate the currency. H and F are distinct currencies; the spot quotation is 1.5 H per F. An institution receives twenty F in an onshore account before a payment deadline. All twenty F can be converted at that spot, but only twelve H can leave that account before the deadline. It also has three unrestricted offshore H and owes twenty H offshore. Calculate total translated assets, usable offshore cash, the payment gap and the assets left onshore after the permitted conversion and transfer. No other facility exists.
  8. Solve a game without calling it a forecast. Two players choose S or R simultaneously. Their payoffs are (4,4) at SS, (1,6) at SR, (6,1) at RS and (2,2) at RR, with the first component belonging to the first player. Find each player's best response to each action and the Nash equilibrium. Compare total payoffs at SS and RR. Does a mathematical equilibrium prove that an observed institution will behave this way?
Diagnostic solutions and five-point scoring
  1. After withdrawal, cash is four, loans 58 and securities 30: assets 92, deposits 62, debt 20 and equity ten. The outside loan then raises cash to nine and debt to 25. Closing assets are 97; liabilities are 87; equity remains ten. Withdrawal reduces cash and the matching deposit claim. Borrowing increases cash and a matching debt; neither creates income. Credit: first entry 1; second entry 1; closing assets/liabilities 1; equity 1; income distinction 1. Review accounting identities and financial statements.
  2. P(pass)=(3/4)(4/5)+(1/4)(1/5)=13/20. P(G|pass)=(3/5)/(13/20)=12/13. The conditional receipt is (12/13)112+(1/13)52=1396/13≈107.384615 CU. Expected gain per tested applicant is (13/20)(1396/13−100)−2=14/5=2.8 CU; it is not the conditional loan margin 96/13. Without the test, the loan's expected receipt is 97 and net gain is −3; rejection gives zero. Thus the stated screening policy improves expected gain relative to both alternatives, while retaining default risk. Credit: pass probability 1; posterior 1; conditional receipt 1; applicant-level test cost and gain 1; alternatives and interpretation 1. Review conditional probability and decisions under uncertainty.
  3. The original value is 10(0.95)+110(0.90)=108.5 CU. Delay gives 120(0.90)=108 CU, a loss of 0.5. Additive pricing requires matching currency, credit, payment and settlement rights and feasible purchase of the component claims. A value is not immediately spendable cash: a sale or eligible borrowing would need its own executable terms. Credit: original value 1; delayed value 1; difference 1; matching rights 1; cash distinction 1. Review dated investment cash flows and no-arbitrage pricing.
  4. Expected loss is 0.15(10)+0.05(50)=four CU. P(L>10)=0.05. The cumulative probability at zero is 0.80 and at ten is 0.95, so the 95% quantile is ten CU. The fifty-CU loss remains possible; the quantile describes a cumulative probability threshold, not a support bound. Credit: expectation 1; strict-tail probability 1; quantile and its convention 2; interpretation 1. Review distributions and expectation.
  5. Without contribution, the full-payment cash projections are 5+4−10=−1 and −1+8−6=1. A contribution x before the first cutoff must satisfy x≥1 and x≥−1, as well as x≥0. The minimum is therefore one CU. Actual closing cash is zero at cutoff 1 and two at cutoff 2. A contribution first received at cutoff 2 cannot meet the earlier obligation under the stated no-delay contract. The negative projection diagnoses failure; it is not permission to execute a negative cash balance. Credit: two prefix requirements 2; minimum 1; actual balances 1; timing interpretation 1. Review short-term financing and funded contracts.
  6. The physical expected payoff is three CU; its price is (1/4)4+(3/4)0=one CU. State prices price dated state claims under the stated trading model; physical probabilities concern the event distribution. Risk compensation can separate the two. Neither a supplied teaching probability nor a pricing weight is automatically an empirical estimate; that requires a specified population, data, estimation method and a defensible stability/information argument. Credit: expectation 1; price 1; roles of the two laws 2; empirical qualification 1. Review state prices and finite replication.
  7. The receipt translates to thirty H; including offshore cash, assets total 33 H. Only twelve H can be transferred, leaving 18 H onshore. Offshore cash is 15 H, and the payment gap is five H. Positive translated net worth of thirteen H does not remove the transfer restriction. No unmentioned facility or later release is allowed to close the gap. Credit: translation 1; permitted transfer and onshore stock 1; usable cash 1; gap 1; location-versus-value explanation 1. Review external accounts and currencies and contractual settlement.
  8. Against S, R gives six rather than four. Against R, R gives two rather than one. The same comparisons apply to both players, so R is strictly dominant and RR is the unique Nash equilibrium. Total payoffs are eight at SS and four at RR; SS is better for each player but is not stable to unilateral deviation. Actual use requires evidence for the action set, information, enforceable payoffs and behavior. The calculation supplies neither frequencies nor a universal institutional diagnosis. Credit: best responses 2; equilibrium 1; payoff comparison 1; evidence boundary 1. Review strategic interaction.

Administration and essential domains

Each written form contains five 20-point questions totaling 100 points. Suggested conditions are 240 minutes, a nonprogrammable calculator and blank paper, without notes, internet or code. Every necessary case parameter is supplied. The timing is an authored design target, not a result of student trials. Take Form A first; use Form B after documented remediation. The forms test the same domains through different situations and have not been statistically equated.

DomainChapter coverageRequired evidence in each form
D1. Intermediation and credit1–3Conditional decisions and incentives, conserved balance sheets, and a borrower repayment source with actual funding.
D2. Market loss and funding4–5A correctly specified finite risk law and cash that is available to the right entity before the deadline.
D3. Controls and joint stress6–7Process or model evidence, common failures, state-consistent loss aggregation and an actionable boundary.
D4. Contingent and international claims8–9Who bears insurance, pension or household risk, and which currency, account and counterparty actually supplies payment.
D5. Systemic decisions and governance10–12Changed incentives, creditor and public-resource accounting, crisis transmission, authority and accountable escalation.

A written pass requires at least 85/100 and 15/20 in every domain. Also complete the separately scored project and oral transfer. These are the library's criteria, not a university, regulator or professional body's requirements. The project is open-resource work: its purpose is reproducible reasoning and a defensible decision, not memorization.

Critical errors require remediation even when the numerical score passes. Examples include creating cash through a valuation or debt conversion, paying an early obligation with a late or blocked asset, counting a pledged asset twice, deleting unpaid claims, adding the same network impairment at both ends, assuming independent controls without evidence, substituting pricing weights for physical probabilities, selecting a favorable equilibrium without justification, or treating international guidance as an actual domestic power. Correct the original reasoning, explain the cause and pass a fresh transfer before advancing.

Use the explicit subpart allocations. Award justified method and follow-through credit after a carried arithmetic slip; do not repeatedly deduct for one error. Independently assess whether the learner recognizes its financial consequence. Preserve enough precision to distinguish a strict inequality from a tie and an executable payment from a shortfall. Open recommendations can differ: score the evidence, assumptions, resources, authority, alternatives and revision conditions. Record domain scores, critical errors and the remediation completed.
