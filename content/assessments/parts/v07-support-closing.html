Oral transfer and remediation

Allow 20 minutes after the written and project work. Explain each prompt for about three minutes, then respond to a follow-up that changes an assumption. A satisfactory answer identifies the entity and timing, uses a balanced argument, names the evidence needed and explains the changed decision without hiding uncertainty. Record the explanation before inspecting these benchmarks; a reviewer should ask for a fresh numerical instance rather than memorized wording.

  1. Profit without cash. A profitable supplier runs out of cash while customers and inventory grow. Explain a coherent mechanism and the records you would request. Benchmark: separate recognized sales from collections, cost of sales from purchases and suppliers' payment dates, and long-lived investment from depreciation. Reconcile opening to closing balances and request receivables aging, invoices, stock records, supplier terms and a dated payment calendar. Growth can consume cash without an accounting error; unsupported receivables or inventory valuation are distinct hypotheses. Follow-up: customer advances arrive before delivery—explain why cash can rise before revenue.
  2. Balanced but wrong. A reviewer says that assets equal liabilities plus equity, so the statements must be reliable. Respond with a complete counterexample. Benchmark: capitalizing a routine expense can raise both an asset and equity through profit while preserving the identity; if tax treatment differs, the tax bridge must also be included. Balance is necessary for a closed ledger but does not establish recognition or measurement. Request the underlying work description and capitalization policy. Follow-up: distinguish a supported change in useful-life estimate from correcting a prior error.
  3. Financing and ownership. A forecast uses whatever new equity is needed to reach target cash. Explain when that is a scenario and when it is supported financing. Benchmark: name the investor, subscription terms, approval and completion dates, cash proceeds and resulting share count; until available, label the transaction conditional and report the residual gap. Debt also needs a limit, interest timing and covenant test. Follow-up: issuing shares at the opening rather than the closing changes weighted shares for earnings per share; an ownership percentage decline alone does not establish value destruction.
  4. Evidence before accusation. Cash conversion deteriorates and management removes a recurring cost from adjusted profit. Give an analytical response. Benchmark: reproduce the adjustment and statutory cash bridge, apply a symmetric policy to gains and costs, compare consistent peers and dates, and identify alternative business explanations. Request targeted corroboration and escalate documented inconsistencies through appropriate governance. A red flag is an investigation trigger, not a conclusion about intent. Follow-up: a missing customer response is missing evidence; obtain other reliable evidence rather than treating silence as confirmation.

For a missed written domain, return to its chapter routes in the coverage table, solve a new problem with changed transaction timing or constraints, and teach back the failed distinction. For a failed reconciliation, list every opening stock and movement before recalculating ratios. For a source mistake, record the applicable framework, jurisdiction, period and adoption status, then separate the cited rule from the analyst's adjustment. Reattempt the other form after practice; repeating memorized solutions is not transfer.

Delayed retention assessment

After two to four weeks, allow 60 minutes without notes, using a basic calculator. Six ten-point questions give 60 points. Aim for at least 51/60 and at least 7.5/10 on each item; correct every material balancing, financing or evidence error. Revisit failed items with fresh values a week later. All amounts below are in thousands of currency units unless a unit cost is explicitly given. Ignore tax unless specified. Cases are independent.

  1. A company opens with cash 20, receivables 30, inventory 20 and net PPE 50; payables are 25, debt 35 and equity 60. Credit sales are 120, collections 110, cost of sales 70, credit purchases 75, supplier payments 60, other cash operating costs 20 and depreciation 10. There are no other transactions. Calculate profit, direct and indirect operating cash flow and every closing balance.
  2. At a year-start review, an asset's carrying amount is 60; the revised remaining life is three years and residual value six. Apply the stipulated prospective straight-line method. Separately, a note opens at carrying amount 100, pays a six-unit coupon at each year-end and has an effective annual interest rate of 8%. Find the next two years' interest expenses and closing carrying amounts. No principal is repaid in these years.
  3. A parent owns 70% of a subsidiary throughout the year. Parent standalone profit is 60, including 14 dividend income from the subsidiary; subsidiary profit is 40. The parent's downstream inventory sale leaves six of unrealized profit in the subsidiary's closing inventory. Ignore tax and other adjustments; find group profit and its attribution. Separately, a newly recorded provision of 12 is deductible only on payment; the case permits full recognition of a deferred tax asset at 25%. Find its initial tax asset, after-tax profit effect and cash effect.
  4. Making 500 units requires avoidable cash cost of six per unit. Buying costs eight per unit. Allocated fixed overhead of three per unit continues under either option. Buying also frees capacity that can earn additional contribution of 1,800 currency units. Compare make and buy. Separately, annual EBIT is 30, the stipulated operating tax rate is 20% and average invested capital is 150; find ROIC under the NOPAT/average-capital convention.
  5. A forecast's cash after all operations, opening-debt interest, investment and planned distributions but before any change in the revolver is −5. Opening revolver debt is 10; target closing cash is 15 and the closing debt limit is 25. New borrowing occurs at the year-end and creates no current interest. Find required closing debt, cash under maximum permitted borrowing and the gap to target. A cancellable distribution of six is then removed; solve again. Separately, holding everything else fixed, what does an increase in closing receivables from 60 to 75 do to cash flow and accrual profit?
  6. Inventory originally cost 50 and was written down to 40. New evidence puts net realizable value at 46. Policy R permits reversal only up to original cost; Policy N prohibits reversing this write-down. Apply both stipulated policies, state the pretax profit difference and identify which facts a real-company comparison would have to verify. A customer does not reply to a receivable confirmation: explain what this establishes and give a useful next evidence step.
Retention answers and scoring
  1. Profit is 120 − 70 − 20 − 10 = 20. Direct CFO = 110 − 60 − 20 = 30. Closing balances are cash 50, receivables 40, inventory 25, net PPE 40, payables 40, debt 35 and equity 80. Thus assets 155 equal liabilities 75 plus equity 80. Indirect CFO = 20 + 10 − (40 − 30) − (25 − 20) + (40 − 25) = 30. No capital expenditure or financing cash movement exists here. Scoring: profit two; direct CFO two; all closing balances and equality three; indirect bridge three.
  2. Prospective depreciation is (60 − 6)/3 = 18 per year, giving a first-year closing carrying amount of 42. No past depreciation is rewritten merely because this supported estimate changed. Note interest is eight in Year 1; closing carrying amount is 100 + 8 − 6 = 102. Year 2 interest is 8.16 and close is 102 + 8.16 − 6 = 104.16. Coupon cash and effective interest expense differ. Scoring: asset amount and prospective interpretation four; both debt rollforwards four; cash/expense distinction two.
  3. Group profit is 60 − 14 + 40 − 6 = 80. Because the eliminated profit was earned by the parent on a downstream sale, NCI receives 30% of subsidiary profit = 12; parent attribution is 68. Eliminate dividend income to avoid counting the subsidiary's earnings twice. The separate provision creates a deferred tax asset of three and net profit reduction of nine; provision and deferred-tax recognition themselves have no immediate cash effect. Future settlement and tax benefit require their own entries. Scoring: group profit three; attribution and direction three; deferred tax, net profit and cash four.
  4. Buying costs 1,000 more in direct cash cost but releases capacity earning 1,800, so buying improves contribution by 800 currency units, or 0.8 thousand. The continuing 1,500 allocated overhead is not avoided and cancels from the comparison. This assumes the alternative contribution is feasible and not already counted elsewhere. NOPAT is 30(1 − 0.20) = 24; ROIC is 24/150 = 16%. Debt interest is excluded from this stated operating return. Scoring: incremental decision and units four; overhead and feasibility two; ROIC and matching four.
  5. Required new borrowing is 20, so closing debt would be 30. The limit permits only 15 of additional borrowing, leaving cash of ten and a five-unit gap to the target. Removing the six distribution makes prefinancing cash one; the required draw is 14 and closing debt 24, with cash 15. This passes the stated year-end limit; no monthly feasibility claim follows. The separate receivables change reduces CFO by 15 without changing accrual profit under the stipulated unchanged recognition and loss estimates. Scoring: original funding/limit calculation four; revised solution and timing four; receivables bridge two.
  6. Policy R carries inventory at min(50,46) = 46 and recognizes a reversal gain of six; Policy N leaves it at 40. R's pretax profit is six greater, with no immediate cash change from the measurement alone. Verify the entity's applicable framework and jurisdiction, inventory category, reporting period, adopted rules and evidence for recoverability before mapping this conditional example to reported numbers. Customer silence does not confirm the receivable or prove it fictitious. Inspect subsequent receipts matched to the exact invoice and obtain reliable delivery, contractual and customer records; consider whether those records support existence, cutoff and collectibility. Scoring: amounts and cash three; applicability three; evidence limitation and targeted follow-up four.

From recorded performance to investment value

This volume establishes a disciplined route from events to statements and from statements to conditional decisions. Recognition and measurement determine what profit means. Working-capital and asset schedules explain why that profit differs from cash. Ownership, tax and financing determine which claims and constraints surround the business. Cost analysis and ratios become useful only after those definitions and boundaries are explicit. A complete reconciliation tests internal consistency; corroborating evidence tests whether the recorded events and measurement choices are supported.

Volume 8 begins capital budgeting and valuation. Accounting earnings supply inputs, not an automatic measure of investment value. The next volume constructs incremental future cash flows, charges opportunity costs, matches risk and discounting, and tests financing and terminal assumptions. Keep the accounting bridges: a valuation can fail because its operating story is implausible, because its cash forecast is inconsistent, or because its discounting is unsuitable. Each requires a different repair. Finance-core assessment follows completion of Volumes 7–11; that later package and the still-planned volumes must not be confused with material already written.
