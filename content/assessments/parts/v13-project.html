Two collaborators want to resume productive work quickly after a warning. The warning is public, but work is hidden and the report is noisy. Faster restoration reduces innocent interruptions; it also reduces the loss from shirking. Your research question is: how quickly can this particular relationship be restored while preserving incentives after every history, and what changes if repair itself requires effort? All numerical laws are original synthetic teaching inputs. Exact computation tests the stipulated model; it is not evidence about real workers, institutions or an estimated treatment effect.

Primitives, timing and real resources

There are two indefinitely lived players, public states G (productive collaboration) and R (review), and actions W = 1 and N = 0 available to each player in both states. The common discount is δ = 4/5. In G, player i earns ui = b aj − c ai, with benefit b = 4 and effort cost c = 1. In R, ui = −k ai, where k = 3/4. Every period supplies fresh capacity for either action; capacity cannot be stored or borrowed. Benefits and effort costs are additive real flows in stipulated common cardinal utility-equivalent units. Adding the two utilities is a declared welfare normalization, not an implication of ordinal preference representation.

  1. 1. Values exist before they are inverted

    Fix the prescribed public actions and let P be their two-state transition matrix and g the per-player current payoff vector. Each row of P is nonnegative and sums to one. Therefore ∥Pz∥∞ ≤ ∥z∥∞, and Σt≥0δtPtg converges absolutely in sup norm because it is bounded by ∥g∥∞/(1−δ). Its sum V satisfies V = g+δPV by shifting the convergent series. If V and W both solve that equation, ∥V−W∥∞ ≤ δ∥V−W∥∞; δ < 1 forces equality to zero. This proves existence and uniqueness of the prescribed values. It does not prove uniqueness of equilibrium.

    VG = 3+δ[αVG+(1−α)VR],
    VR = δ[rVG+(1−r)VR].

    Let D = VG−VR. Subtracting gives D = 3+δ(α−r)D, so D = 3/[1−δ(α−r)]. The denominator is positive for every r ∈ [0,1]. The second equation then gives VR = δrD/(1−δ), and VG = VR+D. At r = 1/2, D = 75/17, V = (225/17,150/17) and U = (45/17,30/17), ordered G,R.

    The invariant probability of G solves πG = πα+(1−π)r, giving π = r/(1−α+r) = 5/6. Its stationary per-agent flow is 5/2. By contrast, normalized discounted G occupancy starting in G is w = (1−δ+δr)/(1−δα+δr) = 15/17, obtained by solving w = (1−δ)eG+δwP. Multiplying by the G flow gives the displayed UG. At r = 0, R is absorbing and the stationary G share is zero although an initial spell in G has positive discounted value.

    Under baseline compliance, each agent receives benefit 4 and consumes cost 1 only in G. Thus per-agent normalized welfare is 3 times discounted G occupancy. Adding across agents multiplies by two under the stipulated common scale. There is no reason to add an imaginary grant or credit effort cost to another player.

  2. 2. A complete deviation theorem

    Claim. Fix a finite public-state profile and its bounded prescribed values Vi. Suppose current payoffs and transition probabilities depend only on the current state and action pair, the opponent uses its prescribed action at every history, and all actions and rewards are bounded. The profile defeats every unilateral history-dependent randomized deviation after every possible continuation history if and only if, for every player, state s and own pure action a,

    Vi(s) ≥ gi(s,a,a−i(s)) + δΣs′P(s′|s,a,a−i(s))Vi(s′).

    Equality holds at the recommended action by the value equation. For necessity, use action a once at a history in state s and return to the profile thereafter. Its gain is the right side minus Vi(s); a positive gain violates the stated continuation requirement. We require every state, including continuations following earlier deviations, rather than only states observed in a finite compliant sample.

    For sufficiency, condition on any private/public history ending at s. Remembering earlier own actions does not change the opponent's prescription, available actions or the conditional reward/transition law. Thus the displayed bound holds at that history for each pure current action, even when the player previously deviated. An arbitrary private randomization averages these bounds. Repeated conditional expectation gives, for every complete deviating strategy and every integer N,

    E[Σt=0N−1δtgi,t + δNVi(sN)] ≤ Vi(s0).

    Here the starting point can be any continuation history; independence of past private choices is not assumed, only the specified conditional Markov law. Since |δNVi(sN)| ≤ δN∥Vi∥∞, the terminal term vanishes uniformly. A bound M on one-period absolute payoff makes the remaining payoff tail at most MδN/(1−δ). Taking limits proves the entire deviating strategy cannot improve value. This proves the required imperfect-public-monitoring result directly; enumerating four stationary policies would not prove it.

    In G a switch W→N saves c = 1 but lowers the next-G probability by α−β. The compliance-minus-deviation margin is mG = −1+δ(α−β)D. In R a switch N→W costs k without changing restoration, so mR = k. At r = 1/2 these are (1/17,3/4). Both are positive for both players by symmetry, so the profile is certified at every history. At this r, allowing δ to vary gives the exact weak patience condition δ ≥ 10/13; equality permits a tied action. Weak IC means nonnegative margins. Strict margins are a separate robustness observation, not a new equilibrium concept or uniform payoff gap for every delayed strategy.

    Always choose N in both states instead. Its value is zero everywhere, and any current W costs either c or k while prescribed future values remain zero. The same theorem certifies this different public equilibrium. We have proved neither unique equilibrium, selection of cooperation, coalitional resistance nor optimality over other automata, signal rules or institutions. A privately persistent state, manipulable report, endogenous monitor or changed timing can invalidate the conditional-law step.

In particular αη = 9/10−2η/5, βη = 3/5−η/10 and the NN probability is 1/5+3η/10.   6. 6. The exact result and both proof directions

    For the stated quadratic representative, fixing others' actions makes own payoff a strictly concave quadratic on [0,∞), with unique best reply xi = max{0,ti−γΣjAijxj}. In the normalized source domain t = 1, replies map [0,1]n continuously into itself. The finite-dimensional Brouwer theorem proved in C04 yields a pure fixed point. This does not imply uniqueness.

    Here is the active-set characterization in original notation. Let S be the set of strictly positive actions. Put x−S = 0. An outcome is equilibrium if and only if

    xS > 0,
    (I+γASS)xS = 1,
    γA−S,SxS ≥ 1.

    Necessity. Each positive action is an interior optimum, so its derivative 1−xi−γΣAijxj vanishes. These are the active equalities. At a zero action, the unconstrained optimum must be nonpositive; otherwise a small positive action improves utility. This gives every inactive inequality. Empty S fails because its inactive left side is zero while the target is one.

    Sufficiency. The active equalities place each positive coordinate at its unique quadratic maximum; the inactive inequalities place every remaining maximum at zero. Thus all players are simultaneously at their global best replies. No local first-order condition is being substituted for global optimality without curvature. If a numerical candidate contains a zero in a supposedly active set, it must be reindexed or deduplicated; true S uses strict positivity.

    Generic qualification. For a fixed finite graph and any active set, det(I+γASS) is a polynomial in γ whose value at zero is one. It is therefore not identically zero and has finitely many real zeros. There are finitely many active sets, so outside a finite exceptional set every active system is invertible and produces at most one candidate. Testing all supports and inactive inequalities then gives a finite complete list. At an exceptional parameter, a singular system can be inconsistent or can admit an affine family. Skipping singular systems is not a complete algorithm there. The Appendix proof and footnote qualification support this distinction; a blanket all-parameter finiteness claim would overread the source.

    For our four-cycle at γ = 1/4, each row has two neighbors, so the reply map is a sup-norm contraction with factor 1/2: the positive-part map is 1-Lipschitz and the weighted row sum is 1/2. Contraction yields uniqueness, and the constant vector (2/3,2/3,2/3,2/3) is a fixed point. All sixteen active sets agree: only full activity survives. Aggregate utility is 8/9, while the potential is 4/3.

    At γ = 1/2, opposite vertices have identical replies, so every equilibrium has x1 = x3 = a and x2 = x4 = b. Nonnegative normalized replies lie in [0,1]. The two equations reduce to a = 1−b and b = 1−a, including the endpoints. Conversely every vector (a,1−a,a,1−a), 0 ≤ a ≤ 1 satisfies all replies. This proves the complete set, not merely a collection of sampled equilibria. The full-support matrix is singular. Its interior solutions supply the entire open segment; two opposite-pair supports supply the endpoints. Reporting only the endpoints would omit infinitely many equilibria.

    At any equilibrium, the active first-order equation gives ui = xi²/2; the zero coordinates satisfy the same payoff identity. Thus aggregate welfare on the critical family is W(a) = a²+(1−a)² = 1−2a+2a², with range (1/2,1). Its minimum occurs at a = 1/2 and maxima at the endpoints. The exact potential Φ(x) = Σxi−Σxi²/2−γΣi<jAijxixj is instead 1 everywhere on the family. A potential reproduces unilateral payoff differences; it is not generally aggregate utility or an equilibrium selection rule.

    A bounded changed-model extension. Now t = (1,1,1,1+ε), 0 < ε ≤ 1, at γ = 1/2. The common action of vertices 1 and 3 is a ∈ [0,1]. The other replies are x2 = max(0,1−a) = 1−a and x4 = max(0,1+ε−a) = 1+ε−a. Substitution into the common reply gives a = max(0,a−ε/2). No a > 0 satisfies it, whereas a = 0 does. Therefore the unique equilibrium is (0,1,0,1+ε). At ε = 1/5 this is (0,1,0,6/5), with aggregate utility 61/50. The target change removes the old degeneracy. It is proved from the new replies, not asserted by inserting heterogeneous targets into an unchanged normalized theorem.
