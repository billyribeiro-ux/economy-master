Capability and prerequisites

After this chapter, you can turn a bond contract into dated cash flows, value those cash flows with a compatible discount curve, reconcile a quoted clean price to the cash invoice, and translate between yield conventions. You can bootstrap discount factors, construct an executable forward loan, prove when a yield-to-maturity root is unique, and explain what an interpolated curve does not identify.

Complete Volumes 2, 3 and 9. In particular, review linear systems, derivatives, integration, dated NPV and IRR, investment return measurement, and replication and positive pricing. Calculus here is ordinary deterministic calculus; stochastic calculus is taught later in this volume. The next chapter develops sensitivities using the price functions established here.

A treasury analyst sees a bond quoted below 100 and describes it as cheap. The settlement desk asks for more cash than the quoted price suggests. Another analyst uses its yield to discount a different bond. A third calls next year's implied forward rate a prediction. Each statement leaves out a necessary part of the contract. Start with what changes hands, when it changes hands, and which transactions are actually available.

1. Write the cash-flow contract before the price

A bond is a claim on specified payments, not a rate printed beside a maturity. Let N be principal, c the annual coupon rate in decimal units, m the number of regular coupon payments per model year, and tj the payment dates after settlement. A regular fixed-principal bond has coupon C=Nc/m at each date and repays N with its final coupon. Its cash-flow vector is (C,…,C,C+N). The coupon rate is measured against principal; a buyer's investment return depends on the price paid.

That vector changes with the contract. A zero-coupon bond has only a redemption payment. An amortizing bond repays principal before maturity, so later interest usually applies to a smaller outstanding balance under its stated terms. A floating coupon or an issuer's right to redeem early makes future cash flows contingent. Default, indexation, embedded options and tax differences require further modeling. This chapter's numerical claims are guaranteed nominal payments in fictional currency units (CU); their certainty is an explicit teaching assumption.

The trade date is when parties agree to exchange; the settlement date is when the transaction's cash and securities are due. Valuation at settlement includes only payments to which that buyer is entitled. Record-date and ex-coupon provisions can separate entitlement from a simple comparison of dates. A holiday adjustment can move a payment date without changing the accrual rule, or can affect both if the contract says so. Neither effect follows merely from the word “bond.” All calendar examples below supply unadjusted dates, full next-coupon entitlement and no ex-coupon interval.

2. Price dated payments with discount factors

At settlement s, D(s,t)>0 denotes the price paid at s for one certain CU at t, for the stated claim and funding perimeter; D(s,s)=1. It is dimensionless: CU today per CU on a named future date. If signed quantities of compatible dated zero-coupon claims can be traded without costs or constraints, a bond with certain payments CFj is replicated by CFj units of each corresponding zero. Therefore its full settlement value is

Pdirty(s) = Σj CFjD(s,tj).

Replication proof. The zero portfolio pays precisely the bond's amount at every payment date. If the bond costs more, sell it and buy that portfolio; the initial difference is a receipt and every subsequent net obligation is zero. If it costs less, reverse the positions. The argument uses identical enforceable cash flows, simultaneous executable quotes, and permission to trade both signs. It does not turn a fitted reference curve into a loan facility or erase issuer, liquidity or settlement differences. The MIT fixed-income recitation is a companion source for these pricing distinctions; the derivation and cases here use their own contracts.

Linearity permits signed cash flows, but a negative component is an obligation that must be financed at its date. Valuing a deterministic vector does not prove that an investor can implement it under collateral limits. With uncertain defaultable cash, replacing CF by a physical expectation and using these discount factors generally fails: state-dependent pricing and recovery enter. Chapter 4 develops that distinction.

Positive D is necessary when a zero with a positive promised payoff is freely tradable: a nonpositive price would give a free positive receipt later, with a nonnegative receipt now. Positivity alone does not require D(s,t)≤1 or a declining discount curve. Those restrictions require additional assumptions about available storage or lending. If unlimited, secure, costless nominal cash storage is available between two dates, one cannot consistently charge more for a later unit than for an otherwise identical earlier unit that can be stored. Without that facility, a negative interest rate is not itself an arbitrage. We will state explicitly when a nonnegative-rate restriction is imposed.

3. Separate accrued interest from value

A dirty price, or full price, includes the settlement value of all entitled future payments. A clean price is that price minus the accrued interest defined by the quotation convention. For a regular coupon period in this chapter's actual-days convention, let a be the previous coupon date, b the next coupon date, n(a,s) the elapsed calendar days and n(a,b) the full period days. Then

AI(s) = C × n(a,s)/n(a,b),   Pclean(s)=Pdirty(s)−AI(s).

The fraction is a fraction of one coupon period, not automatically a fraction of a 365-day year. It allocates part of a coupon to the seller under the supplied convention. It is not an extra payment from the issuer in addition to that coupon. The official TreasuryDirect accrued-interest explanation gives an institutional illustration of interest entering a purchase payment and a later coupon. The fictional schedule and arithmetic below are specified independently of any present market rule.

If a quote is per 100 face and the purchased face amount is Q, multiply both the dirty quote and accrued interest by Q/100. Fees, taxes or financing charges are additional invoice items if the transaction requires them; they are not silently included in AI. Near a payment date, full price loses the coupon when entitlement leaves the purchased claim, while the usual accrual resets. That accounting change is not a loss equal to the coupon for someone who also receives the coupon. In an ex-coupon interval, the right and possibly the quoted accrued-interest convention change; do not apply the simple formula without the actual terms.

Worked example 1 — A clean quote is not the amount to fund

Aster has face 100, annual coupon 6% paid in equal semiannual amounts, previous coupon 15 January 2025, next coupon 15 July 2025, and maturity 15 January 2026. Settlement is 16 April 2025. The buyer receives the next coupon and redemption without default. There are no date adjustments, ex-coupon provisions or fees. The supplied settlement-date discount factors for the two remaining dates are 0.98 and 0.94.

The remaining payments are 3 and 103. Their full value is 3(0.98)+103(0.94)=99.76. The current coupon period has 181 days; 91 have elapsed and 90 remain. Accrued interest is 3(91/181)=273/181≈1.508287. The clean quote is therefore 444589/4525≈98.251713. Discounting 3 and 103 already includes the full next coupon; adding AI to 99.76 would count an accrual allocation twice.

For 250,000 face, the scale factor is 2,500. The full invoice is 249,400 CU, decomposed into approximately 245,629.281768 clean value and 3,770.718232 accrued interest. Round the invoice only under the actual cash-rounding rule; retain unrounded components for reconciliation. A treasury budget of 248,000 fails by 1,400 even though it exceeds the scaled clean quote.

The final payment is 274 calendar days after settlement. The two quoted discount factors belong to those dates. They do not assume that 274 days is exactly one model year or that the next payment is exactly half a year away. At the previous coupon date the accrual would be zero, but this would be a different valuation date with its own discount factors.

4. A rate needs a compounding rule and a clock

For a time interval of τ model years and a known discount factor D, the following are distinct quotations of the same growth factor 1/D:

D=(1+reff)−τ=(1+ym/m)−mτ=exp(−zτ)=1/(1+Lτ).

Thus reff=D−1/τ−1 is an annual effective rate, ym=m(D−1/(mτ)−1) is an annual nominal rate compounded m times, z=−ln(D)/τ is continuously compounded, and L=(1/D−1)/τ is a simple annual investment rate for that interval. The required domains are positive gross factors and positive D. Their numerical values usually differ. Percentage points and basis points are quote units: one basis point is 0.0001 in an annual rate written as a decimal.

For example, a nominal annual 8% rate compounded twice gives annual growth 1.04²=1.0816. Its effective annual rate is 8.16% and its continuous annual rate is 2ln(1.04), approximately 7.844143%. These are equivalent quotations, not three investment opportunities. A bare “8%” does not specify which discount factor to use.

A day count defines τ or an accrual fraction. Actual/365 fixed means actual elapsed days divided by 365; Actual/360 uses 360. The regular actual-days coupon allocation above divides by the actual days in that coupon period and then multiplies by one coupon. Named actual/actual conventions differ in how they treat years, reference coupon periods and stubs. Likewise, “30/360” needs precise end-of-month rules before it defines a calculation. Do not improvise those rules from a label. An irregular first coupon requires its contract amount and reference dates; multiplying N c/m is then not justified automatically.

The clock for accrued interest need not be the clock for a yield quote. A regular semiannual coupon-bond quotation can specify an initial fraction w of a coupon period and price remaining payments with exponents w,w+1,… in (1+y2/2). That is a stated period-based yield convention, not a replacement for actual payment dates in curve valuation. This chapter's calendar case is valued directly with its supplied dated discounts, avoiding an invented yield convention.

A discount-basis bill quote introduces another denominator. If d is a quoted annual discount against face N and τ is its specified year fraction, P=N(1−dτ). The investment gain is N/P−1, so its simple annual investment yield is L=d/(1−dτ). Treating d as L gives a different price. Both formulas are definitions plus algebra; neither identifies an expected future rate.

5. Recover discounts by matching cash flows

Suppose n observed securities have known cash flows at n dates. Stack their cash flows as rows of a matrix A and their dirty prices as p. The pricing equations are A D=p. If A is invertible, these equations identify D at those dates. If rank(A)<n, several discount vectors may fit; if an overdetermined system is inconsistent, there is no exact common-price solution. Mismatched time stamps, quotation conventions, credit or liquidity characteristics can make an observed discrepancy unsuitable for an arbitrage claim.

Triangular bootstrap. If security k pays only at dates 1,…,k and has positive final payment Akk, solve successively:

Dk=[pk−Σj<kAkjDj]/Akk.

The first equation identifies D1. Given the first k−1 discounts, equation k has one unknown and a nonzero coefficient, so the displayed expression is its unique solution. Induction proves uniqueness of the full vector. Positivity is a separate economic check, not a consequence of triangularity. A nonpositive residual must be investigated; replacing it by a small positive number hides a failed contract or price assumption.

A target bond pays (5,5,105). Its value is 5(0.96)+5(0.90)+105(0.84)=97.50. There need not be three separately quoted zeros: use the actual coupon bonds to construct the same payments. The Year-3 equation requires hC=105/106; the Year-2 equation then requires hB=(5−6hC)/104=−25/2756; the Year-1 equation gives hA=(5−4hB−6hC)/100=−25/2756.

These positions cost 97.50 and deliver exactly the target vector. If that identical target is freely shortable at an executable 98, sell one target and buy the replica: receive 0.50 now, with zero net cash at each subsequent date. If 98 is only a stale indication, if the instruments have different default rights, or if shorting is prohibited, this trade has not been established. A model residual is not an executable profit without those conditions.

6. Interpret yield to maturity as a root

Given positive dated payments CFj and a price P, a yield to maturity y is the rate that reproduces P under a specified common-rate discounting convention. At annual dates 1,…,n it solves

P=Σj=1…n CFj(1+y)−j,   y>−1.

Existence and uniqueness. Assume every CFj≥0, at least one is positive, all occur strictly after settlement, and 0<P<∞. The right side is continuous on y>−1. Its derivative is −Σj CFj(1+y)−j−1<0. It tends to infinity as y approaches −1 from above and to zero as y tends to infinity. The intermediate value theorem gives a root for each positive P; strict monotonicity makes it unique. With frequency m and positive exponents τj measured in coupon periods, replace (1+y) by (1+y/m), require y>−m, and the same argument applies.

Negative future obligations, a cash flow at settlement, an embedded exercise decision or uncertain recovery can invalidate those hypotheses or change the interpretation. Earlier IRR counterexamples show why one cannot announce a universally unique return merely because a numerical routine returned one. The bounded lab bisects log(1+y), which preserves the domain; it then reprices the original cash flows to verify the root.

A coupon bond's YTM compresses several discount factors into one number whose weights depend on its payments. It is not generally any one spot rate. For positive cash flows, its annual YTM lies between the minimum and maximum annual spot rates at dates with positive payments: using the minimum spot at all dates cannot price the bond below its curve value, and using the maximum cannot price it above. Monotonicity then brackets the root. This proves a bound, not a license to discount another cash-flow vector at the same YTM.

YTM is also the IRR of paying P and receiving the stated coupons and redemption on schedule. This cash-flow identity does not assume reinvestment. A different statement concerns accumulated terminal wealth: multiplying the yield equation by (1+y)n shows that P(1+y)n equals the terminal amount obtained if every intermediate coupon is accumulated at y. Actual reinvestment at another rate changes that terminal-wealth compound return. Selling early introduces a resale price as well. Distinguish these statements before criticizing or defending a yield quote.

Worked example 3 — One price, several performance questions

Using D1=0.96 and D2=0.90, a two-year bond with face 100 and annual coupon 8 pays (8,108). Its price is 104.88. Put g=1+y. Multiplying the yield equation by g² gives 104.88g²−8g−108=0. The admissible root is g=[8+√(64+4×104.88×108)]/(2×104.88), giving y≈5.362041%; the other quadratic root is negative and violates g>0.

The two-year zero has annual yield 0.90−1/2−1≈5.409255%. Both bonds use the same discount curve. Their different YTMs reflect different cash-flow timing, not evidence that the coupon bond is mispriced. In particular, discounting another claim at 5.362041% does not generally reproduce this curve.

If the first coupon sits in zero-interest cash until maturity, terminal wealth is 8+108=116, and the annualized accumulated return is √(116/104.88)−1≈5.167768%. If it is reinvested for the remaining year at the initial YTM, terminal wealth is approximately 116.428963, exactly 104.88(1+y)² before rounding. The IRR of the dated promised coupons remains y in both descriptions of what the bond itself pays; the final account wealth differs because the coupon strategy differs.

7. Lock a forward loan; do not invent a forecast

For dates s<S<T, the gross amount due at T for one unit lent at S in a zero-value forward loan is G=D(s,S)/D(s,T). To derive it, sell one S-date zero and buy G T-date zeros now. The cost is −D(s,S)+G D(s,T)=0. At S the position owes one CU; at T it receives G CU. That is a commitment to lend at S with a repayment fixed today. Its effective annual rate is G1/(T−S)−1 when time is measured in the same years as the spot quotes; its simple annual rate is (G−1)/(T−S).

The forward identity follows from today's tradable prices. It does not specify the physical distribution of the future short rate. When rates are uncertain, committing now and deciding later are different strategies. Risk premiums and different valuation weights can separate an implied forward from a physical expectation; Chapter 3 builds explicit finite-state examples. Even in a deterministic model, explain why its assumed future path is known before interpreting a forward as that path.

Worked example 4 — A loan with zero initial value still needs cash later

On the bootstrapped curve, D1/D2=0.96/0.90=16/15. A forward loan of 60 CU from Year 1 to Year 2 therefore pays 64 CU at Year 2: a one-year rate of 6⅔%. Sell 60 units of the Year-1 zero for 57.60 now and buy 64 units of the Year-2 zero for 57.60. Initial net cost is zero; the future cash vector is (−60,+64).

Suppose an additional, otherwise identical forward contract can be entered for no initial payment and offers the lender 7%, so it promises (−60,+64.20). Buy that contract and the opposite zero portfolio: buy 60 Year-1 zeros and sell 64 Year-2 zeros. At Year 1 the zeros provide the 60 needed to fund the loan; at Year 2 the loan pays 64.20 against the 64 short-zero obligation. The net gain is 0.20 at Year 2, with all other net flows zero. Equivalently the offered lender claim has current value −60(0.96)+64.20(0.90)=0.18, so it cannot be free under these permissions.

A lender who holds only the fair forward owes 60 at Year 1. Its zero initial value does not provide that funding. The offsetting replicating positions in the arbitrage argument matter; omit them and the purported “free” strategy has an unfunded future obligation. Fees, counterparty credit and margin can also break the idealized comparison.

8. Separate a curve representation from observed information

A finite set of bond prices cannot by itself identify discounts at every real-valued maturity. For example, D(1)=0.96 and D(2)=0.90 leave D(1.5) unspecified. Linear interpolation of discounts gives 0.93. Linear interpolation of log discounts gives √(0.96×0.90)≈0.929516. Both fit the endpoints, but they price a 100-CU payment at 1.5 years at 93.00 and approximately 92.951600. A solver's smooth line is an assumption between the observations.

For log-linear interpolation on [S,T], define θ=(u−S)/(T−S) and ln D(u)=(1−θ)ln D(S)+θ ln D(T). Exponentiation preserves positivity. Differentiating gives a constant continuously compounded instantaneous forward within the interval. More generally, let D be positive and continuously differentiable on [0,T], with D(0)=1, and define for 0<t≤T

f(u)=−d ln D(u)/du,   D(t)=exp[−∫0tf(u)du],   z(t)=−ln D(t)/t.

The integral identity follows by integrating the derivative and using D(0)=1. The continuous zero rate z(t) is an average of instantaneous forwards from 0 to t, not the instantaneous forward at t. A piecewise log-linear curve can have jumps in f at knots while D stays continuous. Smoother f requires an additional fitting choice. A good quote fit, plausible shape, numerical stability and forecasting performance are different evaluation criteria.

The historical Anderson–Sleath study explicitly evaluates curve estimation through fit, smoothness, flexibility and stability. Its setting motivates a research question, not a universally preferred algorithm. In the present example, multiplying the log-linear D(u) on [1,2] by exp[ε(u−1)(2−u)] leaves both observed endpoints unchanged for every finite ε. For ε=0.02 it is positive and still decreasing: its instantaneous forward ranges from ln(16/15)−0.02 to ln(16/15)+0.02, both positive. Yet its midpoint discount is approximately 0.934175. Even adding positive forwards fails to identify the unquoted midpoint uniquely.

Curve validation therefore needs the precise inputs, cash-flow extraction, price residuals, sensitivity to individual quotes, interpolation and extrapolation choices, and a record of which claims are comparable. Repricing calibration inputs is necessary but can be mechanically guaranteed by the number of parameters. A model can fit exactly and be poor at an unobserved maturity. Forecasting the future curve requires still more evidence.

9. Reproduce the valuation and challenge its assumptions

The Aster laboratory supplies original inputs, standard-library Python code, complete reference results, a file manifest and an execution and interpretation guide. Save the five files together and run python3 v10-c01-analysis.py. It works offline without external packages. Solve the four cases first; the implementation is a benchmark to inspect, not evidence that a future quotation is authentic.

The program derives calendar day counts, the invoice, all three bootstrap discounts, the actual coupon-bond replica, the yield root and its repricing residual, coupon accumulation, an early-sale return, forward cash and value, three interpolation choices and bill-quote conversions. Exact rational results are strings; numerical roots and logs are finite floating-point numbers. Inputs use bounded rational or decimal strings to avoid silently converting quotation decimals into binary approximations.

Make documented changes in a copy. Moving settlement within the stated coupon interval changes accrued interest; with unchanged supplied settlement discounts, dirty value stays fixed and clean value offsets accrual. Changing one calibration price can move several recovered discounts, without representing a parallel curve shift. Changing the offered forward rate changes its value, not the fair zero-price rate derived from the curve. The code rejects contradictory fixed payment schedules, nonpositive implied discounts and unsupported numeric domains. It is not a holiday calendar, a general bond convention engine or a model for default and options.

An additional traded claim or a maintained interpolation model is needed to choose one.

  8. With τ=180/360=1/2, P=100(1−0.06/2)=97. Holding gain is 3/97≈3.092784%. Simple annual investment yield on the 360 clock is (3/97)/(1/2)=6/97≈6.185567%; on Actual/365 it is (3/97)/(180/365)=73/1164≈6.271478%. Effective annual yield on the 360 clock is (100/97)²−1≈6.281220%; continuous annual yield is 2ln(100/97)≈6.091841%. These clocks and conventions annualize the same 180-day payoff differently. Misusing 6% as a simple investment rate gives 100/1.03≈97.087379, not 97.

  9. The ex-coupon sale price is 108/1.10=1080/11≈98.181818. Including the received coupon, the holding return is (8+1080/11)/104.88−1=179/14421≈1.241245%. The initial YTM was about 5.362041% on the original two-year promised stream. The early sale substitutes a price reflecting a changed remaining yield; it is not the original redemption. If held longer, reinvestment of interim coupons can also change accumulated wealth. Default, fees and taxes are further possible real-world differences, but were not supplied in this case and must not be invented to explain its arithmetic.

  10. The pricing equations identify three discounts at three dates because the triangular cash-flow matrix is nonsingular. They do not identify discounts between those dates: Exercise 7 gives explicit positive, decreasing alternatives with the same endpoint fit. Moreover, a zero-value forward loan follows from pricing and replication; it does not establish a physical expectation or forecast error property. These are two separate overclaims.

    A defensible evaluation first aligns price times, dirty/clean treatment, entitlement and claim risk. It reports calibration residuals but does not treat a mechanically exact fit as an external test. It then perturbs individual quotes by documented plausible errors, recomputes curve points and unquoted-payment values, and compares interpolation choices with prespecified shape restrictions. A new comparable bond with an intermediate payment supplies evidence about interpolation. If a bond is held out from only three identifying equations, the remaining system is underidentified unless a lower-dimensional model or additional observations are specified; “leave one out” is not by itself a complete experiment. Forecasting needs a separately defined target, information cutoff, out-of-sample observations, benchmark and uncertainty treatment. Credit, liquidity or stale quotes must be investigated rather than forced into a forecast story.

    Grade for the rank/identification distinction, a concrete counterexample, accurate separation of pricing and physical expectations, an implementable data/model design, sensitivity and error diagnostics, and honest limits. Alternative interpolation families can earn full credit. The historical paper's policy-purpose choice does not certify the best method for every trading or liability-valuing task.

Teach-back and delayed retention

Explain to a colleague why a bond below par can require more cash than its clean quote, why two correctly priced bonds can have different YTMs, and why a forward loan with zero initial value still creates a funding obligation. A complete answer identifies the buyer's coupon right and accrual allocation, shows that the common-rate root depends on the payment vector, and draws the forward's negative cash flow at its start date. Saying only “market convention” or “risk” does not supply those mechanisms.

After one week without notes: (1) value a guaranteed claim paying 3 and 103 at two supplied future dates with discounts 0.99 and 0.95, then find its clean price if accrued interest is 1.2; (2) derive the gross one-year forward factor from D1=0.99 and D2=0.95 and describe the cash needed to lend 95 at Year 1; (3) explain whether a price of 101 for a one-year guaranteed 100 payment is necessarily an arbitrage.

Retention answers

(1) Dirty value is 100.82; clean value is 99.62. The invoice before separate fees uses the dirty amount. (2) Gross factor is 99/95, so lending 95 at Year 1 produces 99 at Year 2. Its zero-cost replica shorts 95 Year-1 zeros and buys 99 Year-2 zeros; each side has current value 94.05. It owes the 95 at Year 1. (3) The yield is 100/101−1=−1/101. That alone is not an arbitrage in the defined bond-only market. If costless safe cash storage and shorting are actually available, short the 101-priced claim, retain 100 to meet redemption, and keep the excess 1; those extra permissions change the conclusion.
