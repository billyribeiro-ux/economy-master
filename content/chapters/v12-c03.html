Volume 12 · Chapter 3

Instrumental variables and GMM

A lender's cheapest loan offers may go to the firms it expects to grow. A regression of growth on borrowing then mixes financing with selection. An instrument proposes a source of variation in borrowing that does not carry the omitted growth shock. That proposal has two separate burdens: the variation must move borrowing, and its outcome effect must operate through the specified treatment rather than an omitted route. A large first-stage statistic addresses only part of the first burden.

Capability. Derive IV, two-stage least squares and generalized method of moments estimators; state their identification and inference conditions; distinguish efficient weighting from valid moments; derive the overidentification test and a test that survives weak relevance under an explicit Gaussian contract; and explain which heterogeneous treatment effect an encouragement identifies. The cases and data below are original teaching constructions, not empirical evidence about an actual lender or program.

Prerequisites. Identification and asymptotic tools supplies stochastic-order algebra, compact consistency and the fixed-dimensional iid score CLT, with the Cramér–Wold characterization explicitly invoked. Linear models and robust inference supplies projections and sandwich reasoning. Matrix algebra, conditional expectations and Gaussian vectors come from Volumes 2–3. We prove the IV-specific covariance replacement and weighting arguments here. No dependent-data, growing-instrument or uniform weak-identification theorem is inherited.

1. Validity, exclusion and rank answer different questions

Let y be a scalar outcome, x a k×1 vector of regressors, z an m×1 vector of instruments, and β₀ a k×1 target in an unrestricted linear parameter space. An included intercept is an actual entry of x and z, not an implicit centering operation. Write y=x′β₀+u. Endogeneity permits E[xu]≠0; the proposed identifying restriction is E[zu]=0. Assume the expectations below exist. Define B=E[zx′], an m×k matrix, and a=E[zy], an m×1 vector. The population moment is

g(β)=E[z(y−x′β)]=a−Bβ; g(β₀)=0.

Validity means that the proposed moment holds for the intended target. An exclusion restriction is a substantive restriction on how the instrument can affect the outcome; together with assignment and other conditions it may justify validity. Instrument relevance concerns the rank of B. None of these claims follows from another merely because a program returns a coefficient.

Linear identification proof. If B has full column rank k, g(β)=g(β₀)=0 implies B(β−β₀)=0 and hence β=β₀. Conversely, if rank(B)<k, choose d≠0 with Bd=0. Every β₀+td, t∈ℝ, satisfies the same moments. Thus full column rank is necessary and sufficient for point identification by these linear moments on ℝk. Having m≥k is necessary but not sufficient. This conclusion is about this moment model: additional restrictions can change the identified set, and nonlinear equations require a separate argument.

With one centered regressor and instrument, relevance is E[zx]≠0 and β₀=E[zy]/E[zx]. With an intercept, the slope ratio uses covariances instead: Cov(z,y)/Cov(z,x). Partialling out included controls must partial both the instrument and endogenous regressor consistently. The instrument may affect x strongly and still have a direct effect on y or be associated with u. The ratio remains computable in such a case but need not equal the desired structural coefficient. The scoped framework is developed in MIT's 2017 IV lecture.

Worked case 1: a valid shifter, an endogenous regressor and the wrong residual

Let Z,V,W be independent signs, each equally likely to be −1 or 1. All eight triples have probability 1/8. Define a centered funding index x=Z+V and an outcome y=2x+u, where u=V+W. The outcome and index are normalized units, so the slope is outcome units per index unit. This stipulated law gives E[Zu]=0 but E[xu]=1. Because E[Zx]=1 and E[Zy]=2, the instrument identifies 2. By contrast E[x²]=2 and E[xy]=5, so the population projection slope is 5/2.

The population first-stage projection of x on Z is x*=Z. Regressing y on x* gives slope 2, but its residual y−2x*=3V+W is not the structural residual y−2x=V+W. The correct instrument-score second moment E[Z²u²] is 2; replacing u by that second-stage residual gives 10. The correct root-n IV variance is 2, since B=1. A two-stage recipe therefore reproduces the coefficient without licensing ordinary second-stage standard errors.

If the outcome instead contains a direct effect (2/5)Z, the same relevant instrument has E[Zu]=2/5 when the target coefficient on x remains 2. The IV ratio becomes 12/5. More precise first-stage estimation does not remove that difference. Averaging all eight states verifies these identities exactly; it is not an iid sample confidence calculation or proof of validity outside the stipulated law.

2. From projections to weighted moments

For n observations stack regressors in the n×k matrix X, instruments in n×m Z, and outcomes in y. Assume Z has column rank m and Z′X has rank k. The instrument projection is PZ=Z(Z′Z)−1Z′, a symmetric idempotent matrix. The fitted first-stage regressors are X̂=PZX. Since PZ²=PZ, X̂′X̂=X′PZX and X̂′y=X′PZy. Ordinary least squares of y on X̂ therefore gives

β̂2SLS=(X′PZX)−1X′PZy.

This is the two-stage least squares estimator. Included exogenous columns belong in the instrument span along with the excluded instruments. Sample rank makes the formula exist; it does not establish the population exclusion or make a ratio unbiased in finite samples. For covariance estimation use û=y−Xβ̂, retaining the original X. A nonsingular change of instrument basis Z→ZR leaves PZ unchanged, hence leaves 2SLS unchanged.

Write aₙ=n−1Z′y, Bₙ=n−1Z′X and Sₙ=n−1Z′Z. The sample moment is gₙ(β)=aₙ−Bₙβ. For a symmetric positive-definite m×m weighting matrix Wₙ, possibly estimated from these data, linear generalized method of moments (GMM) minimizes gₙ(β)′Wₙgₙ(β). If Bₙ has column rank k, differentiating gives

Bₙ′Wₙ(aₙ−Bₙβ̂)=0,
β̂=(Bₙ′WₙBₙ)−1Bₙ′Wₙaₙ.

The positive-definite quadratic curvature proves that this solution is the unique global minimum. Setting Wₙ=Sₙ−1 produces 2SLS by substituting the definitions and cancelling powers of n. In an exactly identified system m=k with Bₙ invertible, β̂=Bₙ−1aₙ makes every moment zero, so weighting is irrelevant. With more moments, a fit normally leaves nonzero sample moments and the weights affect the coefficient.

Units matter. If moments are transformed by an invertible m×m matrix T, use gₙ*=Tgₙ and Wₙ*=T−TWₙT−1 to retain the same criterion. Rescaling a noisy moment while leaving an identity weight unchanged changes the estimator. The linear GMM lecture motivates weighting and the 2SLS special case. Here every derivative follows the convention g=a−Bβ, so its Jacobian is G=−B.

3. A complete fixed-dimensional iid inference argument

Conditions. Observations (yᵢ,xᵢ,zᵢ) are iid from one fixed law; k and m are fixed; y=x′β₀+u with E[zu]=0; B=E[zx′] has rank k; E‖zx′‖<∞ and E‖zu‖²<∞; and Wₙ→PW, where W is fixed symmetric positive definite. Let Ω=E[zz′u²]. It is the covariance of the centered instrument score zu. Positive definiteness of Ω will be required for efficient inverse weighting and the nonsingular tests below. The first linear limit can allow singular Ω.

Exact identity and limit. On the sample full-rank event, substitute aₙ=Bₙβ₀+gₙ(β₀) into the estimator:

β̂−β₀=Kₙgₙ(β₀), where Kₙ=(Bₙ′WₙBₙ)−1Bₙ′Wₙ.
√n(β̂−β₀)=Kₙ n−1/2∑ᵢ zᵢuᵢ.

The entrywise LLN gives Bₙ→PB. For d≠0, d′B′WBd=(Bd)′W(Bd)>0; thus B′WB is invertible. Inverse continuity gives Kₙ→PKW=(B′WB)−1B′W, with sample full rank holding with probability tending to one. Define the estimator arbitrarily on the exceptional singular events for this asymptotic statement; software should report those failures explicitly. LLN gives gₙ(β₀)→P0, proving consistency. The iid score CLT and Slutsky then give

√n(β̂−β₀)=KW n−1/2∑ᵢzᵢuᵢ+oP(1) ⇒ N(0,VW),
VW=(B′WB)−1B′WΩWB(B′WB)−1.

No independence between Wₙ and the score is needed: Wₙ converges to a constant. The root-n rate follows from the exact identity rather than being assumed to justify it. If using the general derivative G=−B, the signed multiplier is −(G′WG)−1G′W. The sign does not change a marginal symmetric normal distribution, but it is essential when combining estimator and fitted-moment expansions.

Structural-residual covariance replacement. Let β̃ be any consistent preliminary estimate, ũᵢ=yᵢ−xᵢ′β̃ and Ω̃ₙ=n−1∑zᵢzᵢ′ũᵢ². Add E‖zx′‖²<∞. Set sᵢ=zᵢuᵢ and dᵢ=zᵢ(ũᵢ−uᵢ)=−zᵢxᵢ′(β̃−β₀). Then

n−1∑‖dᵢ‖² ≤ ‖β̃−β₀‖² n−1∑‖zᵢxᵢ′‖²=oP(1).

The last sample average is OP(1) by LLN. Expand (sᵢ+dᵢ)(sᵢ+dᵢ)′−sᵢsᵢ′. Its average norm is bounded by 2{n−1∑‖sᵢ‖²}1/2{n−1∑‖dᵢ‖²}1/2+n−1∑‖dᵢ‖², which is oP(1) by Cauchy–Schwarz and the score second moment. Entrywise LLN for sᵢsᵢ′ therefore proves Ω̃ₙ→PΩ. This argument allows preliminary estimation on the same observations.

With Ω positive definite, Wₙ=Ω̃ₙ−1 is consistent efficient weighting. A practical two-step procedure first estimates 2SLS, forms this uncentered structural-residual meat, and then minimizes the criterion with that fixed weight. Recomputing the meat after the second step is another finite-sample procedure, even when both procedures have the same first-order limit. Centering sample scores also changes finite values; its correction vanishes asymptotically under these conditions because their sample mean tends to zero.

Plugging Bₙ,Wₙ,Ω̃ₙ into the sandwich gives a consistent V̂. For a fixed k×1 contrast c with c′VWc>0, (c′β̂−c′β₀)/√(c′V̂c/n) tends to N(0,1). This is an asymptotic statement under the specified iid law. It supplies no universal finite-sample confidence guarantee, and changing sampling units to clusters or time series changes the score covariance and CLT requirements.

4. Efficient weighting and what the J test can reject

Assume Ω is positive definite. Put K*=(B′Ω−1B)−1B′Ω−1. The efficient choice is W=Ω−1, yielding V*=(B′Ω−1B)−1. This weights noisy and correlated moments jointly, rather than merely assigning large weights to convenient instruments.

Efficiency proof in the stated class. Both KWB and K*B equal Ik. Write D=KW−K*, so DB=0. Because K*Ω=(B′Ω−1B)−1B′, the cross product K*ΩD′ vanishes. Expanding the covariance gives

VW−V*=DΩD′ ⪰ 0.

For every contrast c the difference c′DΩD′c is nonnegative. This is a positive-semidefinite comparison, not necessarily a strict improvement in every direction. It compares regular GMM estimators based on the same correct moments under the stated strong-rank conditions; it does not prove that those moments are valid or establish a universal efficiency claim over every statistical model. If E[u²|z]=σ² and S=E[zz′] is positive definite, Ω=σ²S, so 2SLS weighting S−1 differs from efficient weighting only by a scalar. With heteroskedasticity that simplification generally fails.

For m>k, suppose β̂ minimizes the criterion using Ω̃ₙ−1, with the consistency, covariance and rank conditions above. The J statistic is Jₙ=n gₙ(β̂)′Ω̃ₙ−1gₙ(β̂). Its null is that the specified joint moment system is correct for a parameter satisfying the regularity conditions, not that each excluded instrument is individually certified.

Whitening proof. The exact fitted-moment identity and the signed estimator expansion yield √n gₙ(β̂)=[I−BK*]√n gₙ(β₀)+oP(1). Let H=Ω−1/2B and N=Ω−1/2G₀, where G₀∼N(0,Ω). The whitened limit is

[I−H(H′H)−1H′]N = RN, with N∼N(0,Im).

H has rank k; its projection H(H′H)−1H′ is symmetric idempotent with rank k. Therefore R is symmetric idempotent with rank m−k. An orthogonal diagonalization turns R into m−k ones and k zeros without changing the standard normal law. Consequently ‖RN‖² is the sum of m−k independent squared standard normals: Jₙ⇒χ²m−k. Replacing the population covariance by Ω̃ₙ changes the quadratic form by oP(1), since √n gₙ(β̂)=OP(1) and inverse covariance converges. This supplies the proof behind the scoped GMM/J lecture.

When m=k and sample rank holds, fitted moments are exactly zero and Jₙ=0: there is no positive-degree overidentification test. Under rank failure or a weak sequence the usual χ²m−k argument is not established. Even under strong rank, rejection does not identify whether exclusion, measurement, functional form, sampling or another joint assumption failed. Nonrejection is not proof of validity. If E[zu]=Bδ for δ≠0, all population moments vanish at β₀+δ, and the J test cannot distinguish that fit from the intended β₀ using these restrictions alone.

5. Nonlinear GMM: consistency first, then local linearization

Let the iid observation be w, with an m×1 moment function h(w,θ) and k×1 parameter θ. Define g(θ)=E[h(w,θ)], gₙ(θ)=n−1∑h(wᵢ,θ), and G(θ)=∂g(θ)/∂θ′, an m×k matrix. Unlike the linear case, a full-rank derivative at one point establishes local behavior but does not rule out another root far away.

A sufficient bounded theorem. Take a nonempty compact Θ⊂ℝk. Assume g is continuous and has exactly one zero θ₀ in Θ, with θ₀ interior. Let gₙ be continuous, let Wₙ→PW≻0 be symmetric and independent of the candidate θ, and suppose supΘ‖gₙ−g‖→P0. Assume the suprema used here are measurable. Choose a measurable exact global minimizer of gₙ′Wₙgₙ; existence follows from continuity and compactness, while measurability of the selected minimizer is an explicit condition. For the root-n conclusion additionally assume continuous differentiability on an open neighborhood of θ₀, uniform convergence of Gₙ=∂gₙ/∂θ′ to continuous G there, rank G(θ₀)=k, and √n gₙ(θ₀)⇒N(0,Ω).

These are conditions to verify, not consequences of an optimizer's status. Under iid sampling, c01's finite-net LLN supplies the first uniform convergence when h is integrable at an anchor and has an integrable Lipschitz envelope on Θ. Applying the same argument to each derivative supplies uniform Jacobian convergence when its own anchor and envelope conditions hold. A finite score second moment supplies the CLT. Smoothness alone does not provide integrability or a sampling law.

Consistency. Write Qₙ=gₙ′Wₙgₙ and Q=g′Wg. Since sup‖g‖ is finite and sup‖gₙ−g‖=oP(1), sup‖gₙ‖=OP(1). Consequently

sup|Qₙ−Q| ≤ ‖Wₙ−W‖ sup‖gₙ‖² + ‖W‖ sup‖gₙ−g‖(sup‖gₙ‖+sup‖g‖)=oP(1).

Positive-definite W and the unique zero make θ₀ the unique global minimizer of Q. The compact separation proof in c01 yields θ̂→Pθ₀. An objective error oP(1) would also suffice for this consistency step; it is not enough by itself for the following rate claim.

Signed local expansion without assuming the rate. Consistency and interiority place θ̂ and its segment to θ₀ inside a fixed differentiability ball with probability tending to one. There the exact first-order condition is Gₙ(θ̂)′Wₙgₙ(θ̂)=0. Put Δ=θ̂−θ₀ and Ḡₙ=∫₀¹Gₙ(θ₀+tΔ)dt. The fundamental theorem of calculus gives gₙ(θ̂)=gₙ(θ₀)+ḠₙΔ. Uniform Jacobian convergence, continuity and consistency imply Gₙ(θ̂)→PG and Ḡₙ→PG, where G=G(θ₀). Thus

√nΔ=−[Gₙ(θ̂)′WₙḠₙ]−1Gₙ(θ̂)′Wₙ√n gₙ(θ₀)
=−(G′WG)−1G′W√n gₙ(θ₀)+oP(1).

The first inverse exists with probability tending to one because its limit G′WG is positive definite. Its bounded coefficient and the score CLT establish the root-n rate; no prior rate was used. The covariance is (G′WG)−1G′WΩWG(G′WG)−1. An approximate first-order solver can use this argument if its gradient residual is oP(n−1/2) and it already satisfies the consistency/global-selection conditions. A stationary point far from the global target does not qualify.

For feasible covariance, a local envelope ‖h(w,θ)−h(w,θ₀)‖≤L(w)‖θ−θ₀‖ with E[L²]<∞ and E‖h(w,θ₀)‖²<∞ gives the same squared-difference/Cauchy–Schwarz replacement proof as Section 3. With Ω≻0, consistent inverse weighting and full rank, the efficiency and J projection proofs then apply with H=Ω−1/2G; the sign cancels in the projection, not in the estimator expansion. This theorem remains fixed-dimensional and law-specific.

Worked case 3: a nonlinear calibration with a genuine domain

Suppose iid Y has mean 4 and variance 16, and the economic parameter θ is restricted to Θ=[1,3] with moment E[Y]−θ²=0. θ is measured in the square root of Y's units. The unique target is θ₀=2. The sample criterion is (Ȳ−θ²)², so the exact minimizer is √max(1,min(9,Ȳ)). The population derivative is G=−4; the root-n variance is 16/(−4)²=1.

Uniform moment error equals |Ȳ−4|; the derivative −2θ is deterministic, continuous and bounded on Θ. Thus all the stated consistency and derivative conditions hold. For n=100 and Ȳ=441/100, the estimate is 21/10. The local approximation 2+(Ȳ−4)/4 is 841/400, with exact difference estimate minus approximation −1/400. The population-scale standard error is 1/√100=1/10; substituting θ̂ in the derivative while retaining the supplied outcome variance gives 2/21. These are asymptotic standard-error calculations, not an exact Gaussian law for the clipped square root.

Changing the domain to [−3,3] gives roots −2 and 2, each with a nonzero derivative. A local rank check cannot select between them. Conversely g(θ)=−θ³ has a unique zero at 0 but derivative zero there. With Ȳ−θ³ as its sample moment and iid Y∼N(0,1), the interior cube-root solution has rate n−1/6, not n−1/2. Exercises 8–9 separate these global and local issues from weak linear relevance.

6. Weak relevance and an exactly calibrated candidate test

A fixed nonzero population denominator can justify a pointwise limit while giving poor guidance along a sequence where the denominator shrinks. To see this without simulation, fix a deterministic n×1 instrument z with z′z=n. Let x=zπₙ+v and y=βx+u. The pairs (uᵢ,vᵢ) are iid centered Gaussian with a fixed positive-definite 2×2 covariance matrix; u and v may be correlated. This is a separate fixed-instrument triangular family, not an application of the preceding fixed-law random-design theorem.

Define Uₙ=z′u/√n and Vₙ=z′v/√n. Their joint distribution is the same centered nonsingular Gaussian for every n, because z′z/n=1. Direct algebra gives, whenever the denominator is nonzero,

β̂IV−β=Uₙ/(√nπₙ+Vₙ).

For fixed π≠0 the error tends to zero and √n(β̂−β)⇒N(0,Var(u)/π²). If πₙ=c/√n, however, the error has the same nondegenerate ratio distribution U/(c+V) at every n. Its denominator is zero with probability zero under the continuous Gaussian law, but can be arbitrarily close to zero. No shrinking normal-error claim follows. At zero relevance, these IV moments do not identify β; another model with additional restrictions might. A first-stage F statistic can diagnose relevance in a specified setting, but no universal cutoff certifies causal validity or normal approximation quality.

An exact, deliberately narrow alternative. Let Z now be a fixed n×m full-column-rank instrument matrix, 1≤m≤n. Suppose under the candidate null β=b the scalar-regressor structural residual r(b)=y−bx equals u, with u∼N(0,σ²Iₙ) and known σ²>0. There are no unknown intercepts or other nuisance coefficients in this test. x may depend on u through an endogenous first stage. The fixed Z and structural Gaussian error law are the assumptions; conditioning on an endogenous X is not being asserted.

T(b)=r(b)′PZr(b)/σ².

Choose n×m Q with orthonormal columns spanning Z, so PZ=QQ′. Under the null, Q′u/σ∼N(0,Iₘ); its m coordinates are independent. Therefore T(b)=‖Q′u/σ‖² has exactly χ²ₘ distribution, regardless of first-stage strength. For a supplied upper critical value c≥0 satisfying P(χ²ₘ≤c)=1−α, the set C={b:T(b)≤c} contains the true β with probability exactly 1−α under this contract. The equivalence between coverage and nonrejection at the true candidate proves the statement. It does not require a normally distributed coefficient estimator.

This is a known-variance version of the candidate-null inversion principle in the IV/GMM sources. Unknown variance, estimated controls, heteroskedasticity, clusters or serial correlation require a different statistic and argument; substituting an arbitrary estimated variance cannot inherit this exact χ² law. The general moment test n gₙ(b)′Ω̂(b)−1gₙ(b) has an asymptotic χ²ₘ null only if its candidate-null score CLT and covariance consistency are justified. Pointwise conditions alone do not prove uniform validity over drifting populations or simultaneous coverage of an entire identified set.

Solve the set rather than truncate a grid. Put A=x′PZx, B=x′PZy and C=y′PZy. These letters denote scalar quadratic coefficients only in this subsection. T(b)≤c is A b²−2Bb+C≤cσ². If A>0, the minimum is C−B²/A at b=B/A. A threshold below that minimum gives the empty set; equality gives a singleton; a larger threshold gives the closed interval

[B/A−√((cσ²−C+B²/A)/A), B/A+√((cσ²−C+B²/A)/A)].

If A=0, PZx=0 and hence B=0. The set is all ℝ when C≤cσ² and empty otherwise. A<0, or A=0 with B≠0, cannot arise from this projection contract. The nonnegative quadratic coefficient rules out two disjoint rays. An interval shape from another candidate-dependent variance statistic must be derived from that statistic; it cannot be imported here. A finite grid neither proves that no accepted values lie beyond it nor resolves boundary equality.

Worked case 4: an interval, a singleton, emptiness and no information

Take the two columns of Z to be (1,−1,0,0)′ and (0,0,1,−1)′, x=(1,−1,1,−1)′ and y=(2,−2,0,0)′. With σ²=1, direct projection gives A=4, B=4, C=8 and

T(b)=4(b−1)²+4.

For the supplied χ²₂ critical value c=8, the confidence coefficient is 1−e−4≈0.981684. This follows from the χ²₂ tail exp(−c/2), or by integrating the standard bivariate Gaussian in polar coordinates. Solving the inequality gives 0≤b≤2. It is not a conventional symmetric standard-error interval around an IV ratio. At c=4 the set is the singleton {1}; at c=3 it is empty because the projected mismatch cannot fall below 4.

Keep Z but replace x by (1,1,−1,−1)′. Then PZx=0. With y=(1,−1,0,0)′, T(b)=2 for every b, so c=8 accepts all ℝ. With y=(3,−3,0,0)′, T(b)=18 and the same cutoff accepts none. An empty set rejects all candidates in this tested model; it is not an infinitely precise estimate. All ℝ expresses absence of information from this test. Neither case should be silently replaced by the endpoints of a displayed grid.

7. An instrument can identify a local effect rather than an average for everyone

The constant-slope model above is not a license to call every IV estimate a population treatment effect. For a distinct heterogeneous-effect contract, let Z∈{0,1} be an encouragement, D(z)∈{0,1} the treatment that would be taken under assignment z, and Y(d) the outcome under treatment d. Assume consistency D=D(Z), Y=Y(D), no interference between units, integrable potentials, and 0<P(Z=1)<1. Require independence of Z from the joint vector (D(0),D(1),Y(0),Y(1)); an exclusion restriction that the encouragement has no direct outcome effect; monotonicity D(1)≥D(0); and positive complier mass P(D(1)>D(0))>0.

Independence describes assignment; exclusion describes which intervention affects Y; monotonicity excludes individuals who would take treatment only when not encouraged. These are distinct hypotheses. Observing a positive average first stage does not prove individual monotonicity, and random encouragement does not itself eliminate a direct informational or financial outcome channel.

Compliance proof. Write τ=Y(1)−Y(0). By assignment independence and consistency,

E[Y|Z=1]−E[Y|Z=0]=E[τ(D(1)−D(0))],
E[D|Z=1]−E[D|Z=0]=E[D(1)−D(0)].

Under monotonicity, D(1)−D(0) equals the indicator of being a complier. Dividing by its positive mean gives

βWald = {E[Y|Z=1]−E[Y|Z=0]}/{E[D|Z=1]−E[D|Z=0]}
=E[Y(1)−Y(0)|D(1)>D(0)].

This is the local average treatment effect (LATE). For binary Z, both Cov(Z,Y) and Cov(Z,D) equal P(Z=1)P(Z=0) times their respective conditional-mean differences, so the slope from IV with an intercept equals this ratio. The proof uses no constant individual effect. It identifies the average for people whose treatment responds to this encouragement, not automatically the population ATE, the effect among all treated people, or the response to another policy. The original identification argument is in Imbens and Angrist (1994), especially pages 468–470.

Without monotonicity, write pC and pF for complier and defier shares and τC,τF for their mean effects. The same difference calculation gives (pCτC−pFτF)/(pC−pF) when the denominator is nonzero. The negative weight permits values outside the range of individual effects. A direct outcome effect of Z adds another numerator component instead. Neither failure is repaired by reporting a small standard error.

Now move a share 1/8 from never treated to defiers with D(0)=1,D(1)=0,Y(0)=0,Y(1)=12. Assignment is still independent and exclusion still holds, but monotonicity fails. The treatment difference is 1/2−1/8=3/8; the outcome difference is 2−12/8=1/2; the Wald ratio is 4/3, below every individual effect in the new population. Alternatively retain the original types and add a direct outcome effect (1/2)Z. The unchanged first stage then gives a Wald ratio 5, although the complier treatment effect holding encouragement fixed remains 4.

Even the baseline ATE is not identified from observed (Z,D,Y) by these assumptions alone: changing only the never-treated Y(1) changes an unobserved counterfactual and the ATE, while leaving the entire observed law unchanged. The local estimand is an achievement with a boundary, not a defective estimate of a different target. Transport to a new population or a different encouragement requires further evidence about compliance and effect heterogeneity.

8. From a method to an auditable research claim

Start with a specified outcome, treatment, units, target population and assignment mechanism. State the substantive exclusion argument before computing a first stage. Record included controls and which moments they justify; check sample rank separately from population relevance. Explain whether the coefficient is a constant structural parameter, a projection, or a local effect under a potential-outcome design. A change in weighting does not repair an unidentified causal interpretation.

For inference, name the independent sampling units, score, covariance estimator, rank condition and approximation regime. Preserve structural residuals and every preliminary covariance choice. Report the full test-inversion set, including emptiness or no finite bound; report J as a joint specification diagnostic with its degrees of freedom and limitations. Pre-specify or disclose instrument selection and specification search. An instrument chosen because it produces a desired result raises a separate selection problem that none of the fixed-instrument calculations here solves.

Sources, proof boundaries and next study

The four primary sources are scoped method references: Chernozhukov and Fernández-Val's MIT14.382 Spring 2017 lectures 2–4, and Imbens and Angrist's March 1994 Econometrica paper. The source record identifies the actually inspected pages and equation cautions. Our examples, numerical data, derivations and exercises are original; no empirical instrument from those readings is adopted as valid for a new setting.

The linear iid theorem, structural-score covariance replacement, efficient covariance comparison, J projection, bounded nonlinear argument, exact Gaussian candidate test and binary compliance decomposition are proved here. The prerequisite scalar CLT and finite-dimensional characterization remain the expressly inherited probability results. We have not proved generic dependent GMM, many-instrument asymptotics, a general uniform weak-identification theorem, CUE equivalence, selection-adjusted inference or transport of LATE to a new population. Those require their own assumptions and evidence.
