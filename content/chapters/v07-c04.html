Capability and prerequisites

After this chapter, you can follow a long-lived asset from purchase through use, a revised estimate, impairment and disposal; reconcile its carrying amount to both profit and cash; and distinguish an operating asset from an investment property or financial claim. Retain the debit/credit conventions of accounting as measurement, the three-statement identities and the separation of recognition from settlement in working-capital accounting. Present-value arithmetic comes from Volume 1.

A machine can consume cash before it generates expense. An impairment can reduce profit without a payment. A sale can bring in more cash than its reported gain. These differences have a common explanation: cash records settlement, while accounting also records which resources remain and which have been consumed or lost. An asset register connects the events.

Scope and units. All companies and numbers below are original synthetic cases. Amounts are kCU, thousands of fictional currency units; 1 kCU = 1,000 CU. We stipulate a historical-cost convention for owned service-business assets, except for an explicitly separate investment-property comparison. There are no taxes, borrowing, foreign exchange, leases, inventory, revaluation reserves or held-for-sale classifications in the main case. Depreciation there is a period expense; a manufacturer's depreciation may instead enter inventory cost before becoming cost of sales. We use selected methods from the dated primary texts linked below, including December 2022 compilations of AASB standards incorporating IAS provisions. These are teaching conventions frozen for the examples, including the illustrative 2027 disposal, not a claim about every jurisdiction's current rules. A real filing requires its applicable framework and reporting-date version.

1. Which expenditure creates an asset?

Capitalization records an eligible expenditure as part of an asset's cost. It does not mean that cash was saved or that the expenditure is economically wise. Our machine must be a controlled productive resource with probable future benefits and reliably measurable eligible cost. Its purchase price is reduced by the negotiated discount. Freight, installation and necessary testing put it in the condition and location needed for use. Training, a launch campaign, general administration and ordinary maintenance are separate period costs in this case. The classification follows the nature of the expenditure, not the desired profit number. These distinctions are anchored in AASB 116, paragraphs 7–20.

Ask three questions in order: what resource exists, what measurement rule applies to that resource, and which costs satisfy that rule? “This will benefit us later” is insufficient by itself: a well-trained workforce can be valuable without the training bill qualifying as a separately recognized asset. Conversely, paying for equipment on credit would still create the asset and a payable; cash payment is not a recognition requirement. We use cash purchases to isolate the asset lifecycle, not to define it.

A substantial replacement can create an eligible new component while the replaced component is removed. A routine repair can merely maintain the existing service potential. A large invoice is not automatically an improvement. Keep the engineering description, acceptance date, supplier documents and old-component record. Significant components with different consumption patterns require attention separately. We omit dismantling obligations, borrowing costs and complex construction so they cannot silently become plugs in the cost total.

Worked example 1: Alder's acquisition and first close

Alder Instruments opens 2025 with cash and contributed equity of 200, and nothing else. It pays an invoice of 120 less a discount of 12, freight of 6, installation of 4 and required testing of 2. All prepare one machine for use. It also pays training 3, launch advertising 5, general administration 2 and routine maintenance 4, all consumed in 2025. The machine becomes available on 1 April 2025. There is no output or sale from testing. Its estimated useful life is 72 months from that date and its residual value is 12. Straight-line allocation is appropriate. During 2025 Alder earns and collects service revenue 45 and incurs and pays other operating costs 20; that 20 excludes the separately listed costs.

Eligible cost is 120 − 12 + 6 + 4 + 2 = 120. Period costs from the acquisition list are 3 + 5 + 2 + 4 = 14. Total cash paid for that list is 134. Record Dr Machine 120, Dr the four expense accounts totaling 14, Cr Cash 134. Grouping entries here is for explanation; the register retains each payment and readiness date.

The amount allocated over use is 120 − 12 = 108. Monthly depreciation is 108/72 = 1.5. April through December gives nine months, so depreciation is 13.5: Dr Depreciation expense 13.5; Cr Accumulated depreciation 13.5. Closing machine carrying amount is 120 − 13.5 = 106.5.

Profit is 45 − 20 − 14 − 13.5 = −2.5. Operating cash flow (CFO) is 45 − 20 − 14 = 11. Equipment investment uses 120, so the investing contribution (CFI) is −120. Cash closes at 200 + 11 − 120 = 91. Assets are 91 + 106.5 = 197.5, equal to equity 200 − 2.5. There is no financing flow during the year: opening equity was already funded. Capitalizing the eligible machine cost postpones its allocation to expense; it does not remove the purchase from the cash account.

2. The asset register is a stock-flow reconciliation

Let G be gross historical cost, A credit-normal accumulated depreciation and I a separate credit-normal accumulated impairment balance. We retain the impairment account separately to make this example auditable; this is not a mandatory presentation format for every framework. The carrying amount is B = G − A − I. Neither A nor I is money held for replacement, and neither is a liability to a creditor.

G′ = G + capitalized additions − gross cost removed
A′ = A + current depreciation − accumulated depreciation removed
I′ = I + impairment losses − reversals − accumulated impairment removed
B′ = G′ − A′ − I′.

These equations follow from the debit/credit entries. A new cash purchase increases G and reduces Cash. A depreciation charge increases expense and A by equal amounts. An impairment increases expense and I. On disposal, remove the gross cost and all associated contra balances, not just whichever number is visible on the balance sheet. If an asset register's ending B agrees with the balance sheet but the removed cost and contra balances remain in its detail, the register is still wrong.

For an unchanged straight-line estimate with cost C, residual R and useful life N months, where N > 0 and 0 ≤ R ≤ C, the monthly charge solves N d = C − R. Thus d = (C − R)/N. After m months, 0 ≤ m ≤ N, carrying amount is C − m d. At m = N it is R, not necessarily zero. The derivation is an allocation identity, not a valuation theorem. A fully depreciated machine can still work; a high book value does not guarantee productive usefulness. Review estimates instead of mechanically extending a formula beyond its domain.

The charge begins when the asset is available for its intended use. Holding it idle does not by itself suspend a time-based charge. Land and buildings are separated: ordinary land may have an unlimited life, while some land-related resources have a finite life. Residual value and life are estimates requiring review. These boundaries, including a zero charge if the residual reaches or exceeds carrying amount, are set out in AASB 116, paragraphs 50–62A. The noncash cost-allocation logic is also illustrated in Kothari's MIT lecture; its 2004 teaching examples are not used as current reporting rules.

Usage and component allocation

If use is a defensible measure of consumption, expected lifetime usage H > 0 gives cost per unit (C − R)/H. A period using h units gets a charge (C − R)h/H. For example, cost 48, residual 8 and expected use of 10,000 hours imply 0.004 kCU per hour, or 4 CU per hour. A 1,500-hour period consumes 6 kCU; a zero-hour period consumes zero under this usage model. This differs from a time-based model in which aging continues while the machine stands idle. Sales revenue mixes quantities, output prices and other inputs, so it is not interchangeable with physical usage.

The initial usage schedule has the domain Σh ≤ H. If actual or expected usage changes enough to violate it, revise the remaining estimate before charging more than the depreciable amount. Do not create a negative carrying amount by blindly multiplying an obsolete rate. In the lab, an over-total usage path is rejected to force that review; the program is not choosing a replacement estimate for management.

New information changes the remaining allocation

Suppose the carrying amount just before a revision is B, the revised residual is R*, and revised remaining life is N* months. Under our straight-line convention, the next m months, with 0 ≤ m ≤ N*, receive max(B − R*, 0)m/N*. Past charges remain recorded. If R* > B, the charge is zero rather than negative, and no asset appreciation is recorded by this formula.

A revised life based on new evidence differs from discovering that the original calculation used the wrong invoice or omitted a known fact. The former is an estimate change; the latter may be an error requiring a different correction process. AASB 108, paragraphs 32–40, explains prospective treatment of estimates and its distinction from error correction. Changing the measurement basis is also different from changing a life estimate.

3. Impairment asks whether the remaining amount is recoverable

Depreciation allocates an eligible amount according to expected consumption. Impairment responds to a loss of recoverability that the normal allocation has not captured. Disposal removes an asset because control or the relevant resource is gone. They are three different events and can all occur during one lifecycle.

For our independently cash-generating machine under the stipulated cost model, recoverable amount RA is the larger of fair value less disposal costs and value in use. The impairment loss is max(B − RA, 0), recognized after scheduled depreciation. A higher RA alone does not write an unimpaired cost-model asset up. If cash inflows cannot be assessed independently, the relevant cash-generating unit must be identified; one cannot simply assign arbitrary corporate profits to each machine. Ordinary assets are checked for impairment indicators; some assets require annual testing. These boundaries come from AASB 136, paragraphs 8–22 and 59–63.

Value in use concerns discounted future net cash flows attributable to use and ultimate disposal. Our main value-in-use number is supplied; it is not estimated from the book amount. A valuation must keep currency, nominal versus real amounts, dates and risk treatment consistent. For example, two end-year net receipts of 30 and 36.3 discounted at 10% have present value 30/1.1 + 36.3/1.1² = 630/11 ≈ 57.273 kCU. A 60 carrying amount would imply a loss of 30/11 if the alternative net sale value were 55. Future depreciation is not an additional cash payment to deduct from those already net operating receipts.

Worked example 2: a revised life, impairment and sale

Continue Alder. On 1 January 2026, new technical information supports a revised remaining life of 48 months and residual value 10.5. The 2025 entries were correct when made. The 106.5 opening carrying amount therefore gives a monthly charge (106.5 − 10.5)/48 = 2, or 24 for 2026. Accumulated depreciation becomes 13.5 + 24 = 37.5, leaving 82.5 before impairment. We do not recalculate 2025 as if the revised information had always been known.

At the end of 2026, adverse demand evidence warrants an impairment test. The machine's relevant cash inflows are independently assessable. Fair value less disposal costs is supplied as 62 and value in use as 64.5. Thus RA = 64.5 and the loss is 82.5 − 64.5 = 18. Record Dr Impairment expense 18; Cr Accumulated impairment 18. The 36 remaining months and residual 10.5 then imply a future monthly charge (64.5 − 10.5)/36 = 1.5.

In 2026 Alder earns and collects 60 and pays consumed operating costs of 25. Profit is 60 − 25 − 24 − 18 = −7. CFO is 35; cash increases from 91 to 126. Assets are cash 126 plus machine 64.5 = 190.5, equal to opening equity 197.5 less the loss of 7. The impairment has not sent 18 to anyone's bank account.

Alder uses the machine for the first six months of 2027, recognizing depreciation 9. Just before sale, G = 120, A = 46.5, I = 18 and B = 55.5. On 30 June control passes to a buyer who pays 60 in cash; Alder pays a directly attributable disposal fee of 1.5 on the same date. There was no earlier held-for-sale classification, other impairment or estimate change. Net proceeds are 58.5, giving gain 58.5 − 55.5 = 3.

One combined presentation of the simultaneous settlement is Dr Cash 58.5, Dr Accumulated depreciation 46.5, Dr Accumulated impairment 18; Cr Machine 120, Cr Disposal gain 3. Debits and credits both total 123. The cash ledger separately records +60 and −1.5. Their net investing contribution is 58.5; that arithmetic does not authorize net presentation in every real cash-flow statement. Remove all three machine balances. The 60 cash receipt is not 60 of revenue, and the 3 gain is not an additional receipt.

First-half service receipts are 30 and consumed cash operating costs 10. Profit is 30 − 10 − 9 + 3 = 14. CFO is 20, also obtained as 14 + 9 − 3. Closing cash is 126 + 20 + 58.5 = 204.5; machine balances are zero, so this equals equity 190.5 + 14.

A reversal is bounded

For a non-goodwill asset with qualifying changed recoverability estimates, our cost-model reversal increases B only up to min(RA, Bno impairment). Here Bno impairment is the amount that would remain after depreciation had the earlier impairment not occurred. Passage of time alone is insufficient evidence. The ceiling prevents a recovery entry from becoming an unrestricted revaluation. Goodwill impairment is not reversed under this cited convention. See AASB 136, paragraphs 109–124. Reallocate the resulting carrying amount over the remaining life; do not restore the old monthly charge without checking it.

For example, if current B = 37.5, the no-impairment amount is 60 and a substantiated new RA is 90, the reversal is 60 − 37.5 = 22.5, not 52.5. If RA were 35, the reversal would be zero and a further impairment question would arise. The reversal formula is not a replacement for that separate downside test.

Why lifetime expense reconciles

Let cumulative depreciation be D, net impairment charges after reversals be J, and net sale proceeds be S. With no additions after the original cost C, pre-sale carrying amount is C − D − J, and the gain is g = S − (C − D − J). Rearranging gives D + J − g = C − S. Thus changing the timing of valid cost allocation cannot change the total asset expense once the asset is fully removed and cash proceeds are fixed. It can change individual years' profit and ratios.

For Alder, 46.5 + 18 − 3 = 61.5 = 120 − 58.5. Its pre-asset operating margin over all periods is 135 − 55 − 14 = 66. Cumulative profit is 66 − 61.5 = 4.5, equal to its cash increase from 200 to 204.5. This equality uses the case's absence of other remaining assets, liabilities, owner flows and taxes. It is an undiscounted reconciliation, not a claim that expense timing has no tax, financing or valuation consequences elsewhere.

4. Intangibles: no physical substance does not mean no boundary

A purchased patent or software right can be an identifiable controlled asset. The life used for allocation reflects the expected benefit period within the enforceable-right boundary. For a finite-life asset, amortization is the counterpart of depreciation and begins at availability for use. An indefinite life means no foreseeable limit established by the evidence, not immortality: no regular amortization is charged under the cited convention, but impairment testing and reassessment remain. See AASB 138, paragraphs 88–110.

Our development cases require evidence of technical feasibility, intent to finish, ability to use or sell, probable benefits, adequate resources and reliable cost measurement. Research is expensed; eligible development cost starts only when all relevant criteria are met. Earlier expense is not later revived as an asset. These boundaries are from paragraphs 54–71. Labeling a project “strategic” supplies none of the missing evidence. Acquired rights, internally generated brands, research and purchased goodwill are not interchangeable categories.

Worked example 3: a purchased software right

A separate firm buys a transferable software license for 24 cash. It is available on 1 July. Legal access lasts 36 months, but documented replacement plans limit expected use to 24 months. Assume the license meets the recognition criteria, residual value is zero, benefit consumption is even and no impairment occurs. Monthly amortization is 24/24 = 1. The six-month charge is 6, leaving a carrying amount of 18. The entry is Dr Amortization expense 6; Cr Accumulated amortization 6. The initial payment remains 24 regardless of whether the rights are consumed over two years or three.

Using the legal maximum of 36 months despite the stipulated 24-month benefit period would produce expense 4 and carrying amount 20. It would overstate this year's profit and asset by 2 under the stated evidence. Calling the asset intangible does not justify an indefinite life. This example assumes no residual market or purchase commitment; it does not establish a positive salvage value for software.

5. “Investment asset” is a purpose description, not one measurement rule

The investment-property distinction and the cost/fair-value policy alternatives here follow AASB 140, paragraphs 5–9 and 30–56. We assume an owned completed property with reliably measurable fair value, no special asset pools and no change of use. The policy is applied consistently, not switched property by property to report the preferred profit. Under the fair-value case, the period's value change enters profit; under our cost case, the building is depreciated. Do not impose both full depreciation and an additional fair-value closing adjustment without specifying how the resulting measurement is supposed to work.

Worked example 4: same rental cash, different measurement

An independent rental business buys property for 80 on 1 January: land 20 and a completed building 60, available immediately. The building's life is 30 years and residual is zero; land has an unlimited life in this case. Annual rent of 5 is earned and collected, and cash running costs of 1 are incurred. No impairment, acquisition fee, debt or tax occurs. Year-end property fair value is supplied as 86.

With a fixed cost policy, depreciation is 60/30 = 2, carrying amount 20 + 58 = 78, and profit is 5 − 1 − 2 = 2. With a fixed fair-value policy under the stated convention, closing property is 86, the gain is 86 − 80 = 6, and profit is 5 − 1 + 6 = 10. There is no separate depreciation charge in this fair-value example. Operating cash is 4 in both, purchase cash is −80, and total cash change is −76. The 8 difference in closing asset and profit is exactly 86 − 78; it creates no cash.

The arithmetic compares two supplied measurement policies for the same rental facts. It does not prove that choosing a fair-value policy makes the real project more profitable or permits arbitrary values. A credible valuation and its uncertainty remain evidence requirements.

A financial claim can increase in carrying amount without a cash coupon

Suppose a separate firm pays 100 at date 0 for a claim paying exactly 121 at the end of year 2 and nothing earlier. There are no fees, default losses, options, foreign exchange effects or changes to terms. Stipulate that its business model is to collect contractual payments and its cash flows meet the principal-and-interest condition for amortized cost. That eligibility boundary is described in the IFRS Foundation's public IFRS 9 overview; it does not mean that every bond, share or fund interest qualifies.

The effective annual yield r solves 100(1 + r)² = 121, so r = 10%. Before maturity the recursion is B′ = B + rB − cash collected. Year 1 interest income is 10, cash collection zero and carrying amount 110. Year 2 interest is 11; the claim reaches 121 immediately before collection, and cash receipt 121 then reduces it to zero. Total interest income 21 equals 121 − 100; recording the entire redemption as new income would count the original investment again. This is discount accretion, not a negative physical depreciation charge. We are reconciling total cash and profit here, not prescribing the operating/investing classification of every financial instrument's cash flows. Debt and lease schedules follow in the next chapter.

6. Accounting allocation and economic depreciation answer different questions

For a cash-generating asset valued using unchanged, certain expectations and a constant discount rate r, let Vt−1 be value just after the previous distribution, ct the next net cash receipt, and Vt value just after that receipt. Present-value arithmetic gives (1 + r)Vt−1 = ct + Vt. Define economic depreciation over this interval as Vt−1 − Vt. Then ct = rVt−1 + economic depreciation. Cash contains a return on the opening value and recovery of value consumed. This decomposition depends on the valuation assumptions; realized value changes can also contain news.

Consider a two-year asset producing net cash 55 after year 1 and 60.5 after year 2, with no terminal salvage. At 10%, V0 = 55/1.1 + 60.5/1.1² = 100. After the first cash receipt, V1 = 60.5/1.1 = 55; after the second, V2 = 0. Economic depreciation is 45 then 55. Cash receipts decompose as 55 = 10 + 45 and 60.5 = 5.5 + 55. A separate straight-line book schedule for cost 100 over two years with zero residual gives 50 then 50. Its year-1 accounting profit from this asset is 55 − 50 = 5, whereas the constant-expected-return valuation decomposition gives 10.

If new information after the first receipt raised the remaining expected receipt from 60.5 to 121, the ex-receipt value at the same rate would instead be 110. The observed change from 100 to 110 would mix aging and favorable news. It would not show that physical wear was negative. The accounting cost schedule, an impairment test and an economic valuation can therefore differ without violating arithmetic. An analyst needs the question, evidence and measurement basis before declaring one number the “true depreciation.”

7. Reproducible lifecycle lab

Download the original inputs, standard-library Python analysis and execution and experiment guide. Keep the files together and run python3 v07-c04-analysis.py. The program prints exact rational JSON, with no network call, installation or file mutation. Dates and elapsed months are fixed to Alder's specified timeline; alternate monetary inputs do not silently change the readiness or disposal date.

Read each row as a reconciliation: cash plus carrying amount must equal equity; profit plus the stated noncash adjustments must reproduce CFO; disposal must remove gross cost and both contra accounts. The output includes gross sale cash and the disposal payment separately. It accepts exact integer or string inputs in kCU and rejects a changed unit label, an impossible life, negative supplied amounts or an unfunded closing cash position under the no-overdraft case.

Before running the experiments, predict their signs. A lower sale receipt reduces lifetime profit and cash. A shorter remaining life accelerates expense but, holding cash fixed and removing all balances on sale, cannot alter the lifetime identity. A higher recoverable amount can eliminate impairment without creating a cost-model write-up. Numerical reconciliation checks arithmetic under supplied inputs; it does not independently verify that management's useful-life evidence, discount rate or valuation is credible.

Teach-back and delayed check

Teach-back task. In four minutes, use Alder to explain why cash, profit and carrying amount differ; why an estimate revision is prospective; and why a gain is smaller than sale proceeds. Include one boundary that would require a different measurement model.

Teach-back benchmark

The purchase puts 120 into an asset while spending cash. In 2025 only 13.5 of that amount is allocated to use, so profit −2.5 differs from CFO 11 and from cash change −109. New information in 2026 reallocates opening carrying amount 106.5 over the revised remaining life; it does not repair an error in 2025. The impairment reduces the remaining amount by 18 with no payment. At sale, net cash 58.5 partly recovers carrying amount 55.5, leaving gain 3. A qualifying financial claim accretes interest, and a fair-value investment property follows its chosen measurement policy: neither should be forced into the machine's depreciation schedule. Cash-flow and asset identities are necessary checks, but evidence must support the classification and estimates.

Delayed check, 7–14 days later. Without the worked solution, keep Alder's depreciation, impairment and service operations unchanged but reduce the 30 June 2027 gross sale receipt to 53, retaining the 1.5 disposal cost. Reconstruct pre-sale carrying amount, gain/loss, current profit, CFO, CFI, ending cash/equity and cumulative profit. Then explain why the original impairment of 18 need not equal the later sale loss.

Delayed-check answer

Pre-sale carrying amount remains 120 − 46.5 − 18 = 55.5. Net cash is 53 − 1.5 = 51.5, giving a disposal loss of 4. Current profit is 30 − 10 − 9 − 4 = 7. CFO remains 20, also 7 + 9 + 4; CFI is 51.5. Ending cash and equity are 126 + 20 + 51.5 = 197.5. Cumulative profit is −2.5 − 7 + 7 = −2.5. The impairment compared the then-current carrying amount with a supplied recoverability estimate on an earlier date; later depreciation and a later sale price answer different dated questions. Their losses need not coincide. The lifetime asset expense now equals 46.5 + 18 + 4 = 68.5 = 120 − 51.5.
