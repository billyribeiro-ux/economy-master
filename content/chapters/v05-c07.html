Choose well when the other side chooses too

Capability: identify equilibria and credible strategies in static and dynamic games. Prerequisite: monopoly, oligopoly and pricing, especially a best response that holds a rival's choice fixed. We also use independent probabilities and expected utility. A market-share plan can be attractive against a passive rival and unattractive against an aggressive one. A threatened response can deter entry on paper and become unattractive once entry occurs. We need to specify both the choices and when a player can revise them.

All numerical games here are original hypothetical models. Unless stated otherwise, payoff entries are thousands of USD of profit for a single quarter, ordered (row player, column player), and firms are risk-neutral. Randomizing then maximizes expected profit. For risk-averse decision makers, entries must instead represent their utility of the consequences; averaging dollar profits would change the problem. Players know the specified game and payoffs. The examples supply no evidence that actual firms know these payoffs, randomize this way or behave according to a particular equilibrium.

Actions, strategies, information and best responses

A finite normal-form game is G = (N,(Ai),(ui)): a finite set of players N, a finite nonempty set Ai of pure choices for each player, and a real payoff ui(a) for every profile a = (a₁,…,an). The notation a−i collects everyone else's choices. In a one-move simultaneous game, a pure choice is an action. When a normal form represents a dynamic game, its pure choices are complete contingent strategies: plans specifying an action at every information set where that player might move, including situations the plan itself prevents from occurring. Normal form is a representation, not a claim that all actions physically occur at once.

Complete information means the structure and payoff functions are common knowledge in our model. Common knowledge means everyone knows them, everyone knows that everyone knows them, and so on. Perfect information instead concerns observation within a dynamic game: a moving player knows the full preceding history. A simultaneous game can have complete information about payoffs without observation of the rival's current move. Private payoff types are deferred to information, signaling and selection.

For fixed a−i, the best-response correspondence is BRi(a−i) = arg maxbᵢ∈Aᵢ ui(bi,a−i). Keep every maximizer when choices tie. A pure Nash equilibrium a* satisfies

ui(a*i,a*−i) ≥ ui(bi,a*−i) for every player i and every bi ∈ Ai.

Each player is choosing a best response to the choices actually prescribed for the others. The deviation holds those other choices fixed; it does not let a deviator choose the rivals' reactions anew. There can be multiple equilibria or no pure equilibrium. Mutual best response does not imply efficiency, an agreement, stability under a learning process or a unique forecast. Individual rationality alone does not impose the correct, mutually consistent beliefs used in an equilibrium.

Dominance is stronger than being best against one opponent

Choice bi strictly dominates ai if ui(bi,a−i) > ui(ai,a−i) for every rival profile. It weakly dominates ai if the inequality is weak everywhere and strict somewhere. We require that strict comparison somewhere; identical choices do not weakly dominate one another under this convention. A probability mixture of a player's other actions can also dominate an action even if no single pure action does.

A strictly dominated choice cannot maximize expected payoff under any belief over the finite rival profiles: its payoff disadvantage has a positive expectation under every probability distribution. It therefore cannot receive positive probability in a Nash equilibrium. Weak dominance has a different boundary: a player can be certain of rival actions at which the two choices tie. A weakly dominated choice can then be a best response and occur in equilibrium.

Iterated strict deletion applies the test to the remaining game after earlier deletions. It preserves every equilibrium of the original finite game: the first deleted choices have zero equilibrium probability, and, inductively, any choice strictly dominated on the remaining rival support also has zero probability. The surviving choices need not themselves form an equilibrium. Interpreting repeated rounds as predictions also requires increasingly strong assumptions about players' knowledge of each other's rationality. Weak deletion can remove equilibria and its order can change the surviving sets.

Muhamet Yildiz's Dominance notes, sections 4.1–4.2, give the strict/weak and mixed-dominance definitions and their connection to beliefs. These are Yildiz's 2012 notes, hosted with MIT's 2025 course; the numerical comparisons here are original.

Worked example 1: expansion without an enforceable agreement

Two firms simultaneously choose to maintain capacity, M, or expand aggressively, A. There are no later rounds, enforceable side payments or binding joint agreements in this game.

Quarterly payoffs, thousands of USD
Firm 1 / Firm 2MA
M(7, 7)(2, 9)
A(9, 2)(4, 4)

For firm 1, A earns 9 rather than 7 against M and 4 rather than 2 against A. A strictly dominates M. The same comparisons apply to firm 2. Thus the unique equilibrium is (A,A), with profits (4,4). This also rules out additional mixed equilibria: neither firm can put positive probability on its strictly dominated M.

Both firms prefer (M,M), where each earns 7. But a firm deviating alone from M to A earns 9, a gain of 2. The mutually preferred outcome is not a Nash equilibrium. A joint agreement or repeated relationship might alter incentives, but would require another model of enforcement or future rewards. The fact that total profit is 14 at (M,M) rather than 8 at equilibrium does not tell us consumer welfare or a complete social objective.

A small weak-deletion trap

Weak dominance with an equilibrium at a tie
Row / ColumnLR
U(2, 2)(2, 0)
D(2, 2)(0, 2)

U weakly dominates D, and L weakly dominates R. Yet both (U,L) and (D,L) are Nash equilibria: at (D,L), row is indifferent and column is indifferent. Delete D first and then L strictly beats R, leaving only (U,L). Delete R first and U and D tie against the only remaining column, so neither can now be deleted by our weak-dominance definition. This order leaves both row actions. The first procedure discarded the original equilibrium (D,L). “Delete every weakly dominated choice and report all equilibria” is therefore an invalid general method.

Mixed strategies and the support test

A mixed strategy σi is a probability distribution over i's pure choices. Its support contains the choices assigned positive probability. A pure strategy is the special distribution putting probability one on one choice. In a mixed Nash profile, different players' randomizations are independent, so the probability of pure profile a is ∏jσj(aj), and expected payoff is

Ui(σ) = Σa[∏jσj(aj)]ui(a).

A player's belief about a rival's action is distinct from the player's own randomization. Equilibrium requires the beliefs used in best responses to agree with the prescribed rival distributions. A randomized action is implemented as one of the available actions; a 40% chance of opening a branch does not mean opening 40% of a branch. A shared random device that correlates players' choices requires a different equilibrium concept.

Fix the rivals' distributions and let vi(bi) be the expected payoff from pure choice bi. Expected payoff from a mixture is Σbᵢσi(bi)vi(bi). Consequently a profile is a mixed Nash equilibrium exactly when every supported choice attains the same maximal payoff, and every excluded choice gives no more, for each player. To prove necessity, shift probability from any supported inferior choice to a maximizing one. The gain is positive. For sufficiency, every alternative mixture is a weighted average of pure payoffs no larger than that maximum. This also proves that checking all pure deviations suffices to check all mixed deviations.

For a proposed two-action support, make each player indifferent by solving for the opponent's probability. Then check that probabilities are nonnegative and sum to one, that supported probabilities are positive, and that excluded actions cannot improve payoff. Indifference equations alone can describe an invalid candidate.

Asuman Ozdaglar's author-hosted Strategic Form Games and Nash Equilibrium, sections 2–3.1, supplies the finite-game definitions, independent mixed-payoff construction and support characterization. The chapter's calculations below apply those conditions directly.

Worked example 2: an action outside the proposed support

Two firms choose incompatible scheduling policies. Row can choose U, D or Idle; column chooses Left or Right. These are labels for discrete policies, not directions of a quantitative marginal change.

Scheduling-policy payoffs, thousands of USD for the quarter
Row / ColumnLeftRight
U(5, 0)(0, 3)
D(0, 4)(2, 0)
Idle(1, 1)(1, 1)

No pure cell passes both best-response tests. Against Left, row uniquely prefers U, but against U, column prefers Right. Against Right, row uniquely prefers D, but against D, column prefers Left. Idle is not optimal against either pure column choice. Initially propose that row mixes between U and D. Let x = P(U) and y = P(Left).

Row's three expected pure payoffs are 5y, 2(1−y) and 1. Equating the first two gives y = 2/7. Column's payoffs, given the proposed row support, are 4(1−x) and 3x. Equating them gives x = 4/7. Thus row uses (4/7,3/7,0) and column uses (2/7,5/7). Row earns 10/7 from both U and D, exceeding Idle's 1; column earns 12/7 from either choice. All support and excluded-action inequalities pass.

The four positive joint probabilities are 8/49, 20/49, 6/49 and 15/49 for (U,Left), (U,Right), (D,Left) and (D,Right). Directly averaging the entries gives row payoff (5·8 + 2·15)/49 = 10/7 and column payoff (3·20 + 4·6)/49 = 12/7, in thousands of USD.

We can also establish uniqueness. The mixture putting 1/3 on U and 2/3 on D gives row 5/3 against Left and 4/3 against Right, strictly exceeding Idle's 1 at both. Idle is strictly dominated by a mixture, although neither U nor D alone dominates it. Thus no equilibrium uses Idle. In the remaining 2×2 game, a pure choice by either player forces a unique rival best response and then a profitable deviation by the first player. Both must mix, and the two indifference equations have the unique solution above.

What the existence theorem does and does not provide

Finite mixed-equilibrium theorem: every game with finitely many players, finite nonempty pure-choice sets and finite real expected-utility payoffs has a Nash equilibrium in independent mixed strategies. A pure equilibrium counts as a degenerate mixed equilibrium. The theorem does not require a pure equilibrium and guarantees neither uniqueness, efficiency nor convergence of behavior to an equilibrium. It does not by itself cover unbounded or continuous action sets such as unrestricted quantities.

The proof route takes the product of probability simplices, a nonempty compact convex set. Expected payoffs are continuous in all probabilities and affine in a player's own probabilities. Best responses have nonempty convex values and the appropriate upper-hemicontinuity property; Kakutani's fixed-point theorem then supplies a profile that belongs to its own best-response set. We invoke that fixed-point theorem here; we do not prove it. The support proof and examples are complete, but they are not a general existence proof. Ozdaglar's section 4.1 states finite existence and explicitly leaves the fixed-point proofs outside that introductory treatment; graduate microeconomic theory is the extension route.

Utility scaling matters for randomization. Replacing each player's payoff by αiui + βi, with αi > 0 constant across all outcomes, preserves all that player's expected-payoff rankings and hence equilibria. A general increasing transformation preserves pure-outcome rankings but can change comparisons between lotteries. Neither utility numbers across different players nor their arbitrary sum automatically measure social welfare.

Histories, information sets and contingent plans

An extensive-form game specifies possible histories of actions, who moves after each nonterminal history, available actions, information at the decision, and payoffs at terminal histories. The empty history is the start. If chance moves are included, its probabilities must also be specified. Our worked trees have finitely many nodes and no chance moves.

An information set groups decision histories that the moving player cannot distinguish. They belong to the same player and offer the same actions. A pure strategy chooses one action at each of that player's information sets: it cannot condition on a history the player cannot observe. With perfect information every information set is a singleton. We assume perfect recall: players do not forget their own earlier actions or previously known information.

A mixed strategy randomizes over complete pure plans. A behavioral strategy instead specifies a local randomization at each information set. We will use pure contingent plans in the entry tree; no equivalence theorem between these two randomization descriptions is needed for its solution. Always write the plan at unreached information sets too. Otherwise a claimed strategy profile is incomplete.

Yildiz's Representation of Games, sections 3.1–3.3, gives the history/information-set and complete-plan representations. The important modeling decision is which histories a player can distinguish, not merely how a tree is drawn.

Subgames, sequential rationality and backward induction

A subgame begins at a decision node that is a singleton information set, includes all its successor nodes, and does not cut an information set: if it contains one node of an information set, it contains the whole set. The whole game is a subgame; a proper subgame is smaller. A subgame-perfect Nash equilibrium (SPNE) is a complete strategy profile whose restriction is a Nash equilibrium in every subgame, including those not reached in play. It is therefore also a Nash equilibrium of the whole game.

Sequential rationality asks for an optimal continuation at every information set, given beliefs about the histories in that set and the other players' continuation strategies. In perfect-information games these histories are known. With imperfect information, subgame perfection alone can leave behavior at some information sets undisciplined, because no proper subgame starts there. Beliefs and stronger refinements will matter in the next chapter. Do not run backward induction separately at two nodes that the moving player cannot distinguish.

Yildiz's Subgame-Perfect Nash Equilibrium, section 11.1, defines subgame closure and equilibrium in every subgame, and explains why that criterion does not fully capture sequential rationality in all imperfect-information games. The chapter here is restricted to the finite complete-information core.

For a finite perfect-information tree, backward induction first chooses an action maximizing the moving player's payoff at each last decision node. Replace each solved continuation by its entire payoff vector; then solve earlier nodes until the root. Retain every maximizing action at a tie. Different tie choices can generate different pure SPNE, and mixtures among tied continuations can add mixed ones. A unique chosen path is not, by itself, a complete strategy profile.

Here is the restricted proof. At a last decision node, the selected maximizing action makes its one-player subgame a Nash equilibrium. Suppose all child subgames have selected equilibrium continuations. If the current mover changes the initial action and later choices, equilibrium within the chosen child means that no later unilateral change can improve on that child's prescribed payoff. Choosing the best of those payoffs therefore blocks any such combined deviation. Other players cannot improve by changing their plans within the reached child; their choices elsewhere do not affect this subgame's outcome. Thus the combined plan is a Nash equilibrium at this node and remains so in every descendant subgame. Induction over the finite tree proves subgame perfection. This proves existence of at least one pure SPNE for this scope; it does not prove uniqueness or extend the argument to an infinite horizon.

Yildiz's Backward Induction, sections 9.1–9.2, develops the finite-tree procedure and its optimality argument. Finiteness and information conditions are substantive assumptions, not drawing conventions.

Worked example 3: a threat, then a changed game

An entrant E chooses Out or Enter. After observed entry, incumbent I chooses Accommodate or Fight. Payoff order is now explicitly (entrant, incumbent), still thousands of USD for the quarter.

Complete entry tree described by histories

Empty historyEOut, Enter
Out(0, 8)Terminal
EnterIAccommodate, Fight
Enter, Accommodate(3, 4)Terminal
Enter, Fight(−2, −1)Terminal

The incumbent's pure strategy specifies its response even when E stays out. In normal form, the Out row gives (0,8) against either incumbent strategy; the Enter row gives (3,4) against Accommodate and (−2,−1) against Fight. There are two pure Nash equilibria. At (Enter,Accommodate), E prefers 3 to 0 and I prefers 4 to −1. At (Out,Fight), E prefers 0 to −2, and I cannot change its payoff of 8 by changing an unreached response.

But the proper subgame after Enter has a unique optimum for I: 4 > −1, so Accommodate. Backward induction then makes E enter, earning 3 rather than 0. The unique SPNE is (Enter,Accommodate). The Fight response in the other Nash equilibrium is a noncredible threat: it fails optimality at the history where it would need to be carried out. Ordinary Nash did not test a deviation conditional on that unreached history.

Now change the game. Before E moves, I may choose No commitment or an observed, irreversible Lock. Lock costs I c = 1 regardless of entry and physically removes Accommodate if entry occurs. After Lock, staying out gives (0,7), while entry leads automatically to (−2,−2). This is a specified technology constraint; a verbal announcement alone does not remove an action.

E stays out after Lock, because 0 > −2. After No commitment, the original entry subgame yields (3,4). At the first node I therefore chooses Lock: 7 > 4. The full SPNE says I chooses Lock and would Accommodate after No commitment followed by entry; E chooses Out after Lock and Enter after No commitment. The realized payoff is (0,7). The unreached Accommodate instruction is still required.

For general c ≥ 0, I compares 8−c under Lock with 4 under No commitment: Lock is optimal when c < 4, No commitment when c > 4, and both are optimal at c = 4. If Lock merely pays a sunk cost but leaves both responses available, I instead Accommodates after entry because 4−c > −1−c. E enters, so paying a positive c has no deterrence benefit and I chooses No commitment. Commitment works in the first version by changing feasible future responses; its success cannot be inferred from the size of a past expenditure alone.

How to assess a strategic explanation

Start by listing who chooses, what each can choose, the order and observability of choices, and the payoff consequences. Ask whether an alleged commitment is observable and costly to reverse in the specified mechanism. Then test all unilateral deviations, preserving off-path plans. If several equilibria survive, report the set or explain the additional selection assumption. If no pure equilibrium exists, investigate mixing before calling the game inconsistent.

Changing a firm's payoffs, revealing a move and making an action irreversible are different changes to the model. A repeated relationship also requires a horizon, discounting and monitoring assumptions; this chapter's one-shot conclusions cannot establish cooperation in every repeated game. Model fit requires evidence on these ingredients and on alternatives. An equilibrium comparison is a conditional deduction, not a causal estimate of a policy or a guarantee about a negotiating opponent.

Complete solutions

  1. Against M, A earns 9 > 7; against A, A earns 4 > 2. Both firms have the same strict comparisons. Their only equilibrium, pure or mixed, is (A,A); strict dominance excludes all probability on M. Both would gain 3 by jointly switching to (M,M), but each could then gain another 2 by unilaterally switching to A. After the transformation, (M,M) gives (16,16) and (A,A) gives (7,7). Every expected-payoff difference is multiplied by positive 3, so best responses and all equilibria are unchanged. The sum is greatest at (M,M): 14 versus 11 at either off-diagonal and 8 at (A,A). Maximizing it allows a joint choice absent from the noncooperative game. Moreover these are firm profits, with no consumer or external effects modeled; they do not constitute a full welfare measure.

  2. Against L both row actions maximize at 2; against R row strictly prefers U. Against U column prefers L (2 > 0); against D both column actions give 2. The pure equilibria are exactly (U,L) and (D,L). Delete D first because U is weakly better. With only U, delete R because L strictly beats it; only (U,L) remains. Delete R first because L is weakly better. With only L, U and D tie at 2, so neither dominates under the required strict-somewhere convention, leaving both (U,L) and (D,L). At (D,L), D's disadvantage occurs only against R, assigned probability zero. D can therefore be a best response although weakly dominated. The first deletion order removes (D,L).

  3. For fixed rival distributions let m = maxbᵢvi(bi). If a proposed mixture puts probability ε > 0 on a choice with payoff v < m, moving that ε to a maximizing pure choice raises expected payoff by ε(m−v) > 0. An equilibrium thus supports only maximizers. Conversely, if every supported choice yields m, the player's payoff is m and no alternative mixture can exceed m, since it averages pure payoffs bounded by m. Applying this to every player proves the characterization and sufficiency of pure deviations.

    U gives 0 < 1 against Right, and D gives 0 < 1 against Left, so neither dominates Idle. The mixture (1/3,2/3,0) gives 5/3 > 1 against Left and 4/3 > 1 against Right. Its advantage over Idle is therefore strictly positive under any column distribution. More generally, a strict dominator has a positive difference at every rival profile; a weighted average with total weight one remains positive. Replacing any positive probability on the dominated action with that dominating choice or mixture raises payoff, contradicting best response.

  4. With x = P(U), y = P(Left), row's support indifference is 8y = 2(1−y), giving y = 1/5. Column's remains 4(1−x) = 3x, giving x = 4/7. Both probabilities are interior. Row's payoff is 8/5, strictly exceeding Idle's 1; column's is 12/7. Idle is still strictly dominated by the mixture (1/3 U,2/3 D): it gives 8/3 against Left and 4/3 against Right. In the remaining 2×2 game neither player can be pure in equilibrium, by the same strict best-response cycle as before; the indifference solution is unique. Row's mixing probability is fixed by column's payoff indifference, while column's probability is fixed by row's indifference. The unchanged x is a feature of this support and these payoffs, not a universal own-payoff comparative-statics rule.

  5. The old profile gives row 10/7 from U and D, below Idle's 3/2, so it fails. If row plays Idle with certainty, column earns 1 from either action and may choose any y = P(Left) satisfying 5y ≤ 3/2 and 2(1−y) ≤ 3/2. These require 1/4 ≤ y ≤ 3/10. For every such y, row optimizes by choosing Idle and column is indifferent, so the whole interval is equilibrium. The endpoints retain row ties but do not permit extra equilibrium row mixing.

    To see exhaustiveness, if U has positive probability and D has zero, column strictly prefers Right, because its gain from Right equals three times the positive U probability; row then strictly prefers Idle to U. If D has positive probability and U has zero, column strictly prefers Left, and row strictly prefers Idle to D. If both U and D have positive probability, row's indifference requires y = 2/7, giving them 10/7 < 3/2, again impossible. These cases exhaust all non-Idle row supports. There is no pure equilibrium because the allowed column probabilities lie strictly between zero and one.

  6. The normal form has columns Accommodate and Fight. The Out row is (0,8),(0,8); the Enter row is (3,4),(−2,−1). The pure equilibria are (Enter,Accommodate) and (Out,Fight). If E enters with any positive probability, I strictly prefers Accommodate: the expected advantage is 5 times that probability. Thus I must Accommodate with certainty, making E strictly prefer Enter; this gives the first equilibrium.

    If E stays Out with certainty, I is indifferent over its unused response and may mix. E's payoff from Enter would be 3(1−z)−2z = 3−5z; Out yields zero. Hence every profile with E certainly Out and z ∈ [3/5,1] is a Nash equilibrium. At z = 3/5 E is indifferent, but positive entry probability would make I strictly prefer Accommodate, so it does not generate an additional mixed family. These profiles plus (Enter,Accommodate) are the complete equilibrium set. In the subgame after entry, I strictly prefers Accommodate, requiring z = 0 there. Only (Enter,Accommodate) is subgame-perfect.

  7. In the irreversible version, E chooses Enter after No commitment and Out after Lock. I chooses Accommodate after No commitment followed by entry; after Lock it has no response choice because Fight is forced. Initially I chooses Lock for 0 ≤ c < 4, No commitment for c > 4, and either or any mixture of them at c = 4. These continuation plans apply even if the corresponding initial choice is unused. Payoffs are (0,8−c) under Lock and (3,4) under No commitment.

    In the reversible version I Accommodates after entry following either initial choice, because the payoff advantage of accommodating is 5, including after the cost is sunk. E therefore enters after either choice. Initial No commitment yields I 4; paying c yields 4−c. No commitment is uniquely optimal for c > 0; both initial choices and their mixtures are optimal at c = 0. A useful factual inquiry asks whether the alleged commitment observably eliminates accommodation or can be reversed, rather than merely whether it costs money. A complete critique identifies observability, feasible future responses, the cost's timing and the payoff assumptions; it does not assume that a press announcement enforces a physical constraint.

  8. In the simultaneous game, the pure equilibria are (A,a) and (B,b). Let x = P(A), y = P(a). Row's indifference is 6y = 2(1−y), giving y = 1/4. Column's is 3x = 5(1−x), giving x = 5/8. The mixed payoffs are 3/2 for row and 15/8 for column. Any nondegenerate equilibrium must mix both players: a pure rival action has a unique best response. Hence these three profiles exhaust the simultaneous equilibrium set.

    When column observes, its pure plans are (a,a), (a,b), (b,a), (b,b), listing the action after A first and after B second. It uniquely chooses a after A (3 > 0) and b after B (5 > 0). Row then chooses A because 6 > 2. The unique SPNE is row A and column's plan (a,b), yielding (6,3).

    Without observation, column's nodes after A and B belong to one information set. It has only the pure strategies “a at that set” and “b at that set,” not a contingent choice distinguishing A from B. The normal form is the original simultaneous matrix. There is no proper subgame: neither column node is a singleton information set. Subgame perfection therefore imposes only Nash equilibrium of the whole game, preserving both pure equilibria and the mixed equilibrium. Payoffs can remain common knowledge throughout; lack of observation is a distinct information restriction.

Teach-back and Delayed check

Teach-back: explain to a colleague why a probability that makes you indifferent is chosen by your opponent, and why a Nash threat at an unreached history can fail subgame perfection. Use one payoff comparison and write one complete contingent plan. A satisfactory explanation distinguishes best response from dominance, includes the excluded-action test, and names the information or commitment assumption needed for the dynamic conclusion.
