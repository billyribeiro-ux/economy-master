A country's spending, production and income reports appear to describe three different things. Properly constructed, they describe the same current production from three directions. The hard part is deciding what belongs in that production, when it occurred, whose income it generated, and how to separate changing quantities from changing prices.

Capability. Reconcile production, expenditure and income accounts; construct and interpret real measures; distinguish production, purchasing power, wealth and welfare. Prerequisites are the mathematics, statistics and microeconomics of Volumes 2, 3 and 5. In particular, an accounting identity is not a causal model. All numerical economies below are original teaching examples, with exact records and no statistical discrepancy unless stated. Currency amounts are millions of a common currency per accounting period; physical units and dates are stated separately.

What production the account measures

Gross domestic product (GDP) measures the value of final goods and services produced within the domestic economy during a specified period. “Domestic” concerns the location of production under the accounting residence rules, not the nationality of owners. “Gross” means before subtracting consumption of fixed capital. “Product” includes services, including specified nonmarket and imputed services. “Final” prevents counting an input once when it is sold and again inside the product it helps make.

A factory's component purchases are intermediate consumption; a machine used across periods is fixed investment. The distinction concerns the use of a product, not its physical appearance. Flour bought by a bakery is intermediate; flour bought for household consumption is final. A newly produced house is fixed investment; its land is an existing nonproduced asset. Housing services then flow over time, with owner-occupied housing services imputed in the accounts. A used house's resale does not recreate the house, although newly supplied brokerage services are current production.

A purchase of a bond or an existing share is a financial transaction, not GDP investment. It can finance production, but the financing and the produced asset are separate entries. A government pension payment transfers purchasing power; it does not itself purchase current production. If the recipient buys a haircut, the haircut is consumption. Government purchases of services and newly produced assets enter the expenditure account. A change in asset ownership without a newly supplied service does not create output.

These boundaries and the three accounting approaches follow the inspected production, expenditure and income sections of BEA's NIPA Handbook, Chapter 2, updated December 2024. The definitions here distinguish the general accounting logic from U.S. presentation conventions. For example, U.S. expenditure tables include government gross investment within government spending, whereas some model notation includes all investment in I. Always check the table's definitions before substituting numbers.

One production flow, three views

For producer j, gross value added is gross output minus intermediate consumption. Gross output includes current output added to inventory, even if it has not yet sold. Summing gross output across all producers double-counts domestic intermediates. Summing value added removes those internal transactions: the seller's output and the buyer's intermediate input cancel.

Gross value addedj = outputj − intermediate consumptionj.

Prices must be consistent. If industry value added is reported at basic prices, add taxes less subsidies on products to obtain GDP at market prices. Other taxes on production, such as a production-related property tax under the relevant classification, belong inside the value-added income allocation. Do not add the same tax twice. Our first example assumes no taxes or subsidies on products and no trade or transport margins. Its stated output and intermediate values are therefore directly comparable; its production taxes are other taxes on production, net of subsidies.

For expenditure, let C be household and associated private final consumption, I private gross fixed investment plus the change in private inventories, G government consumption and gross investment, X exports, and M imports. Under this convention:

GDP = C + I + G + X − M.

Why subtract imports? Expenditure categories initially include imported goods and services. Subtraction removes their foreign-produced content. An imported machine costing 100 can raise I by 100 and M by 100, with zero direct addition to domestic production. Imports are not an independent penalty mechanically reducing GDP while every other entry remains unchanged. The response of domestic output to trade is a behavioral question that requires a model and evidence.

The cancellation can be derived. Total domestic output is sold to domestic intermediate users, domestic final users and foreign users, or placed in inventory. Total intermediate consumption includes both domestic and imported intermediates. Subtracting intermediates from output leaves domestic final production plus exports minus imported intermediates. Final domestic expenditure already includes imported final products, so subtract those too. Imports of both kinds produce the expenditure identity. Inventory accumulation closes the gap between production and sales.

Value added is distributed as compensation of employees, taxes on production and imports less subsidies, and operating surplus or mixed income. Mixed income recognizes that an unincorporated producer's labor and entrepreneurial return may not be separately observable. Distinguishing gross from net surplus gives:

Gross domestic income (GDI) = compensation + net production/import taxes + net operating surplus and mixed income + consumption of fixed capital.

Consumption of fixed capital (CFC) is the measured using-up of fixed assets through ordinary wear, aging and expected obsolescence. It is not necessarily a firm's tax or historical-cost depreciation. Gross output supports both net income and replacement of capital consumed in producing it. Catastrophic losses and holding gains require separate stock-reconciliation entries, not ordinary CFC or current production.

Domestic, national, gross, net and measured

Net domestic product is NDP = GDP − CFC. To move from domestic production to income accruing to residents, add primary income received from abroad and subtract primary income paid abroad. Primary income includes relevant cross-border labor and investment income; it is not the same as exports less imports. Let NPI denote this net primary income. In exactly reconciled accounts, gross national income is GNI = GDP + NPI, and net national income is NNI = GNI − CFC.

In Worked example 1, suppose resident primary-income receipts from abroad are 8 and payments abroad are 18. Then NPI = −10, GNI = 160, NDP = 150 and NNI = 140. Foreign ownership of a domestic factory does not remove its production from GDP; its payments to nonresident owners affect the national-income bridge. Resident investment income from foreign production affects GNI without becoming domestic output.

Actual production and income statistics draw on different imperfect observations. In the inspected BEA Chapter 2 discussion of principal aggregates, PDF pages 11–17, the statistical discrepancy is SD = measured GDP − measured GDI. U.S. gross national product is presented as GNP = GDP + NPI, while measured GNI = GDI + NPI = GNP − SD. Thus an empirical GDP-to-GNI bridge must respect the series' discrepancy convention. The conceptual equivalence of income and product does not make independently estimated numbers identical.

Saving requires one further convention check. Split our G into government consumption Gc and government gross investment Ig, and define total domestic investment Iall = I + Ig. The expenditure identity becomes Y = C + Gc + Iall + X − M, with Y = GDP. Define gross domestic saving as Sdom = Y − C − Gc; then Sdom − Iall = X − M. Deducting government investment as if it were consumption would understate saving. For example, reallocating 4 of the worked economy's government purchases from consumption to fixed investment gives Gc = 16, Ig = 4, Iall = 54 and Sdom = 170 − 135 − 16 = 19; 19 − 54 = −35 still equals net exports.

This is an identity, not proof that reducing consumption causally produces a matching trade surplus. National saving uses residents' disposable income, so cross-border primary income and current transfers must also enter its current-account bridge. We develop those accounts later in this volume; do not relabel net exports as the full current account without the required assumptions. Many models write G for government consumption alone and I for all investment. Their familiar S = Y − C − G formula is consistent with that alternative convention.

Separate price changes from quantity changes

For a positive final-output basket, let pit be the price of product i in period t and qit its quantity. Nominal value is Vt = Σipitqit. Quantities may include quality adjustments: a unit delivering more measured service is not necessarily the same quantity as its predecessor. Changes in V can therefore reflect quantities, prices, or both.

A fixed-base real measure at base-period prices pi0 is Rt0 = Σipi0qit. Its implicit price deflator is Dt = 100Vt/Rt0. This is a price index normalized to 100 in the base period. Nominal growth factors equal the product of real growth and deflator growth factors, so the exact growth-rate identity is

1 + gnominal = (1 + greal)(1 + πdeflator).

Subtracting inflation from nominal growth is an approximation that omits the product term. A GDP deflator concerns domestic production; a consumer price index concerns a defined consumer basket. Imported consumer prices, export prices, investment and government production have different roles in the two measures. Deflating every income or expenditure series by whichever inflation number is convenient changes the economic question.

Fixed prices make components additive but can use increasingly unrepresentative weights. A chain index updates the comparison weights along a sequence of adjacent periods. For two adjacent dates, called 0 and 1 just within this comparison, the Laspeyres quantity link values both quantity baskets at old prices; the Paasche link uses new prices:

QL = Σp0q1/Σp0q0,  QP = Σp1q1/Σp1q0,  QF = √(QLQP).

The Fisher quantity link is their geometric mean. Its price counterpart is PF = √(PLPP), where PL = Σp1q0/Σp0q0 and PP = Σp1q1/Σp0q1. Multiplying the four ratios cancels the two cross-valuations, proving

(QFPF)² = (V1/V0)²,  hence QFPF = V1/V0.

All sums and links here are positive, so taking the positive square root is legitimate. Starting a quantity index at J0 = 100, chain it by Jt = Jt−1QF,t. To express it in reference-period-r currency units, use CDtr = VrJt/Jr. Multiplying every level by a constant changes the reference scale without changing growth. Changing the weights of a fixed-base index, by contrast, generally changes measured growth.

The Fisher links, chaining, reference scaling and nonadditivity follow the actual formulas and discussion in BEA NIPA Handbook Chapter 4, PDF pages 18–21 and 26–28, December 2023 version. The examples below derive ideal positive-basket annual indexes; they do not reproduce the agency's full quarterly benchmarking or special treatment of net flows. Applying these square-root formulas blindly to a negative inventory or net-export series is invalid. Use the agency's appropriate aggregate method and published contribution series.

Its chain level divided by the aggregate chain level is about 480/340.295 > 1, which is not a valid expenditure share. Rebasing the aggregate chain series at period 1 multiplies every level by 320/281.709; it leaves both adjacent growth rates unchanged and does not repair nonadditivity in every other period.

Production volume and real income can move differently

“Real income” needs a stated deflator and question. Domestic production volume asks how much the economy produces after removing output-price changes. Purchasing power asks how much a specified income can buy. The terms of trade compare export prices with import prices. When imports become dearer relative to exports, unchanged domestic production may purchase fewer foreign goods.

Worked example 3: unchanged production, fewer imports affordable

A small economy produces 100 physical units of wheat, consumes 40 domestically and exports 60. Wheat's price is 1 currency unit throughout. A distinct imported consumption good initially costs 2, so the export receipts of 60 purchase 30 imported units. There is no investment, government, foreign income or external borrowing. Consumption spending is domestic wheat 40 plus imported goods 60 = 100; exports and imports both equal 60, so GDP = 100 + 60 − 60 = 100.

Hold wheat production, export quantity and its price fixed, and let the import price rise to 3. The same receipts now buy 20 imported units. Consumption expenditure remains 40 + 60 = 100 and GDP remains 100. Domestic production volume is unchanged, but the feasible consumption bundle loses 10 imported units. Expressed in imported-good units, nominal GDP's command over that good falls from 100/2 = 50 to 100/3 ≈ 33.333. These are purchasing-power units, not a physical sum of wheat and imports. The terms-of-trade ratio falls from 1/2 to 1/3. This is a deliberately fixed-production comparison, not a prediction of optimal responses or a complete equilibrium trade model.

Terminology varies. In the inspected BEA principal-aggregates discussion, “real GDI” uses the GDP implicit deflator, while command-basis GDP deflates nominal GDP by the gross domestic purchases price measure to reflect purchasing power. Related international accounting presentations use different labels for terms-of-trade-adjusted real income. Read the construction, not just a series name. Our imported-good-unit comparison is transparent but is not asserted to be the agency's comprehensive command-basis aggregate.

A stock has a date; a flow has a period

Wealth and capital are stocks measured at a date. Output, investment and income are flows during an interval. A GDP flow of 100 per year cannot be added to a capital stock of 500 without specifying the investment retained, capital consumed and valuation changes. Nor does buying financial claims necessarily increase aggregate produced capital.

For a stylized single homogeneous physical capital good, let Kt be units at the start of period t, δ ∈ [0,1] the fraction of that opening stock consumed during the period, and It units of gross new capital available at the next date. With no other losses,

Kt+1 = (1 − δ)Kt + It.

This timing is the interface to the next chapter's growth model. With Kt = 100, δ = 0.1 and It = 15, closing physical capital is 105. It rises by 5 units, not by gross investment 15. Heterogeneous official capital measures require their own aggregation and asset-lifetime methods; do not apply this physical equation directly to independently chained currency series as if additivity were guaranteed.

Suppose the replacement price is 2 at the opening date, rises to 3 before all the period's capital use and acquisitions, then stays there. Opening capital value is 200; closing value is 315. The increase of 115 decomposes into a holding gain of 100 on the opening stock and net capital formation valued at the transaction price: 45 of additions minus 30 of CFC = 15. A price gain did not create 100 extra units of capital or GDP. If a disaster separately destroys capital, record that loss in other volume changes rather than quietly raising ordinary depreciation.

Inventory changes likewise require current-production valuation. Suppose old inventory carried at 10 rises in price to 12 and is then sold to households for final consumption, with no new handling or retail service. Consumption is 12, the withdrawal from inventory is −12 at the current price, and their GDP contributions cancel. The 2 holding gain is not current production. Comparing the opening and closing book balances without separating revaluation would misclassify the gain.

Measurement discipline before economic judgment

GDP is useful because it applies a common production boundary and reconciles many records. It is not a complete welfare ranking. Much unpaid household work is outside the measured production boundary, although specified own-use and housing imputations are included. Environmental damages, leisure, security, distribution and the value of health outcomes are not summarized by market production. Public nonmarket services often require input-based valuation; a higher recorded cost alone does not prove a better outcome. Disaster rebuilding can raise current construction while wealth remains below its pre-disaster path.

Real GDP per person divides a volume measure by population. It is an average production measure, not median household disposable income, consumption utility, or the income of residents after foreign payments. Comparisons across countries also require a consistent price-level conversion: translating every economy at a volatile market exchange rate need not compare domestic purchasing power. Later chapters develop exchange rates and international comparisons.

An empirical report should record the release and vintage, geographic coverage, frequency, currency and scale, seasonal treatment, nominal or real construction, reference period and population denominator. Separate a quarter-on-quarter rate from its annualized version: a quarterly growth rate g implies (1 + g)4 − 1 when compounded for four quarters. An annualized quarterly level is also a scaling convention, not four quarters of additional output. Revisions and a GDP–GDI discrepancy represent measurement uncertainty even when an accounting identity is exact.

We cite the actual December 2024 concepts and December 2023 methods documents; these dates do not establish that every later methodological change is incorporated. This chapter introduces stable methods and synthetic examples, not an invented October 2026 data release.

Complete exercise solutions

  1. Farm value added is 80 − 20 = 60; manufacturer value added is 200 − (80 + 20) = 100. GDP is 160. C = 90 + 15 = 105, I = 50 + 10 = 60, G = 20, X = 30 and M = 20 + 20 + 15 = 55. Thus 105 + 60 + 20 + 30 − 55 = 160. Compensation totals 100, CFC 20, net production taxes 10 and net surplus 30, again totaling 160; each industry's income entries also reconcile. NPI = 12 − 22 = −10 gives GNI = 150, NDP = 160 − 20 = 140 and NNI = 150 − 20 = 130. Neither the 80 domestic intermediate nor the 40 imported intermediates is an additional final-use item.

  2. The old goods sell for 25, so C = 25. Current-price inventory investment is new additions 30 minus withdrawals 25 = 5. GDP = 25 + 5 = 30, matching current production. Opening inventory is 20 and closing inventory 30, a value change of 10. It equals inventory investment 5 plus holding gain 5. Treating the balance change of 10 as investment would overstate GDP by that gain.

  3. I rises by 105 and M by 100, so the direct GDP addition is 5, the domestic installation output and value added. The foreign machine is present in I and removed through M. The loan exchanges financial claims and finances the acquisition; it is not another machine or service. A separately charged newly produced bank service would require its own entry, but none is specified here. The statement holds other production fixed and does not assert a complete causal effect of the borrowing.

  4. V0 = 2·30 + 3·20 = 120 and V1 = 3·40 + 2·30 = 180. The two cross-valuations are p0·q1 = 170 and p1·q0 = 130. Therefore QL = 17/12, QP = 18/13 and QF = √(51/26) ≈ 1.400549. PL = 13/12, PP = 18/17 and PF = √(39/34) ≈ 1.071008. Their product is √[(51·39)/(26·34)] = √(9/4) = 3/2 = V1/V0. Chain value is 120√(51/26) ≈ 168.066 and the implicit deflator is 100√(39/34) ≈ 107.101. Fixed-base value 170 implies growth 17/12 − 1 = 41⅔%, whereas Fisher growth is approximately 40.055%. They use different weights; neither changes the underlying recorded quantities.

  5. SD = 500 − 490 = 10 and NPI = 30 − 50 = −20. GNP = 500 − 20 = 480; GNI = 490 − 20 = 470 = GNP − SD. NDP = 500 − 40 = 460; net domestic income = 490 − 40 = 450 = NDP − SD; NNI = 470 − 40 = 430. Applying the same adjustment to both differently estimated starting measures preserves their gap. The discrepancy is not an income tax, capital consumption or foreign payment; those are separate bridges.

  6. CFC is 80/8 = 10 physical units. Closing capital is 80 − 10 + 20 − 5 = 85. Opening value is 160 and closing value 255, giving a 95 increase. Revaluing the opening stock gives a holding gain of (3 − 2)80 = 80. At the subsequent price, gross investment is 60, CFC 30 and catastrophic loss 15. Thus 60 − 30 − 15 + 80 = 95. The catastrophe is separate from ordinary capital use. Gross investment 60 is neither the 95 value change nor the 15 net change after all physical reductions at current prices.

  7. Export receipts fall from 40·5 = 200 to 40·4 = 160. At import price 2 they buy 100 then 80 imported units. Nominal GDP is 100·5 = 500 then 100·4 = 400. Production volume remains 100 physical units; at initial prices real GDP remains 500. Initially C = 60·5 + 100·2 = 500 and X = M = 200; subsequently C = 60·4 + 80·2 = 400 and X = M = 160. Both expenditure totals match production GDP. Nominal GDP in imported-good units falls from 250 to 200. The feasible consumption bundle retains 60 domestic units but loses 20 imported units. Domestic volume is unchanged while purchasing power deteriorates; a utility ranking would additionally specify preferences.

  8. Mean income rises from 100/3 to 40, a 20% increase, while median income falls from 30 to 20, a one-third decline. Two of three households have less income. “Typical” must be defined; using the median directly contradicts the analyst's claim. Even an average-income improvement does not numerically establish a proportional welfare improvement. Additional measurements could include household consumption and disposable income across the distribution, environmental damage or health outcomes, and unpaid work or leisure. A defensible welfare analysis would specify preferences or an explicit social welfare criterion, relevant prices and quantities, distributional weights and omitted outcomes. Full credit requires the correct calculations, the distribution/production distinction, three relevant measurements and an explicit evaluative criterion. No particular political weighting is required; an unsupported GDP-to-welfare equality fails.

Teach-back and Delayed check

Teach-back. A colleague says, “Imports lower GDP, rising house prices are economic growth, and adding the real consumption and investment series gives real GDP.” Explain the accounting error in each sentence, using a transaction or index calculation.

Elements of a complete explanation

An imported final product appears in an expenditure category and is removed by imports; its direct entries offset, so a causal output claim requires further reasoning. An existing house's price gain revalues an asset rather than producing another house; current housing or brokerage services are separate production. Independently chained components need not add to the aggregate, as 310 versus 281.709 shows. Add current-price accounts, use the defined aggregate volume series and use appropriate growth contributions. A good explanation identifies the relevant boundary, valuation and timing instead of merely reciting three slogans.

Delayed check. After a gap, consider one final good with price 10 and quantity 8 initially, then price 12 and quantity 9. Population rises from 4 to 5. Compute nominal growth, real growth at initial prices, deflator inflation and real GDP per person growth. Check the exact compounding identity.

Delayed check answer

Nominal values are 80 and 108, so growth is 35%. Initial-price real values are 80 and 90, so real growth is 12.5%. The deflator rises from 100 to 120, or 20%. Exactly, 1.125·1.2 = 1.35; subtracting 20% from 35% would give the wrong real rate. Real GDP per person falls from 80/4 = 20 to 90/5 = 18, a 10% decline. Aggregate real production can rise while production per person falls.
