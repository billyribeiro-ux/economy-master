A price changes who is willing to trade. A credential changes what an employer believes. A menu changes which product a buyer selects. When people possess different information, solve those responses together: taking the population average, trusting an announcement or assigning contracts by an unobservable type can give the wrong answer.

Capability. Derive participation and conditional quality in a lemons market; construct and check pooling and separating signaling equilibria; and design a two-type menu with explicit participation, incentive and welfare calculations. Use Chapter 7 for strategies and deviations, conditional probability for beliefs, and the earlier constrained optimization and surplus methods. All numerical economies here are original teaching models, not observations of actual markets.

Specify what is private, when it is known and what can be contracted on

A type is a payoff-relevant characteristic known to its owner: an asset's quality, a buyer's willingness to pay or a worker's productivity. Nature draws it according to a specified prior distribution. The owner observes it; the other party may observe only an action, a report or an outcome. Common knowledge of the distribution is compatible with private knowledge of the realized type. Write down the timing before solving incentives.

Adverse selection concerns hidden information present before contracting and the composition of people or assets that choose a transaction. At a common price, low-quality sellers may participate while high-quality sellers retain their assets. In an insurance setting, people with greater expected claims may be especially willing to buy coverage. Selection does not require a false statement. Moral hazard concerns an action imperfectly observed or enforceable after contracting, such as prevention or effort. The next chapter develops that different constraint. A transaction can contain both problems.

Information may be observed by a trading partner yet unverifiable to an enforcing third party. Our contracts specify which quantities and payments are enforceable; they cannot simply command an unobservable type. Cheap announcements alone need not reveal information: if both seller types prefer a high price, both can announce “high quality.” Credibility must come from incentives, verifiable evidence or a specified communication equilibrium.

If the prior high-type share is α and the probabilities of participation are rH and rL, Bayes' rule gives

Pr(H | participates) = αrH/[αrH + (1 − α)rL].

This differs from α unless participation rates coincide, apart from degenerate cases. The denominator must be positive. When no one participates, this formula does not define the quality of a hypothetical trade. Specify which offer would induce which types to participate instead.

Lemons: the object being priced changes with the price

Consider a mass one of sellers, each owning one indivisible asset. Type H has seller reservation value cH; type L has cL. A seller trades if the highest available price is at least its reservation value and otherwise retains the asset. We explicitly assume participation at equality. Buyers value the asset at vH or vL, with vθ > cθ for both types. Quality is known by its seller and cannot be verified before trade. Buyers are risk neutral; they have sufficient funds and can make open price offers for whatever mass accepts. There are at least two competing buyers, no transaction costs, no capacity constraint and no additional signal.

A competitive positive-volume price must give zero expected profit using the participating composition. But that condition is only a candidate test. Check that a buyer cannot offer a different price, attract a different composition and earn positive profit. Lower offers cannot attract sellers already receiving a higher acceptable offer. At identical offers, split sellers independently of quality; no buyer has privileged access to high types. These trading rules make the deviation calculation precise.

The quality-dependent participation mechanism is introduced in MIT 15.010's asymmetric-information handout, PDF pages 1–4. Its bargaining prices depend on bargaining power. Our different posted-offer competition protocol pins down a zero-profit price and requires the deviation checks below.

Worked example 1: missing high-quality trade, and a misleading candidate equilibrium

Let (cL,vL) = (4,6), (cH,vH) = (12,18), in dollars per asset, and α = 1/3. If every asset traded, expected buyer value would be (2/3)6 + (1/3)18 = 10. An offer of 10 cannot sustain that composition: high types retain assets worth 12 to them.

At p = 6, all low types sell, high types retain, and the buyer's posterior high-quality probability is zero. Expected buyer profit is zero. An offer strictly between 6 and 12 still attracts only low types and loses money. An offer of 12 or more attracts both types but pays at least 12 for expected value 10. Lower offers attract no assets away from the existing price. Thus p = 6 survives both participation and offer-deviation tests.

With observable quality, all assets could trade, generating total gains (2/3)(6 − 4) + (1/3)(18 − 12) = 10/3 dollars per initial seller. With hidden quality, only the low assets trade: gains are (2/3)2 = 4/3. The loss of 2 is the unrealized gain on the high assets. The low sellers receive the gains from their trades under this buyer-competition protocol; the buyers earn zero. Prices redistribute trade surplus, while omitted trades reduce it.

Now change only α to 3/4. All-trade expected value becomes 15, exceeding both reservation values. The competitive pooling price p = 15 is sustainable: a higher offer loses money and a lower offer attracts no one. Merely writing p = 6 with low types selling still passes local zero-profit and participation conditions. It fails the full equilibrium test: a buyer offering 13 attracts both types and earns expected profit 15 − 13 = 2 per asset. The trading mechanism permits this deviation. Population composition therefore changes which outcome survives.

More generally, the all-trade value is 6 + 12α. Full participation requires it to be at least 12. At α = 1/2, the high seller is indifferent at p = 12; participation at equality and the possibility of zero-profit deviations matter. Under our weak best-response criterion, both the low-only p = 6 outcome and all-trade p = 12 can be supported at this boundary. A move to 12 is not strictly profitable against the low-only outcome. Do not infer uniqueness from a weak inequality.

Complete unraveling is possible, but not automatic

For a continuous illustration, let quality z be uniform on [0,1], seller reservation value z dollars and buyer value az dollars, where 1 < a < 2. Sellers know z. At an offer 0 < p ≤ 1, precisely z ≤ p participate, giving conditional mean quality p/2. Expected buyer value ap/2 is below p, so every such positive-volume offer loses money. An offer p > 1 attracts everyone but yields expected value a/2 < 1 < p. No positive-volume trade can survive. A price of zero attracts at most the measure-zero type z = 0.

Nevertheless, every positive-quality asset has buyer value above its seller's reservation value. With verifiable quality, all could trade, generating ∫01(a − 1)z dz = (a − 1)/2. The obstruction is the interaction of selection and information, not a lack of potential gains. If a exceeds 2, an all-trade competitive price a/2 exceeds every seller's reservation value. The same private information then need not eliminate trade.

An intervention also needs a mechanism and a resource ledger. Perfect inspection can reveal quality, but costs resources; certification must be credible and financed. A warranty must be enforceable and must alter incentives differently across types if it is to separate them. Reputation needs repeated transactions, observable outcomes and future value. These possibilities do not establish that any advertised warranty or disclosure rule solves a particular market. Specify participation, deviations and net costs again.

Signals require strategies and beliefs

In a signaling game, the informed sender observes type θ, chooses an observable signal e, and the receiver responds. A strategy tells each type which signal to choose; a receiver strategy specifies a response after every possible signal. An assessment includes these strategies and beliefs μ(e) = Pr(H | e). For an observed signal with positive probability,

μ(e) = πσH(e)/[πσH(e) + (1 − π)σL(e)],

where π is the prior high-type probability and σθ(e) is its probability of selecting that signal. This formula applies directly to discrete signals or atoms, including the pure strategies below. A continuous mixed signal distribution requires conditional densities or a suitable conditional-probability formulation. We do not divide by the zero probability of a single continuous draw.

For these signaling games, a perfect Bayesian equilibrium requires each type's signal to maximize its payoff given receiver responses, receivers to respond optimally to their beliefs after every signal, and beliefs to obey Bayes' rule at every positive-probability signal. A signal used in equilibrium is on path. An unused signal is off path; Bayes' formula alone leaves its belief unspecified. One must still supply that belief and an optimal response, then check whether a type would deviate there. This is a signaling-game definition, not a complete treatment of every general extensive-form PBE convention.

Pooling means both types choose the same signal; that signal leaves the prior unchanged. Separating means they choose different signals, so each used signal reveals its sender's type. Partial separation is also possible when distributions overlap. A signal need not improve the object being evaluated: its information effect and its direct productive effect are separate assumptions.

The strategy, belief and equilibrium distinctions follow the signaling framework in Alexander Wolitzky's MIT 14.126 Lecture 3, slides 3–11. Our numerical construction checks competitive employer bidding explicitly.

Worked example 2: an informative credential can consume resources

A worker's productivity is yL = 4 or yH = 10 dollars per job, with prior π = 1/3 for H. The worker chooses credential effort e ≥ 0 before employers bid. Its real opportunity cost is cL(e) = 3e or cH(e) = e dollars. Effort has no productive benefit in this model. At least two risk-neutral employers observe e and simultaneously bid wages; the worker accepts the highest wage, with a type-independent tie rule and outside payoff zero. Employers have no capacity constraint or private information.

Given belief μ(e), expected productivity is 4 + 6μ(e). Both employers bidding that value is an equilibrium of the wage stage: an overbid wins at an expected loss, and an underbid loses to the other bidder. Each obtains expected profit zero. Thus specify wage w(e) = 4 + 6μ(e) after every e. The worker maximizes w(e) − cθ(e).

Propose eL = 0 and eH = s > 0. Bayes' rule sets μ(0) = 0 and μ(s) = 1, giving wages 4 and 10. Set μ(e) = 0 at every other signal. Low types must prefer their own option, 4 ≥ 10 − 3s; high types must prefer theirs, 10 − s ≥ 4. Therefore

2 ≤ s ≤ 6.

Those two inequalities also control all remaining deviations: every other e pays 4 and costs a nonnegative amount, giving at most 4. Thus every s in this interval supports a separating equilibrium under the stated beliefs. At the endpoints one type is indifferent; its prescribed action remains a weak best response. With s = 3, low types obtain 4 while high types obtain 7. Low types would get only 1 from mimicking; high types would get 4 from choosing zero. All deviations are checked, not only a derivative at the proposed signal.

Expected gross productivity is 6, and expected wage is also 6. Expected real signaling cost is (1/3)3 = 1, so total output net of this cost is 5. Wage payments transfer resources between employers and workers; effort costs consume resources. The credential reveals productivity without increasing it. This does not show that real education has no productive effect.

A pooling equilibrium also exists under different beliefs: both types choose e = 0, μ(0) = 1/3 and w(0) = 6; set μ(e) = 0 and w(e) = 4 for every e > 0. A deviation lowers the wage and adds cost, so neither type gains. Expected net output is 6. High types prefer the separating s = 3 payoff of 7 to pooling's 6; low types prefer pooling's 6 to separating's 4. Higher total surplus does not mean a Pareto improvement.

The off-path beliefs are consequential. If employers instead assigned μ(e) = 1/2 after every e > 0 in the proposed pooling outcome, those deviations would pay 7. A high type could choose any 0 < e < 1 and gain; a low type could gain with 0 < e < 1/3. That assessment is not an equilibrium. Reporting “everyone chooses zero” without the response after deviations is incomplete.

Jonathan Levin's Dynamic Games with Incomplete Information, sections 2.2–2.3 develops two-type incentive inequalities, off-path beliefs and refinements. Our construction verifies its own parameters. At pooling wage 6, a deviation to e = 2 could never benefit L even at the maximum wage 10: its payoff would be 4. It could benefit H, whose payoff at wage 10 would be 8. This motivates restrictions that assign such a deviation to the type that could gain. Such refinements add restrictions to basic PBE; their general theory belongs in graduate game theory. Basic PBE alone does not select a unique signaling cost.

The observed separating wage gap is 6, while the assumed causal productivity effect of the credential is zero. The model is an identification counterexample: a wage–credential association alone cannot distinguish learning, selection and signaling. Real evidence needs a defined causal question, credible variation, potential changes in composition and attention to whether employers observe the intervention. A pure signaling model is not itself an empirical estimate of education's return.

Screening: offer options that people willingly sort into

Now the uninformed firm moves first. A buyer knows its willingness-to-pay type θ ∈ {θL,θH}, where 0 < θL < θH. The firm knows high-type probability λ ∈ (0,1), commits to a menu of two verifiable quantity–payment pairs (qL,tL), (qH,tH), and permits the outside option (0,0). A buyer chooses at most one contract and cannot resell or combine options. Quantity q ≥ 0 has a specified common unit; θ is dollars per unit, t is dollars, and utility is θq − t. Production cost is C(q) = q²/2 dollars, with the cost coefficient expressed in the corresponding units. There are no further costs or externalities.

Individual rationality (IR) says participating beats the outside payoff zero. Incentive compatibility (IC) says the intended option beats the other option. Labeling a contract “high type” does not prevent low types from choosing it, or conversely. The four restrictions are

IRL: θLqL − tL ≥ 0;   IRH: θHqH − tH ≥ 0;
ICL: θLqL − tL ≥ θLqH − tH;
ICH: θHqH − tH ≥ θHqL − tL.

Adding the two IC inequalities yields (θH − θL)(qH − qL) ≥ 0, so implementable quantities must satisfy qH ≥ qL. For any such quantities, IRL implies tL ≤ θLqL; ICH then implies tH ≤ θHqH − (θH − θL)qL. Both upper bounds can be attained simultaneously:

tL = θLqL,   tH = θHqH − (θH − θL)qL.

Check the other constraints rather than assuming them away. High-type utility becomes (θH − θL)qL ≥ 0; low-type utility from the high contract is (θH − θL)(qL − qH) ≤ 0. Hence all four hold. Since these fees attain an upper bound on each payment for the fixed quantities, they maximize revenue at those quantities. High types receive an information rent: the surplus needed to keep them from taking the low contract. The firm cannot extract this rent without changing incentives or quantities.

Bengt Holmstrom's MIT 14.124 screening slides, Part 3, slides 2–17 derive the analogous IC/IR trade-off for procurement from privately informed suppliers. Here payments flow in the opposite direction, from a privately informed buyer to the seller. The displayed constraints and proof establish the appropriate signs for our model.

Worked example 3: optimal quantities, information rent and the loss from distortion

Let θL = 4, θH = 8 and λ = 1/3. The maximal implementable fees are tL = 4qL and tH = 8qH − 4qL. Substituting into expected profit gives

Π = (2/3)(4qL − qL²/2) + (1/3)(8qH − 4qL − qH²/2)
= (4/3)qL − qL²/3 + (8/3)qH − qH²/6.

This is strictly concave. Its derivative equations give qL = 2 and qH = 8, which satisfy nonnegativity and monotonicity, so they give the global maximum over feasible quantities. Fees are tL = 8 and tH = 56. At these contracts, L gets 0 from the intended option and 32 − 56 = −24 from the high option. H gets 64 − 56 = 8 from the high option and 16 − 8 = 8 from the low option. All participation and incentive inequalities hold.

Specify tie behavior: an indifferent buyer chooses its intended contract rather than the outside or the other option. This is a weak implementation convention. It can be approximated with strict choices: replace fees by 8 − ε and 56 − 2ε for 0 < ε < 1. L then gets ε > 0, while H gets 8 + 2ε from the high contract versus 8 + ε from the low one. L still rejects the high contract. Expected profit falls by 4ε/3 and approaches the weak optimum as ε tends to zero. Without a tie convention, do not assert exact attainment by the original fees.

Expected profit is 12; expected buyer surplus is 8/3; total surplus is 44/3. If types were observable and prices could be conditioned on them, efficient quantities would maximize θq − q²/2, giving q = θ. The firm could charge 16 for qL = 4 and 64 for qH = 8 under the same weak participation convention, earning expected profit and total surplus 16.

Private information lowers total surplus by 16 − 44/3 = 4/3. The high quantity remains efficient; the low quantity is reduced to make the low contract less attractive to high types. The firm's profit falls by 4, comprising information rent 8/3 transferred to buyers and resource-allocation loss 4/3. Rent itself is not a social loss in this unweighted quasilinear ledger.

Check simpler alternatives: serving only H at q = 8, t = 64 yields expected profit 32/3; offering one option to both at q = 4, t = 16 yields profit 8. Both are below 12. More generally, the reduction already includes exclusion through qL = 0 and pooling through qL = qH. Its concavity proof is stronger than checking only these two alternatives.

For arbitrary 0 < λ < 1 and positive types, the same fee substitution gives qH = θH and

qL = max{0, θL − [λ/(1 − λ)](θH − θL)}.

To derive it, differentiate expected profit in qL: (1 − λ)(θL − qL) − λ(θH − θL). Strict concavity gives the interior maximizer; if its candidate is negative, the constrained optimum is zero. The result never exceeds θL, so it respects qL ≤ qH. The firm weighs low-type production surplus against the rent it must concede to high types. With types 4 and 8, low types receive zero quantity when λ ≥ 1/2. This is exclusion under the model, not evidence that a particular real firm's product restrictions are optimal.

The upper type's efficient quantity and the lower type's downward distortion depend on this single-dimensional, quasilinear, deterministic menu with common zero outside options, commitment and no resale. Different outside options, capacity constraints, competition, multidimensional preferences or unverifiable quality can change the result. Incentive compatibility also does not establish distributional fairness. Later chapters extend contracting and mechanism design; this chapter supplies a complete solved core, not a general solution for all information problems.

Complete solutions and evaluation criteria
  1. The listing mass is (1/3)(1/4) + (2/3)(3/4) = 7/12. The H portion is 1/12, hence posterior 1/7 and expected value 18/7 + 36/7 = 54/7. The population mean 10 ignores different listing probabilities; the simple average 12 assigns equal shares without justification. Expected value alone does not fix a transaction price: specify buyer competition or bargaining, sellers' reservation values, whether listing responds to offers, and any further information. This is an exogenous listing calculation, not a replacement for the endogenous participation model.
  2. At α = 3/4, value is (1/4)6 + (3/4)18 = 15; both reservations are below 15. Higher offers lose money, and lower offers cannot attract sellers from 15. Against p = 6, an offer of 13 attracts all types and yields 2 per asset, defeating that candidate. At α = 1/3, inspection permits gross gains 10/3 and costs one dollar per initial seller, giving net 7/3. Uninspected surplus is 4/3, so the improvement is one dollar per initial seller. The charge is fixed and inspection mandatory, so it does not create a new voluntary-inspection selection problem; it is counted once as a real cost. A voluntary fee paid only by sellers who certify would require new participation and belief calculations. Distribution depends on who finances the charge, even though aggregate resources here are fixed.
  3. For a = 3/2 and 0 < p ≤ 1, per-purchase expected profit is 3p/4 − p = −p/4 < 0. For p > 1 it is 3/4 − p < 0. At zero, only a measure-zero type accepts; there is no positive-volume trade. Full-information gains are (a − 1)/2 = 1/4. For a = 5/2, all-trade expected value is 5/4 ≥ 1, so everyone accepts p = 5/4. Higher offers lose money and lower offers do not attract sellers away. All assets trade and total gains are 3/4. The answer depends on gains relative to the adverse-selection effect.
  4. Low IC gives s ≥ 2, high IC gives s ≤ 6. All remaining signals pay 4 and have nonnegative costs, so yield no more than choosing zero. At s = 1, L would mimic and get 7 > 4; separation fails. At s = 2, L is indifferent at 4, so separation is a weak equilibrium with prescribed type choices. At s = 3 both cross-type comparisons are strict. At s = 7, H gets 3 and prefers the payoff 4 at zero. Expected signal cost is s/3: 2/3 at s = 2 and 1 at s = 3. Expected gross productivity remains 6; net output is 16/3 and 5. The less costly separating outcome has greater total surplus, but basic PBE permits the full interval under appropriate beliefs.
  5. With μ = 0 off path, a positive signal pays 4 instead of 6 and adds cost; both types prefer pooling at zero. With μ = 1/2, any positive signal pays 7; H gains for e < 1, and L gains for e < 1/3. Thus those beliefs defeat the pooling assessment. At e = 2 and the maximum wage 10, L gets 4 < 6 while H gets 8 > 6. A refinement may therefore reject the belief that this deviation comes from L and instead credit H. This is motivation for an additional selection restriction, not a claim that arbitrary off-path beliefs are already fixed by Bayes. A strong empirical answer separates an observed association from the causal productive effect, notes sorting on ability and signaling incentives, and proposes credible exogenous variation with attention to employer information and composition. Merely repeating “correlation is not causation” without identifying the mechanism is incomplete.
  6. The general formula gives qL = 3 − (1/3)4 = 5/3, qH = 7; fees are tL = 5 and tH = 49 − 20/3 = 127/3. L gets 0 from its option and 21 − 127/3 = −64/3 from H's option. Expected profit is (3/4)(65/18) + (1/4)(107/6) = 43/6. Expected buyer surplus is (1/4)(20/3) = 5/3; total is 53/6. Observable-type quantities are 3 and 7, with expected total surplus (3/4)(9/2) + (1/4)(49/2) = 19/2. The loss is 2/3, also (3/4)(3 − 5/3)²/2. The profit difference 7/3 equals rent 5/3 plus distortion 2/3.
  7. qL = max{0,4 − 4λ/(1 − λ)} = max{0,4(1 − 2λ)/(1 − λ)}. It reaches zero at λ = 1/2 and stays there above. At λ = 2/3, the low option is (0,0); the high option is (8,64). L rejects it because 32 − 64 < 0. H gets zero and chooses its intended option under the tie convention. Expected profit and total surplus are (2/3)(64 − 32) = 64/3; rents are zero. Observable-type total surplus is (1/3)8 + (2/3)32 = 24. The loss is 8/3 from excluding low types. Negative quantity is infeasible; a negative stationary candidate means the nonnegativity constraint binds, not that the seller should deliver a negative service.
  8. With observable-type fees, H obtains 0 at (8,64) but 8(4) − 16 = 16 at (4,16), violating high IC. For adjusted screening fees, L obtains ε at its option, −24 + 2ε at the high option and zero outside; its own option is strictly best for 0 < ε < 1. H obtains 8 + 2ε at its high option, 8 + ε at the low option and zero outside, so strictly selects high. Costs are unchanged. Expected fees, and hence profit, fall by (2/3)ε + (1/3)2ε = 4ε/3, giving profit 12 − 4ε/3. Self-selection is implemented by these preference comparisons; contract names or requests for honesty alone do not impose them.

Teach-back and Delayed check

Teach-back. Explain to a colleague why a population average cannot automatically price a selected pool, why a signaling equilibrium needs beliefs after unused signals, and why a screening menu must be tested against a buyer choosing the “wrong” option. Use one equation for each mechanism. A complete explanation identifies who knows the type, the order of moves, the relevant deviation and which costs consume real resources. Distinguish the seller's rent loss from a total-surplus loss.

Delayed check. Without consulting the examples, solve a signaling variant with productivities 3 and 9, prior H share 1/2, costs cL(e) = 2e and cH(e) = e. Let L choose zero and H choose s; assign every other signal to L. Find the separating interval. At s = 4, give both worker payoffs, expected real signal cost and net output. Then name the missing calculation in a lemons “equilibrium” justified only by a zero-profit price.

Delayed check answer

Low IC is 3 ≥ 9 − 2s, so s ≥ 3; high IC is 9 − s ≥ 3, so s ≤ 6. Other signals pay 3 and cost a nonnegative amount, so they do not improve either type's payoff. At s = 4, payoffs are 3 and 5. Expected cost is (1/2)4 = 2, and expected productivity is 6, so net output is 4. The lemons candidate also needs participation-consistent composition and a check that alternative offers attracting a different pool cannot earn positive profit.
