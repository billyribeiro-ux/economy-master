Policy synthesis and institutional feasibility

Capability. Defend a policy whose counterfactual, welfare criterion, distribution, financing, administration and revision rule fit together. The prerequisite is urban economics, housing and inequality, especially its distinction between an area's measured success and the welfare of its original residents. We also use convex optimization, causal identification, political decision rules and Bayesian choice. Every population, payment and probability below is synthetic. None is a current legal recommendation or an estimate of actual program effectiveness.

A policy memo can be numerically precise and still answer the wrong question. Gross benefits may be compared with tax receipts instead of real costs; an experimental estimate may be moved to a different population without justification; an efficient allocation may require unavailable staff; compensation may be promised only after the votes needed to authorize it. Good synthesis makes the dependencies explicit. A favorable benefit–cost ratio is one input to a decision, not a substitute for an implementable institution.

Throughout, money and consumption goods are measured in the same numeraire unless stated otherwise. Transfers move purchasing power between people; resource costs use goods or time that have another use. A distributional weight specifies how an evaluator values a person's marginal gain. The status quo is a feasible alternative with incremental value zero. Political rules are stipulated institutions, not assertions about any jurisdiction. Quantities can be divisible populations or service intensity; integer projects would require a discrete feasible set.

1. Start with a resource ledger and named people

Let bᵢ be person i's consumption-equivalent benefit, tᵢ its tax payment and zᵢ its net transfer. Suppose implementation consumes C real goods and transfer administration consumes A. A balanced government satisfies Σtᵢ=C+A, while pure transfers satisfy Σzᵢ=0. Here the tax ledger includes the administrative bill. Equivalently, one can put administration in the transfer outlay ledger, then use Σtᵢ=C and Σzᵢ=−A. Never charge the same administrative resources in both ledgers. With consistent accounting, Σgᵢ=Σbᵢ−C−A. Taxes cannot be subtracted as resource costs and then subtracted again through financed spending. Conversely, canceling taxes does not cancel their distribution or any actual behavioral distortion they induce. Our examples use fixed lump-sum levies and quasilinear consumption so that there is no unmodeled tax distortion.

For transparent transfer administration, record the recipient's receipt x and payer's outlay (1+a)x separately: x is a transfer and ax is a real administrative cost. Aggregation then leaves −ax. Distributional weights wᵢ produce objective Σwᵢgᵢ. Unless all weights are equal, a pure transfer can change that objective without creating resources. This is a chosen social criterion. A policy is a Pareto improvement only if every relevant person's actual gain is nonnegative, with at least one strict gain; hypothetical compensation is not enough.

Worked case 1. An affordable compensation plan

Three households each have initial consumption 20. A service gives benefits (12,6,2), consumes 12 goods and is financed by fixed levies (3,4,5). No external party receives unrecorded profits. The initial gains are (9,2,-3), summing to eight. Household 3 loses despite positive aggregate surplus. Every levy is affordable before benefits arrive.

A verified transfer x from household 1 to household 3 uses administrative resources ax with 1/5. Household 1 pays (6/5)x, household 3 receives x, and the service provider receives and consumes x/5. The resulting gains are g₁=9−6x/5, g₂=2 and g₃=−3+x. The complete Pareto-feasible interval is (minimum x,maximum x)=(3,15/2). The lower bound protects household 3; the upper bound protects household 1. Household 2 already gains. Every point in the interval fits within household 1's initial resources after its original tax, so feasibility does not depend on borrowing against a hoped-for benefit.

At minimum compensation x=3, (g₁,g₂,g₃,aggregate)=(27/5,2,0,37/5). The aggregate falls from eight by the real administration cost 3/5, not by the entire payment 18/5. The transfer can still turn the policy into an actual Pareto improvement. Within the Pareto interval, equal-weight welfare is 8−x/5 and therefore globally maximized at its lower endpoint. If the evaluator instead uses weights (1,1,2), the objective is 5+4x/5, globally maximized at the upper endpoint, where gains are (0,2,9/2) and weighted value is eleven. The feasible set has not changed; the normative objective has.

The example is deliberately small enough to audit. In a real application, identify who owns firms, who moves, who remains eligible, and who is missing from the measurement frame. Following only residents after a place improves can omit those displaced by it. Benefits measured as willingness to pay may already include certain private costs; adding those costs again would double count. Claims about distribution require a stable population and a payment rule, not merely a positive total.

2. Translate evidence into a decision without hiding the gap

For two populations and two dates, let D be the observed difference between their outcome changes. Define τ as the treated population's average treatment effect and γ as the difference between the treated and comparison populations' untreated changes. Then D=τ+γ is an accounting decomposition of potential outcomes under no anticipation and consistent outcome measurement. Parallel trends sets γ=0; data on the four observed means alone do not establish that restriction.

If credible substantive knowledge instead restricts γ to a closed interval [l,u], then τ∈[D−u,D−l]. This interval is sharp in the simple unrestricted-mean setting: for each γ in the allowed interval, define the treated population's unobserved untreated follow-up mean as its baseline plus the comparison change plus γ. The treatment effect then reproduces the observed follow-up exactly. If outcomes have additional bounds, those must also restrict this construction. An identified set represents structural ambiguity; it is not a confidence interval accounting for sampling error. The Rambachan–Roth method route motivates explicit trend-departure restrictions; the elementary sharp interval below is independently derived and does not invoke their general inference machinery.

Transport adds another assumption. If conditional effects are stable across settings and the target has support in the studied groups, reweight those conditional effects by target shares. A change in the mixture alone changes the average effect. Changes in implementation, local prices, congestion or take-up can change the conditional effects themselves, making simple reweighting inadequate. A decision rule must state how it treats uncertainty left after both identification and transport.

Worked case 2. Strong trial appeal, ambiguous target value

For group A the observed means are (treated before,treated after,comparison before,comparison after)=(10,15,8,10). Let γ∈[−1,2]. Group B's effect is known to be zero in this synthetic example. Conditional effects are assumed stable between trial and target, and both groups are represented. Trial share A is 3/4; target share A is 1/4. A unit outcome improvement is worth eight consumption goods and serving one target person consumes four goods.

The difference in differences is three, so (A effect low,A effect high,trial average low,trial average high,target average low,target average high)=(1,4,3/4,3,1/4,1). The trial's net value per person would lie in [2,20], while the target's lies in (-2,4). These are exact population means, with no sampling error in the exercise. A treasury with an ex ante appropriation of four per person can implement the program in either state; an uncertain benefit is not used to finance its cost.

A maximin evaluator compares the program's worst payoff −2 with the status quo's zero and declines. A minimax-regret evaluator compares the loss relative to the best action in each state. Deterministic adoption has worst regret two, while deterministic rejection has worst regret four, so it adopts. These criteria answer different questions. With an ex ante lottery that adopts with probability r, expected regret is 2r in the low state and 4(1−r) in the high state. The maximum is minimized at their intersection: (adoption probability,worst expected regret)=(2/3,4/3). To prove global optimality, below 2/3 the decreasing high-state regret exceeds 4/3; above it the increasing low-state regret exceeds 4/3.

This lottery concerns an evaluator's expected regret, not a guarantee for each realization or person. The full appropriation of four must be available whenever adoption occurs; expected spending 8/3 is not sufficient cash to fund an adopted program without contingent finance. Random implementation could also create ethical or administrative concerns absent from this model. State those concerns as constraints instead of pretending that the mathematical optimum resolves them.

3. Administrative capacity is a constraint, not a footnote

Money and staff time are different inputs. An agency may have funds but insufficient trained capacity to process cases correctly. Conversely, expanding workload without a financed operating budget can leave authorized services undelivered. Institutional investment often occurs before current policy choices; the reused Besley–Persson state-capacity source supplies that timing context. Our capacity-allocation problem is a separate original model, not a calibration of that paper.

Let x,y≥0 be divisible service intensities for populations A and B. Gross benefits are 14x−x² and 12y−y². Each unit consumes two goods, so incremental net benefits are F(x,y)=12x−x²+10y−y². Existing trained staff can supply K workload units, and each A unit uses two while each B unit uses one: 2x+y≤K. Existing staffing costs are common sunk costs relative to the status quo and are excluded from incremental F; acquiring additional capacity has a separately specified real cost. A treasury ceiling of 30 covers operating outlays at every optimum considered. Actual levies equal actual outlays and households have sufficient prior endowments; unused authorization is not counted as spending.

For fixed finite K the feasible set is compact and F strictly concave. Its unique optimizer is characterized by 12−2x≤2λ, 10−2y≤λ, λ≥0, equality when the corresponding service is positive, and λ(K−2x−y)=0. At a positive interior solution λ measures the gain from one extra workload unit, not the gain from one extra dollar. Solving all regimes gives x=0,y=K,λ=10−2K when 0<K≤2; x=(2K−4)/5,y=(K+8)/5,λ=(34−2K)/5 when 2≤K≤17; and x=6,y=5,λ=0 for K≥17. At K=0 the allocation is zero. Extra capacity above 17 is unused because both net marginal benefits have reached zero.

Worked case 3. The value of a trained processing unit

At (K,A gross intercept,B gross intercept,unit cost,A workload,B workload)=(8,14,12,2,2,1), the solution is (x,y,λ,operating cost,net value)=(12/5,16/5,18/5,56/5,224/5). Workload is exactly eight and expenditure 56/5 is below the appropriation. A funder with 100 initial goods pays that amount; suppliers use the same goods to deliver services. Gross service benefit is 56 and real net benefit 224/5. No transfer from tax collection is added as a separate gain.

A full global certificate follows by expanding the quadratic around this point: F(x*,y*)−F(x,y)=(x−x*)²+(y−y*)²+λ(8−2x−y). Every term is nonnegative for a feasible alternative, and equality requires the same allocation. This proves global optimality rather than simply checking first-order equations. For the small-capacity branch an additional nonnegative term records the inactive A margin; the concave tangent argument establishes sufficiency there too.

The multiplier 18/5 is a marginal value. Expanding K from eight to nine raises value by the integral of the declining multiplier, 17/5, not 18/5. If one extra trained unit costs three real goods upfront, the funded expansion has incremental net value 2/5. Its operating allocation must be recomputed; benefits do not arise from purchasing training without actually reallocating services. The operating bill after expansion is 62/5 and total outlay including training is 77/5, still below 30.

In practice, processing errors, appeal rights, staff turnover and bottlenecks at complementary agencies can alter both workload and benefits. Measure cases completed correctly, waiting time, error rates and outcomes for rejected applicants. A target for raw case throughput can reward an agency for skipping verification or excluding difficult cases. Such responses need an incentive model or evaluation design; our known concave benefits do not make them disappear.

4. An approval rule and a promise are different constraints

Consider three districts with a common policy proposal and a public, verifiable compensation schedule. Each representative supports adoption exactly when its district's net consumption-equivalent gain is nonnegative; ties support adoption. This is a stipulated voting rule with no strategic misreporting. District 1 sponsors the proposal and chooses the cheapest compensation that obtains approval, maximizing its own gain. Compensation must be escrowed before voting, enforceable after adoption, and drawn from district 1's resources. A fixed authorization limits total compensation outlay including administration.

This institution is deliberately narrow. The schedule compensates measured harms transparently; no current legal permission to buy votes is implied. Different agenda rules, strategic voting, private information or electoral objectives can change the outcome. The point is to exhibit the feasibility calculation rather than call every positive-surplus proposal politically implementable.

Worked case 4. Majority, veto and unanimity

Districts have prior resources 100 each. Policy gross benefits are (12,1,3), and equal lump-sum taxes of four finance a real implementation cost 12. The direct gains are (g₁,g₂,g₃,administrative rate,compensation authorization)=(8,-3,-1,1/4,4). A receipt t₂ or t₃ costs district 1 five quarters as much, with the extra quarter consumed by administration. Its gain is 8−5(t₂+t₃)/4, while the other gains are −3+t₂ and −1+t₃.

Under a two-of-three majority, district 1 needs one additional supporter. Securing district 2 requires t₂≥3; district 3 requires t₃≥1. Nonnegative transfers and linear outlay make the cheapest winning coalition uniquely districts 1 and 3, with (t₂,t₃)=(0,1). All constraints hold, including the authorization. Resulting gains are (g₁,g₂,g₃,total,outlay)=(27/4,-3,0,15/4,5/4). The losing district remains worse off. Majority approval is therefore not a Pareto certificate.

If district 2 has an additional veto, it must receive at least three; district 1 plus 2 then constitutes a majority without paying district 3. Gains become (g₁,g₂,g₃,total,outlay)=(17/4,0,-1,13/4,15/4). This is feasible under authorization four. Under unanimity both losses must be covered, requiring total recipient receipts four and outlay five. Authorization four makes that proposal infeasible even though district 1 could economically afford it. With unchanged authorization the institution selects the status quo.

The calculations enumerate every minimal winning coalition, and additional compensation only lowers the sponsor's objective. Thus they are global solutions within the stated institution. A requirement to compensate everyone may protect losers but block adoption under an inadequate budget. Increasing the authorization can change feasibility; it does not erase the administrative resource cost. Distributional safeguards and political rules should be compared openly, not smuggled into an unexplained discount rate.

Escrow supplies commitment. If district 1 could revoke all transfers after approval, and its only subsequent objective were its own consumption, it would revoke them. Forward-looking representatives 2 and 3 would then refuse based on uncompensated losses, leaving only one supporter. A paper promise would fail to implement the stated coalition. Credible contracts, enforceable rules or a different repeated-game mechanism are needed to alter that continuation decision; we assume the first and do not claim the others.

5. Pilot when information changes a feasible decision

A pilot has value through the actions it changes, after paying its real cost and accounting for any delay. Let the unknown state be H or L, with prior probability p of H. Adoption gives net benefits vH or vL and rejection gives zero. A signal Z arrives before adoption. Expected-value maximization selects adoption after Z exactly when its posterior expected benefit is nonnegative. At a zero posterior value both actions are optimal. This is a risk-neutral social criterion over specified aggregate net benefits, not a claim that uncertainty is unimportant.

The best informed expected payoff before the pilot's cost is E[max{0,E[v|Z]}]. It is at least max{0,E[v]}, because ignoring the signal and using the original action remains feasible. It is at most E[max{0,v}], the value under perfect information: conditional on each signal, no action can earn more than the statewise best action in expectation. Subtracting the no-information optimum gives lower and upper information-value bounds. These inequalities require the same feasible final actions with and without learning; pilot-induced delay, capacity changes or harm would have to enter explicitly.

Worked case 5. A useful pilot with an actual reserve

A program costs ten real goods and produces benefits 18 in state H or six in L, giving net values eight and minus four. The prior and signal primitives are (p,Pr(positive|H),Pr(positive|L),vH,vL,pilot cost)=(2/5,3/4,1/4,8,-4,3/4). The pilot consumes 3/4 goods, does not change benefits or delay implementation, and produces only this signal. The public fund has 12 prior goods; it can reserve the full ten for adoption after either signal and pay for the pilot without relying on uncertain benefits.

Without information, adoption has expected net value 4/5 and is optimal. A positive signal has probability 9/20 and posterior H probability 2/3. A negative signal has probability 11/20 and posterior 2/11. Posterior adoption values are four and −20/11. Thus the globally optimal informed rule adopts only after a positive signal: conditional maximization separates across the two signals. The numerical account is (positive probability,positive posterior,negative posterior,positive value,negative value,gross informed value,net pilot value,gain over immediate adoption)=(9/20,2/3,2/11,4,-20/11,9/5,21/20,1/4).

Expected fiscal outlay is pilot cost plus ten times the adoption probability, 21/4. Maximum outlay is 43/4, below the reserve 12. In each realized branch the remaining fund equals initial resources minus actual resource purchases; when adopted, realized benefits accrue to the named beneficiary population. Expected benefits are (2/5)(3/4)18+(3/5)(1/4)6=63/10; subtracting 21/4 gives 21/20. The realized low-state adoption loss is possible and has not been replaced by its expectation. Perfect information has value (2/5)8=16/5 before any information cost, so its incremental value is 12/5. This pilot's gross information gain of one satisfies that bound.

The reserve is not a social cost merely because it is authorized. Unused money remains public wealth. But an agency possessing only expected spending 21/4 could not pay the possible 43/4 bill without an additional enforceable funding arrangement. A proposal should state both quantities. Likewise, a pilot conducted in highly capable sites might have limited information about a nationwide program with different delivery constraints; the stated likelihood model then needs revision rather than automatic Bayesian precision.

Monitoring is a second decision problem. Repeatedly checking a five-percent test and stopping on a favorable result changes the probability of at least one false alarm. One transparent error budget sets the level at check t to αₜ=α/[t(t+1)] for t=1,2,… . If each actual false-alarm event has null probability at most αₜ, the union bound gives Pr(any false alarm)≤Σαₜ=α because 1/[t(t+1)]=1/t−1/(t+1). No independence is required. The assumption of valid marginal probabilities remains essential; adaptive data collection or a misspecified null can invalidate an ordinary test's nominal level. This elementary guarantee is not a general theorem that arbitrary repeatedly inspected confidence intervals remain valid.

A monitoring plan should connect evidence to action: who observes errors, who can suspend the next stage, how beneficiaries are protected during suspension, what funds remain, and how a revised intervention will be evaluated. Statistical false-alarm control, benefit-risk tolerance and legal authority are distinct. Prespecifying a trigger improves accountability, but does not justify a threshold with no substantive meaning. Record the original rule and every departure so an evaluator can reconstruct what was actually implemented.

Teach-back

Present a seven-minute policy defense containing one resource ledger, one counterfactual assumption, one administrative bottleneck, one harmed group, one approval requirement and one funded revision rule. Explain why changing any of these can reverse a recommendation.

Answer guide

A strong defense distinguishes real administrative cost from compensation receipts, structural identification bounds from confidence coverage, and available staff from authorized money. It follows the harmed group through actual compensation, shows the continuation incentives supporting approval, and finances the worst implementation branch. It names the welfare criterion and admits where an alternative criterion chooses differently. Cases1–5supply a complete numerical example for each claim; combining their numerical budgets without defining a joint economy would be an error.
