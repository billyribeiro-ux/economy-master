Capability and prerequisites

After this chapter, you can reconstruct an investment's return from its dated wealth and cash flows, choose an appropriate compounding and performance measure, and describe the feasible payoffs behind a portfolio choice. You can explain why a manager can report a positive time-weighted return while a client loses money, why an impressive foreign-currency return may not preserve home purchasing power, and why a portfolio's log return is not an average of its assets' log returns.

Complete Volumes 3 and 8. Review expectation, variance and covariance, risk preferences, and dated cash flows, NPV and IRR. This chapter supplies a common measurement language for portfolio mathematics and diversification, followed by allocation and equilibrium pricing. The later chapters remain production work until their acceptance is recorded; their titles are not evidence that their content is complete.

An investment committee receives four claims about one mandate: its price index rose 5%, its reinvested return exceeded 17%, a large depositor lost cash, and the manager beat a benchmark by three percentage points. Some can be simultaneously true. Before judging the manager or choosing an allocation, identify the asset, investor, currency, dates, cash treatment and question behind each number. A measurement error can survive every subsequent optimization calculation.

1. State the return contract

A payoff is an amount delivered in a specified state and date. A return relates a payoff and any intervening distributions to the capital committed under a stated strategy. The same terminal amount can represent different returns at different purchase prices. Equally, a price change can omit a substantial part of the owner's receipt. A return is dimensionless, but its horizon, currency and economic perimeter are indispensable units of interpretation.

Minimum information accompanying a reported return
QuestionRequired answer
Whose wealth?One security, a self-financing portfolio, a managed account, or the investor after all contributions and withdrawals.
What interval?Beginning and ending valuation dates; the timing of every material distribution, fee and external flow.
What value?Unadjusted price, total-return index, actual account assets, or a documented estimate; consistent treatment of accrued income and corporate actions.
What cash policy?Reinvested distributions, distributions held separately, or consumption; external contributions distinguished from investment income.
What deductions?Trading costs, management and performance fees, entry/exit charges and taxes, with their actual bases and dates.
What comparison?Same currency and horizon, explicit benchmark strategy and costs, and an appropriate choice between investor and manager performance.

All examples below are original synthetic cases in fictional currency units (CU). Unless specified otherwise, dates 0, 1 and 2 are equally spaced annual dates; distributions and valuations occur at the stated interval endpoints. There are no taxes or transaction costs unless explicitly introduced. These conventions make the arithmetic auditable; they are not claims about actual market practice or achievable investments.

For one share bought at positive price P0, with ex-distribution terminal price P1 and a cash distribution D1 paid at that same terminal date, the one-period simple total return is

R = (P1 + D1 − P0)/P0,   G = 1 + R = (P1 + D1)/P0.

G is the gross growth factor: terminal wealth per unit of initial wealth. Here “gross” means one plus the simple return; it does not mean before management fees. R is sometimes called a net return because the original principal has been subtracted, even when no fee has been deducted. Use “simple return,” “growth factor,” and an explicit fee label to prevent this terminology collision.

The price return, (P1 − P0)/P0, omits D1. A price series already adjusted for reinvested distributions must not have the same dividends added again. A stock split changes the share count and price per share; it does not by itself create wealth. If a distribution arrives earlier than the endpoint, the single-period expression using its raw cash amount represents a specified zero-return cash treatment, not automatic reinvestment. Split the timeline at the distribution or accumulate that cash using its actual subsequent strategy.

2. Link wealth across time before averaging returns

For a positive-wealth strategy with no external investor flows, Wt = Wt−1(1+Rt). Repeated substitution gives the exact identity

WT/W0 = ∏t=1…T(1+Rt),   R0:T = ∏t=1…T(1+Rt) − 1.

For two intervals, the cumulative simple return is R1 + R2 + R1R2. Adding returns discards the cross-product. Adding 20% and −10% gives 10%; investing through both intervals produces 1.2 × 0.9 − 1 = 8%. The loss applies to the enlarged capital. A decline of fraction d, with 0 ≤ d < 1, requires a subsequent gain h satisfying (1−d)(1+h)=1, hence h=d/(1−d). A 30% loss requires 42.857143%, and a complete loss cannot be repaired by multiplying zero wealth by a finite subsequent growth factor.

For T equally long intervals, the arithmetic average is R̄ = ΣRt/T. The geometric average is g = [∏(1+Rt)]1/T − 1. Only the latter, compounded T times, reproduces that realized terminal wealth. The arithmetic average remains relevant to a one-period expected-return estimate under appropriate sampling assumptions. Neither interpretation makes one statistic universally superior. In particular, a realized geometric growth rate is not automatically an estimate of next period's expected simple return.

If a positive cumulative growth factor G spans y years, its annual equivalent is G1/y−1. The elapsed-time convention must be stated; this chapter uses equal model years rather than a calendar day-count rule. A six-month gain of 6% has an annual equivalent 1.06²−1 = 12.36%, not a promise that the next six months will match the first. An annual quoted rate compounded monthly, a one-month realized return and a continuously compounded annual rate are different inputs.

Worked example 1 — Same share, different distribution strategies

Buy one Serein teaching share for 80 at date 0. At date 1 its ex-dividend price is 78 and it pays 6; at date 2 its ex-dividend price is 84 and it pays 3. The per-share distributions are paid on every share then held. Prices are unadjusted. Fractional shares can be bought immediately at the ex-dividend price without costs.

The annual total growth factors are (78+6)/80 = 21/20 and (84+3)/78 = 29/26. Immediate reinvestment gives cumulative growth (21/20)(29/26) = 609/520, so the two-year return is 89/520 = 17.115385%, with annual equivalent 8.219862%.

Reconstruct actual holdings to verify the linked index. The first dividend buys 6/78 = 1/13 of another share, giving 14/13 shares. At date 2 those shares plus their distribution are worth (14/13)(84+3) = 1218/13 = 93.692308. Dividing by 80 gives exactly 609/520. Reinvesting the final distribution at the endpoint changes the share count to 29/26 but not that endpoint wealth.

Under a different policy, hold the original one share and retain both distributions as zero-yield cash. Final wealth is 84+6+3 = 93, a 16.25% return. The price return alone is 84/80−1 = 5%. All three numbers answer different, fully specified questions. The reinvested strategy earned an additional 0.692308 because the first dividend participated in the second period; it did not receive a mysterious extra dividend on the original share. Reinvestment need not outperform retained cash on another price path.

3. Logs add over time, not across assets

For a strictly positive growth factor, define the log return ℓ = ln(G) = ln(1+R). Conversely, R=eℓ−1. Thus logs contain the same information when G>0. Applied to a correctly constructed total-return wealth series, the logarithm of the cumulative growth factor is the sum of period log returns:

ℓ0:T = ln[∏(1+Rt)] = Σ ln(1+Rt),   g = exp[(Σℓt)/T] − 1.

In Worked example 1, the annual log returns are approximately 0.048790164 and 0.109199292; their sum is 0.157989456. Exponentiating that sum, then subtracting one, recovers 17.115385%. Calling 15.798946% the simple cumulative return would confuse a log rate with a percentage change in wealth.

For small R, Taylor expansion gives ln(1+R)=R−R²/2+O(R³). This is a local approximation, not a license to replace large or near-total losses with the same number in log units. At R=−1 there is no finite real log return: ln(0) tends to −∞. At R<−1, terminal net wealth is negative and the real positive-wealth log formula is unavailable. A fully paid limited-liability long claim cannot lose more than its initial investment, but a financed or separately obligated position can require further payment; its equity return cannot be treated as a positive-wealth total-return index through insolvency.

Concavity explains the difference between arithmetic and geometric means. For positive growth factors, Jensen's inequality gives (1/T)Σln(Gt) ≤ ln[(1/T)ΣGt]. Exponentiating implies g≤R̄, with equality exactly when all realized growth factors are equal. For a random positive G with the required finite expectations, E[ln G]≤ln E[G]. Under independent identically distributed period returns with a finite mean, E[WT]=W0(1+E[R])T; independence is needed to factor the product's expectation. This expected wealth differs from exp(E[ln WT]), the exponential of expected log wealth. A “volatility drag” slogan does not remove those distinctions or prove that variance alone ranks every return distribution.

For a primary teaching route, Eric Zivot's return-calculation chapter treats simple and log returns, compounding and portfolio aggregation. Our examples derive their own dated wealth paths. The source's historical asset examples, data software and unrelated tax discussion are not inputs to this chapter.

4. Separate investment performance from investor cash timing

A client's deposit is not investment income. Let Vj− be portfolio value immediately before an external flow Cj, positive for a contribution into the account, and Vj+=Vj−+Cj immediately afterward. If nothing else changes at that instant, the flow itself creates no investment return. Measure each intervening subperiod from its post-flow opening value to its next pre-flow value. For positive denominators, the true time-weighted return is

1+TWR = ∏j [next pre-flow value / preceding post-flow value].

This links the growth of a hypothetical continuing unit of capital, eliminating the direct arithmetic effect of the external flows. It does not erase a flow's possible influence on actual trading costs, capacity or decisions. A contribution can change subsequent strategy performance even though it is not itself counted as profit. A security dividend retained inside the account is investment income, not an external investor contribution. If income is then paid out to the investor, include it in the pre-withdrawal value and record the withdrawal consistently.

The money-weighted return reflects the size and timing of investor flows. With investor receipts Fj positive and contributions negative, it is an IRR r satisfying ΣFj/(1+r)tj=0, including the final liquidation or attributed ending value. Periods tj must match the rate's unit. It can be a useful measure of the investor's experience or a decision-maker's control of capital calls. When the manager does not control external flows, it is not a clean isolation of security-selection performance. Report the measure suited to the question, often both.

Worked example 2 — Positive TWR, negative investor return

A separate account starts with 100 at date 0. Just before date 1's investor contribution it is worth 120. The investor contributes 180 immediately after that valuation, taking the account to 300. At date 2 the account is liquidated for 270. There are no other fees or flows.

Cash-flow boundary values

Initial investment100 after contribution−100

The first year's return is 120/100−1 = 20%; the second is 270/300−1 = −10%. TWR is 8% over two years, or 3.923048% annually. Yet the investor paid 280 and received 270: the net cash gain is −10. More capital experienced the losing second year.

Put x=1+r. The money-weighted equation is −100−180/x+270/x²=0, equivalently 100x²+180x=270. Its positive solution is x=(√351−9)/10, giving r=−2.650060% annually. Substitution reproduces the ending 270. Uniqueness here follows because 100x²+180x is strictly increasing for x>0, starting at zero and growing without bound. This proof does not extend to arbitrary cash-flow signs.

Suppose the beginning, contribution and final values are known, but the date-1 pre-flow valuation is missing. Another possible path has pre-flow value 90, post-flow value 270 and final value 270. Its TWR is 0.9×1−1 = −10%, while its investor IRR is unchanged. The endpoint and cash-flow records alone cannot identify true TWR.

When valuations at all flow boundaries are unavailable, an explicitly labeled approximation may be useful. The Modified Dietz calculation over a horizon T is

RMD = [Vend−Vstart−ΣCj] / [Vstart+ΣαjCj],   αj=(T−tj)/T.

Here α is the fraction of the horizon for which the contribution is invested; a withdrawal is negative. The numerator is investment gain, and the denominator approximates capital exposed over time. For Worked example 2, it is −10/(100+0.5×180) = −1/19 = −5.263158% over the entire two-year horizon. It equals neither true TWR nor the annual IRR. Large flows and uneven performance can make the approximation poor; a zero or negative denominator requires investigating the capital path instead of interpreting an ordinary positive-capital return.

The November 2020 GIPS Handbook's calculation discussion distinguishes flow-boundary valuation, geometric linking, money weighting and approximations such as Modified Dietz. Its fee discussion also distinguishes transaction costs from management fees. These are scoped methodological references; using the identities here is not a claim of GIPS compliance.

With repeated changes between investor receipts and payments, there can be multiple IRRs or none. A reported solver root is not proof of uniqueness. Revisit the capital-budgeting root analysis, inspect all cash dates and signs, and use an appropriate NPV comparison when making a decision. An account with no external flows has a money-weighted annual rate equal to its annualized terminal-wealth return; that special case does not eliminate the distinction when flows occur.

5. Reconcile costs, currency and purchasing power

A return before investment management fees may already include transaction costs. A return after management fees may still omit an investor's entry charge or taxes. “Gross” and “net” without a stated perimeter cannot settle a comparison. Fees can be based on opening assets, ending assets, committed capital, a hurdle or an incentive allocation, with different timing. Derive the cash consequences of the actual rule rather than subtracting a displayed annual percentage automatically.

Under the specific rule that a management fee f is charged on assets after the period's market return Rpre, the resulting growth factor is (1+Rpre)(1−f). Thus Rafter = Rpre−f−fRpre, not generally Rpre−f. Charging f times opening assets instead would produce the latter expression under that different rule. With positive assets and a nonnegative fee, the fee lowers wealth; a negative market return does not turn it into an investment contribution.

For a foreign investment, define S as home-currency units per one foreign-currency unit. If all foreign distributions are already captured in a foreign total-return growth factor GF, and the beginning and ending currency conversions use S0 and S1, then

Ghome = GF(S1/S0),   Ghome,real = Ghome/(1+πhome).

The first equation follows by converting one home unit to 1/S0 foreign units, growing that amount by GF, then converting back. With the reciprocal currency quote, invert the corresponding ratio. If foreign distributions are converted at earlier dates, value those conversions on their actual dates; a single terminal FX factor would describe a different policy. A currency hedge adds its own cash flows and costs. The second identity compares terminal money with the terminal price level: inflation belongs in the denominator, not in an exact subtraction formula. Log currency and inflation adjustments add or subtract only after taking logs of the corresponding positive growth factors.

6. Define the opportunity set in payoffs and budgets

Suppose asset i costs Pi,0>0 and delivers total terminal payoff Xi,s in state s. For one common horizon, Ri,s=Xi,s/Pi,0−1. Choose quantities ni before the state is known. With initial net wealth W0>0, full financing means ΣniPi,0=W0. Define beginning-wealth exposures wi=niPi,0/W0, so Σwi=1. Then

W1,s/W0 = Σwi(1+Ri,s),   Rp,s=ΣwiRi,s.

This is a budget identity, not a pricing model. It requires that distributions, costs and funding are consistently included. Weights measure beginning market-value exposures, not share-count fractions or end-of-period weights. For example, 150% in a risky asset funded with −50% in a borrowing account is a fully financed exposure, not a portfolio missing half its budget. If borrowing and lending have different rates or short positions incur separate costs, use the actual signed funding terms. A zero-initial-cost payoff can have a meaningful monetary profit but has no standalone return obtained by dividing by zero.

Given feasible quantities, the investment opportunity set is the set of achievable payoff vectors, or equivalently the induced return distributions for a specified initial wealth. With fractional positions, only long positions, no costs and full investment, weights lie in the simplex wi≥0, Σwi=1. For a fixed initial budget W0, attainable payoff vectors are convex combinations of the budget-normalized vectors W0Xi/Pi,0; these describe investing the entire budget in each asset. Equivalently, attainable portfolio-return vectors are convex combinations of asset-return vectors. Raw per-unit payoff vectors Xi cannot be combined using wealth weights without accounting for unequal purchase prices. Borrowing, shorts, holding limits, transaction costs, integer lots and liability requirements change that set. A point outside the mandate is not an alternative merely because its expected return is attractive.

If physical probabilities ps are supplied, E[Rp]=ΣpsRp,s=ΣwiE[Ri] by finite-sum rearrangement. These probabilities describe an assumed distribution; they are not automatically the probabilities used to price claims. A list of historical observations does not by itself justify treating each as an exhaustive equally likely future state. State-payoff pricing is developed later in this volume.

For positive portfolio wealth, its log return is ln[ΣwiGi], not Σwiln(Gi). The logarithm transforms total portfolio wealth after combining the assets. For nonnegative weights summing to one and positive asset growth factors, concavity implies ln(ΣwiGi)≥Σwiln(Gi), with equality when all positively weighted growth factors coincide. That Jensen comparison does not apply unchanged to negative weights. Neither expression makes a state-by-state decision to own only the eventual winner available before the state occurs.

This is a one-dimensional feasible-set calculation. It does not prove that this portfolio is best for every risk preference, that the three states exhaust actual risk, or that its expectation is a required equilibrium return. If the mandate instead includes an additional stress state or a different loss floor, recompute the set. 7. Keep weights and benchmarks consistent through time

The return identity uses each interval's beginning weights. In a buy-and-hold portfolio without intermediate external flows, weights generally drift: wi,tafter = wi,t−1Gi,t/Gp,t. Replacing those weights with the initial weights every period describes a rebalanced strategy, requiring trades. Using end weights for the interval just completed uses information from its outcome and usually misstates the actual return.

The lab illustrates this with 100 split equally between A and B. Annual returns are A: +40%, then −25%; B: −10%, then +20%. After Year 1, holdings are 70 and 45, so next year's buy-and-hold weights are 14/23 and 9/23. Final wealth is 70×0.75+45×1.20 = 106.5. Resetting each holding to 57.5 at date 1 instead produces 57.5×0.75+57.5×1.20 = 112.125. The resulting cumulative returns are 6.5% and 12.125%. These are two strategies on this path, before any trading costs. The example is not evidence that rebalancing must outperform; continued relative strength of the winner can reverse the ranking.

A benchmark is a specified comparison strategy or index, with a currency, opportunity universe, weighting/rebalancing policy and distribution treatment. A price-only index cannot silently stand in for a reinvested total-return alternative. A benchmark before costs and a client after costs can both be useful, but the difference includes the unmatched cost treatment and must be labeled. Evaluating a client's money-weighted experience against a benchmark may require applying that client's same dated flows to the benchmark strategy; comparing its simple index return directly to the account IRR answers a different question.

For a common period, the arithmetic active return is Rp−Rb, stated in percentage points. A relative wealth return is Gp/Gb−1, defined when benchmark growth is positive. At portfolio return 8% and benchmark return 5%, these are 3 percentage points and 1/35 = 2.857143%, respectively. Across periods, relative growth factors multiply; arithmetic active returns do not simply compound as 1+(Rp−Rb). Neither a positive active return nor a higher money-weighted return establishes skill, risk-adjusted alpha or causal manager value. Those require models and evidence developed later.

8. Reproduce and challenge the measurement

The Serein lab provides synthetic inputs, standard-library Python analysis, preserved results, a file-integrity manifest and a complete execution guide. Put the five files together and run python3 v09-c01-analysis.py. It requires no account, data download or package installation. Try the calculations before opening the benchmark.

The model keeps monetary arithmetic and most returns as exact rational strings. Logs and roots are finite floating-point values; JSON null represents the absence of a finite log at zero wealth. It reports the two dividend strategies, TWR and unique bounded-case IRR, explicit fee balances, currency and purchasing-power conversion, a state-constrained opportunity set and both rebalancing policies. It is a small teaching model, not a general performance-reporting or tax engine.

Check investment profit separately from external cash. Reconstruct reinvestment with actual shares rather than only multiplying a supplied index. Price the state-floor mix from quantities at the stated prices. Change one input in a copy and predict which results should and should not move: moving the date-1 valuation with fixed investor flows changes TWR but not IRR; tightening the loss floor changes feasible weights; changing FX does not change foreign-currency performance. Those invariants make stronger checks than comparing one rounded display.

  5. Local after-fee growth is 1.15×0.98=1.127. Multiplying by the FX factor 0.92 gives home nominal growth 1.03684, a 3.684% nominal return. Divide by 1.03 for real growth: 25921/25750, so real return is 171/25750≈0.664078%. Subtraction ignores both the management fee's post-return base and cross-products in currency conversion; inflation is an exact division by the price-level factor. A different fee base or intermediate distribution-conversion policy would require a different cash sequence.

  6. After Year 1, holdings are 125 and 100, total 225, so both strategies return 12.5%. Buy-and-hold begins Year 2 at weights 5/9 and 4/9. It ends with 125×0.8+100×1.1=210: Year 2 return −1/15=−6.666667%, cumulative return 5%. Rebalancing sets each holding to 112.5, ending with 90+123.75=213.75: Year 2 return −5%, cumulative return 6.875%. Year 1's portfolio log return is ln(1.125), whereas the average asset log is ½ln(1.25)+½ln(1)=ln(√1.25); √1.25≠1.125. Under the changed second year, buy-and-hold ends at 150+110=260, while rebalancing ends at 135+123.75=258.75. The respective cumulative returns are 30% and 29.375%, reversing the ranking. None of these comparisons includes trading costs.

  7. The income tax is 4×0.25=1; the capital-gain tax is (112−100)×0.20=2.4. Terminal after-tax wealth is 112+4−1−2.4=112.6. Simple return is 12.6%; log return is ln(1.126)≈0.118671530. A tax on the whole 116 would incorrectly tax returned principal and combine two different bases and rates. With a zero terminal payoff and positive initial cost, the separate long claim has simple return −100%, growth factor zero and no finite log return. A missing log must not be replaced by zero, which would falsely report no loss.

  8. The down-state condition is 0.12−0.32w≥−0.02, hence w≤7/16. The middle state is always above the floor on [0,1], while the up state requires −0.04+0.34w≥−0.02, or w≥1/17. Thus the complete feasible interval is [1/17,7/16], not [0,7/16]. Expected return increases with w, so the highest-mean feasible weight is 7/16, producing −2%, 6.625%, 10.875% and mean 177/3200=5.53125%. Check every state rather than copying the previously binding constraint.

    The proposed report uses outcome-dependent weights for an earlier holding interval; it substitutes hindsight selection for a strategy feasible before the state; and it conflates unmatched distribution treatment and raw outperformance with risk-adjusted skill. A replacement needs dated quantities and external flows, the actual decision rule and information available when chosen, consistent costs/currency/total-return benchmarks, and a justified risk model plus an appropriately designed empirical assessment. Credit a precise conditional alternative; no particular investment opinion is required.

Teach-back and delayed retention

Explain in five minutes why the client in Worked example 2 loses cash while TWR is positive. Then explain why a weighted average of asset log returns is not the portfolio's log return. A successful explanation reconstructs wealth and timing rather than reciting names; a claim that one measure must be “wrong” requires repair.
