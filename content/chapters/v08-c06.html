Capability and prerequisites

You can choose a payout and liquidity policy that respects investment needs, dated cash and actual financing constraints. Use incremental NPV, FCFE and the same-date equity bridge, risk-matched discounting and the capital-structure and financing-frictions contract. The question is not simply whether a company has positive earnings or whether a repurchase raises EPS. Ask what owners receive, what claim remains, which investments still get funded, and what happens in the states where cash is scarce.

A board proposes a payout of 25 because the loan agreement permits distributions up to 25. Treasury objects that the largest payout compatible with its supplied operating scenarios is only 10. Both statements can be correct: a contractual ceiling is not a source of cash. We first establish a frictionless payout benchmark, then identify the specific assumptions that fail in this treasury decision.

1. Identify the payment and the remaining ownership claim

A cash dividend distributes cash to the entitled shares while leaving the number of shares unchanged. A share repurchase pays selling holders and reduces the shares outstanding for our calculations. Its authorization is permission to buy, not proof that any cash was spent. A program may be partly executed or offset by new issuance. Track actual dividends, actual repurchases, cash raised from owners and the outstanding-share count separately. A share split or stock dividend changes units of ownership without itself sending cash out of the business.

A regular dividend can establish an expectation of repeated payment; a special dividend can communicate a particular distribution without the same intended recurrence. Neither description proves sustainability. Market purchase programs and offers to buy a specified quantity at a stated price also differ in execution discretion and who sells. We do not infer current exchange, tax or legal rules from these descriptions. Announcement, entitlement and payment dates can differ; our algebra below places the cash transfer and valuation comparison at one instant. With a later payment, the entitled cash claim must be valued at its actual date.

Distinguish four quantities. Accounting earnings measure profit under a recognition system. FCFE is a modeled shareholder cash residual after investment and financing. Accessible cash is money that can actually be used at a date. Permitted payout is limited by the supplied contracts and other applicable restrictions. A firm can have positive earnings and FCFE while a receivable, blocked deposit, debt maturity or future project creates a cash shortage. A payout policy should not substitute one of these quantities for another.

2. Derive the benchmark equivalence

Assume fixed operating and investment policy; sufficient genuinely excess cash worth its face amount; no tax, transaction costs, asymmetric information or financing consequences; and fair pricing of divisible shares. Let E be ordinary-equity value immediately before payout, N shares outstanding and P = E/N the fair share price. Distribute Q, with 0 ≤ Q < E. The MIT payout-policy notes present irrelevance under perfect-market conditions; the following equations show precisely what is held fixed in our original case.

With a cash dividend, d = Q/N goes to each share, equity remaining in the firm is E−Q and the ex-distribution share value is (E−Q)/N. A holder of h shares has:

Wealth after dividend = h(Q/N) + h[(E−Q)/N] = hE/N.

With a repurchase at the fair pre-payout price P, the firm retires q = Q/P shares. Remaining shares are N′ = N−Q/P, so:

Post-repurchase price = (E−Q)/(N−Q/P) = P,
because E = NP and E−Q = P(N−Q/P).

A nonselling owner keeps h shares worth hP; the ownership percentage increases but applies to a smaller equity value. An owner who participates proportionately sells hQ/E shares, receives hQ/N and retains shares worth h(E−Q)/N. The cash-plus-share wealth is the same as under the dividend. Conversely, an owner can manufacture cash by selling a suitable fraction of a no-dividend holding or reinvest a dividend to maintain exposure, under the same frictionless trading assumptions. The theorem compares total claims, not just quoted prices.

Worked example 1 — Portico's equal wealth and unequal EPS

Portico Instruments is a fictional all-equity company with operating value 800 kCU and genuinely excess cash 200 kCU, hence equity value 1,000 kCU. There are 100 thousand shares, worth 10 CU each. The cash is unnecessary for its already fixed investment plan. A payout of 200 happens immediately before the next full forecast year. There are no taxes, costs or new information. Repurchased shares cease to be outstanding immediately.

The dividend's price decline from 10 to 8 is not an extra loss in addition to the dividend: it removes cash already transferred to the holder. Actual observed price changes can also reflect news, taxes and trading conditions, so the benchmark is not a prediction of every market quote.

Next-year income before payout is supplied as 100, including 10 earned on the 200 cash at 5%. Operations still earn 90 after either payout. A full-year dividend policy therefore has EPS 90/100 = 0.90 CU; a full-year repurchase has EPS 90/80 = 1.125 CU, up 12.5% from the original EPS of 1. The repurchase created no benchmark wealth. Its smaller denominator outweighed the foregone cash income. Historical reported EPS with weighted-average shares is a different measurement from this explicitly dated forward EPS comparison.

For a cash-financed repurchase, let forecast income before payout be Y and the applicable after-tax cash-income rate be y. Then EPS after repurchase at price p is (Y−yQ)/(N−Q/p), assuming unchanged operations and no other changes. For Q > 0 and a positive share denominator, EPS rises exactly when Y/N > yp. At fair p = E/N this is an earnings-yield comparison Y/E > y, not a value-creation test. Borrowing to repurchase changes interest, risk and financing constraints as well; substituting an interest cost into an EPS calculation does not establish an economically attractive trade.

3. Identify transfers when execution differs from value

Keep the same total pre-trade intrinsic equity E and payout Q, but stipulate an execution price p different from P = E/N. The continuing operations and cash removed are unchanged; remaining intrinsic equity is E−Q. There are N−Q/p remaining shares, provided this number is positive. Relative to the remaining shares' pre-trade intrinsic value, their aggregate gain is:

Gremain = (E−Q) − (N−Q/p)P = Q(P/p−1).
Gsell = Q − (Q/p)P = −Gremain.

This is a redistribution between owners, not new operating value. The intrinsic price used for the comparison must be supported independently. A manager's announcement that shares are cheap is not such evidence. If every owner knows the same fair value and can freely wait, nobody is compelled to tender below it. Underpriced executions therefore need a stated mechanism such as different information, liquidity needs or constraints; they cannot be assumed to be unlimited free gains.

Worked example 2 — Who gains from Portico's price?

Portico still spends 200. At a hypothetical execution price 8, it buys 25 thousand shares and leaves 75 thousand. Post-trade intrinsic value is 800/75 = 32/3 ≈ 10.6667 CU per share. Sellers receive 200 for claims previously worth 250, losing 50; remaining owners' claims rise from 750 to 800, gaining 50.

At execution price 12.5, only 16 thousand shares are bought and 84 thousand remain. Their value is 800/84 = 200/21 ≈ 9.5238 each. Sellers receive 200 for claims previously worth 160, gaining 40; remaining owners lose 40. EPS still rises to 90/84 = 15/14 ≈ 1.0714, although continuing holders lose value. This directly defeats the claim that EPS accretion proves a repurchase is good for nonselling owners.

An owner who sells exactly the same proportion of each holding as the company repurchases bears both sides proportionally: cash plus remaining intrinsic value is unchanged in this costless model even at either stipulated price. Distributional consequences depend on participation. If the transaction changes taxes, fees, investment or information, total value can change too; then identify that mechanism separately from the transfer.

4. Retain cash for a reason, and account for its cost

Keeping a freely accessible unit of cash that earns a competitive risk-matched return need not destroy a unit of value. It may support an investment, meet seasonality, avoid costly emergency financing or protect against a shortfall when outside funding is unreliable. A cash asset and risky operating profits should not automatically be discounted at the same rate. Conversely, calling every retained balance “financial flexibility” does not establish its benefit.

For an original narrow example, management irrevocably locks 40 kCU for one year at 1%, then distributes 40.4. There is no liquidity or investment benefit, no tax and no interim access. The comparable certain outside return is 5%, so the value of this restricted policy is 40.4/1.05 = 808/21 ≈ 38.4762, versus 40 distributed now. The loss is 32/21 ≈ 1.5238. That loss follows from the particular low-return restriction. It would be wrong to value cash freely withdrawable today below 40 merely by assuming owners must wait.

The agency hypothesis says that excess resources can support wasteful investment or private benefits when managers' incentives differ from owners'. Distributing cash can reduce that opportunity, but insufficient liquidity can also destroy valuable investment. Monitoring, credible investment budgets, compensation and payout policy are complementary choices rather than substitutes by definition. A sustainable regular dividend, an occasional special distribution and a discretionary repurchase program can create different expectations and adjustment costs.

5. Treat taxes and information as explicit frictions

Taxes can distinguish otherwise equivalent distributions. A dividend may be taxed on its gross amount, while a sale may recognize proceeds less the sold shares' basis; remaining shares retain a tax position. Rates, realization rules, exemptions and company-level costs must be specified for the relevant holder and transaction. Exercise 4 supplies fictional rules and liquidates both remaining holdings so that comparing cash received does not hide different remaining tax liabilities. No tax rate or sale treatment in that exercise is asserted to be current law.

Payout announcements may reveal management's assessment of future cash, the lack of attractive investments, concern about undervaluation or a desire to constrain discretionary spending. A formal signaling explanation needs more than a price reaction: the action must convey information, be interpreted through beliefs, and be sufficiently different in cost or attractiveness across types to discourage imitation. If weak and strong firms can finance the same payout equally cheaply, the proposed separating mechanism fails. Borrowing to fund a payout may conceal operating weakness rather than establish strength.

Historical evidence should discipline these stories. Brav, Graham, Harvey and Michaely's 2005 study surveyed 384 financial executives and separately interviewed 23. Respondents often regarded repurchases as more flexible than maintained dividends and paid attention to EPS; their accounts gave limited support to deliberate costly signaling. Those are dated reported views, not a universal rule or proof of causal effects. The paper also discusses the survey's selection and interpretation limits. Our calculations test mechanisms without treating a reported motive as evidence of fair value.

6. Build a dated liquidity contract

Use a cash calendar, not an annual average. For each date and state, start with accessible cash, add receipts and committed financing available then, and subtract operating payments, tax, interest, principal, investment and payout. Cash required for normal activity is not excess merely because it has not yet been spent. Customer delays increase receivables; supplier prepayments create assets until applied. Both can absorb cash without a contemporaneous expense or economic loss.

A line's headline amount is only one limit. Existing utilization, total-debt covenants, collateral conditions, maturity, fees and counterparty capacity can constrain actual access. Acharya, Almeida, Ippolito and Perez's monitored-liquidity model explains one mechanism through which monitoring and revocation can limit credit-line insurance. We do not import that model's rules into the case below: Dunlin has an expressly supplied commitment with only the stated conditions. The point is to read the applicable contract rather than identify a line balance with cash already in hand.

Worked example 3 — Dunlin's payout, working capital and investment

Dunlin Packaging is another original fictional firm. Money is kCU. Dates 0, 1 and 2 are 1 January, 1 April and 1 July 2027, with each interval treated as one quarter. All figures are hypothetical. Opening accessible cash is 60; term principal is 50. The company pays initial payout Q at date 0 from existing cash. Its fictional agreement permits at most 25 at that date, with no revolver borrowing to make the initial payout. Minimum cash is 20 after each modeled payment batch.

Base operating receipts are 100 at date 1 and 120 at date 2; base operating payments are 95 and 105. These numbers exclude financing and the separate project. The state is learned at date 1, after Q has irrevocably been paid. The following timing changes leave total operating cash over the two dates equal to 20. There are no supplied state probabilities, so we test every listed state.

Corporate tax and interest on cash are zero in this case. Term debt and revolver both charge 4% per quarter on opening principal. Term principal remains 50 through date-2 interest; then 10 is repaid, leaving 40 beyond this planning horizon. Term interest is therefore 2 at each date. The revolver starts at zero, can be drawn for date-1 operating and project needs, and must be repaid in full at date 2, with no rollover. There are no fees, other draw conditions or unlisted equity contributions. Draws can be synchronized with date-1 payments; cash and debt limits are checked after that batch. New date-1 borrowing incurs interest only from date 1 to date 2.

Q ≤ 60 + O1 − 2 − 30 + 30 − 20.

Reducing the planned payout before date 0 preserves the investment without pretending future receipts or an unapproved equity issue are cash today.

7. Turn the cash test into a defensible policy

For Dunlin, 10 is the largest payout that meets all supplied states and dates, not a universally optimal payout. It leaves no usable debt headroom in the combined state even though the nominal line has 10 undrawn: the total-debt covenant is binding. A policy of Q = 8 leaves 2 of usable date-1 headroom in that state; cash still closes at 20 after a draw of 28. Its date-2 cash is 64 normally, 63.20 with slow collection and 62.88 in the combined state. These higher cash balances partly reflect 2 less already paid to owners; they are not themselves evidence of 2 of new firm value.

An illustrative board choice is 8 now while retaining authority to reconsider a later distribution after collection and funding risks are resolved. That sacrifices immediate access to 2 in exchange for an explicitly measured extra margin. A choice of 10 is defensible if the board accepts the stated boundary and has evidence that the scenario set and commitments are adequate. A smaller amount can be defended by additional evidenced risks. Without probabilities, risk preferences, costs of cash retention and credible alternative financing, the model establishes feasibility rather than a unique expected-value optimum.

Preserve the order of reasoning: establish viable operating and investment choices; map dated cash and debt; assess uncertainty and accessible financing; choose a payout amount; then choose an execution method using valuation, taxes and owner participation. An investment with negative NPV should not be retained merely to avoid paying cash, while a payout target should not automatically displace a valuable project. Delay a discretionary repurchase before treating a binding essential payment as optional. The applicable contract determines which payments can actually be changed.

A decision memo should state the amount and date, the investment being protected, the binding state and constraint, the cash/funding headroom, and the evidence that could change the recommendation. Monitor collections, supplier terms, actual repurchase executions and dilution, rather than only announced authorization and annual EPS. If a line's commitment, a customer's payment or a project's payoff is uncertain, model that uncertainty directly. Scenario coverage at a few dates is not a guarantee of cash sufficiency every day; Dunlin's synchronized batches are an explicit teaching simplification.

A repurchase retires 10 thousand shares and leaves 50 thousand at price 12.

    The owner receives 12 kCU as dividend and retains shares worth 60, totaling 72. A proportionate repurchase sells 1 thousand shares for 12 and leaves 5 thousand worth 60, also 72. Acceptable assumptions include fixed investment/operating value, truly excess cash, no taxes, no transaction or financing costs, no information change, and divisible fair-price trading. Answer: Portico's fixed investments, excess cash, fair-price trading and absence of taxes/costs preserve cash-plus-share wealth; the methods divide the same claims differently.
