Build the smallest model that answers the question

Capability. Develop a parsimonious model with explicit assumptions, an equilibrium argument, interpretable comparative statics and observable implications. The prerequisite is research questions and design contracts; bring the distinction between a mechanism, an estimand and evidence. Optimization and competitive equilibrium from the graduate theory sequence supply the mathematical tools. The original constructions below concern credit allocation, our macro-finance specialization, but are static partial-equilibrium teaching models. Their numerical values are invented. They are neither fitted banking models nor new research findings.

Suppose a bank loses internal funding and its lending falls. Is total borrower credit necessarily reduced by the same amount? A useful theory separates the bank's financing margin, borrower demand and substitution to other lenders. Begin with one margin whose logic you can prove. Then add the omitted margin that matters for the question. A larger model is useful when its extra structure changes an inference or connects a previously unmeasured object to evidence; additional equations alone do not establish progress.

The deliverable is a model memo containing a question, primitives, choices and timing, equilibrium definition, solved result with conditions, economic mechanism, observable restrictions and a justified extension. The organization follows the bounded methodological guidance in Board and Meyer-ter-Vehn's October 2018 theory-writing note: explain what the model accomplishes and make its primitives and reasoning legible. The models and proofs here are original instructional constructions, not examples taken from that note.

1. Turn a mechanism into primitives and choices

Primitives are the preferences, technologies, endowments and institutional rules taken as given within the model. Endogenous variables are determined by agents' choices and consistency conditions. An equilibrium requires both individual optimality under the stated information and market consistency. A solution to one first-order condition does not establish all three. A comparative static compares equilibria as a primitive changes; it is not automatically a time path or a causal estimate from observational data.

Ask: holding borrower demand and financing technology fixed, how does a change in internal funding capacity D affect credit quantity and its price? There is one homogeneous divisible loan service, one period, a competitive lending sector and an aggregate borrower. Quantity Q is in normalized loan units. R is a price in numeraire per loan unit, not an annualized interest rate or a percentage. Total benefits and costs are in numeraire. There is no default, collateral, maturity transformation, borrower heterogeneity or aggregate income feedback. This deliberately narrow object cannot answer whether a real policy raises national investment or welfare.

The positive-part cost means internal capacity is available at the same opportunity cost c as the base component of external finance; only the extra financing friction differs. D is not free social wealth: its acquisition cost is outside this conditional model. Do not add D to output or subtract it from total cost a second time. Perfect competition and an aggregate benefit function also exclude bargaining and distributional weights. Each simplification serves the funding-margin question; each limits its interpretation.

Derive choices before solving market clearing. Strict concavity of borrower utility gives demand Q=max{(a−R)/b,0}. The lending objective has derivative R−c−γ(q−D)+. If R<c it decreases immediately and q=0. At R=c every q in [0,D] maximizes it; supply is a correspondence, not a single number. If R>c it increases through D and decreases beyond q=D+(R−c)/γ, which is its unique maximizer. These are global optima because the objectives are concave and their derivatives have the stated signs; the negative quadratic term prevents unbounded profit at high q. The interpretation is the usual price-taking profit choice discussed in the retained Condie supply notes, §3.2; existence for this particular cost is proved here, rather than inferred from a generic closed production set.

2. Solve the equilibrium and prove the claim's scope

Let θ=a−c. If a<c, zero trade is the unique allocation and every R in [a,c] clears the market: borrowers demand zero and lenders optimally supply zero. Thus allocation uniqueness does not imply price uniqueness. If a=c the allocation remains zero and R=c. If a>c, write q̄=θ/b. The equilibrium is

D≥q̄: Q=q̄, R=c;   D<q̄: Q=(θ+γD)/(b+γ), R=a−bQ=c+γ(Q−D).

Existence and uniqueness proof. Consider S(Q)=B(Q)−C(Q). Its derivative S′(Q)=θ−bQ−γ(Q−D)+ is continuous and strictly decreasing, and S(Q) tends to minus infinity as Q tends to infinity. If θ≤0 the unique maximum is Q=0. If θ>0, the derivative starts positive and has exactly one zero. If that zero is at or below D, solving θ−bQ=0 gives q̄ with the stated condition. Otherwise solving θ−bQ−γ(Q−D)=0 gives the second expression, which exceeds D precisely when D<q̄. Set R=c in the first positive-trade branch and R=a−bQ in the second. The demand and supply choices derived above then coincide, so these candidates are actual competitive equilibria. Conversely, any positive-trade equilibrium has borrower marginal benefit equal to price and price equal to marginal cost, hence S′(Q)=0; uniqueness of its root gives uniqueness of quantity and price. The separate zero-trade price interval completes the proof. Maximizing surplus alone would not have established decentralization without the choice verification.

In the binding funding branch, a small rise in D lowers marginal financing cost, induces more credit and moves the borrower down the demand curve. Direct differentiation yields

∂Q/∂D=γ/(b+γ),   ∂R/∂D=−bγ/(b+γ);
∂Q/∂a=1/(b+γ),   ∂R/∂a=γ/(b+γ);
∂Q/∂γ=[bD−θ]/(b+γ)²<0.

These derivatives hold while the branch remains binding. In the slack branch ∂Q/∂D=∂R/∂D=0; ∂Q/∂a=1/b and ∂R/∂a=0 while slackness persists. At D=q̄, the quantity and price levels agree across branches but the one-sided D derivatives differ. A finite intervention crossing that boundary requires the piecewise formulas, not one derivative multiplied by the whole change. The strict sign for the γ derivative also disappears in the slack region. Conditions belong in the proposition, not in a footnote after an unconditional headline.

Worked case 1. A solved funding mechanism and its failure boundary

Take (10,1,2,1,2) for (a,b,c,γ,D). Then q̄=8 and D=2 is binding. Substitution gives (5,5,3) for (Q,R,e). Directly, borrower marginal benefit is 10−5=5; marginal cost is 2+(5−2)=5. At that price the borrower and supplier independently choose five. A one-unit reduction in D, remaining in the binding branch, reduces Q by one-half and raises R by one-half. The price increase moderates borrower demand; it is why lending does not fall one for one with capacity.

The allocation accounts also reconcile. At Q=5, B=75/2 and C=29/2. Borrower surplus B−RQ=25/2, lender profit RQ−C=21/2, and their sum B−C is 23. In that order these quantities are (75/2,29/2,25/2,21/2,23). The price payment cancels; treating it as a social resource cost would double count. Surplus is conditional on D and on these complete benefit/cost specifications; the model has not priced a policy that changes D.

For D=0,8,10, respectively, the (Q,R,e) rows are ((4,6,4),(8,2,0),(8,2,0)). Capacity loss from two to zero lowers credit by one and raises price by one. But increasing D from eight to ten changes neither price nor credit. The blanket claim “more internal funds always expand credit” is false in this same model. The failure is economically informative: once the marginal financing premium is absent, additional capacity has no effect without another margin.

Observable implication. Conditional on fixed a,b,c,γ, a binding decrease in D predicts lower Q and higher R. A demand-intercept decrease predicts lower Q and lower R. These opposite price signs can help organize a study, but a price–quantity pattern alone does not prove which primitive changed. Measurement of loan quality, fees and maturities must correspond to the homogeneous price; changes in borrower composition can alter measured averages. A joint fall in price and credit contradicts the pure binding funding-loss comparative static under the maintained conditions, not every possible funding mechanism.

3. Add one omitted margin and resolve the whole model

The baseline equates lending at the modeled sector with total borrower credit. To investigate replacement borrowing, add an alternative lender with quantity z≥0 and cost hz+z²/(2κ), where h≥c and κ>0. Its price-taking supply is z=κ(R−h)+: the derivative of profit is R−h−z/κ, giving zero at the corner when R≤h and the interior expression otherwise. Larger κ means a more elastic alternative supply schedule. Let x denote incumbent lending; borrower total credit is Q=x+z, and the original borrower demand remains unchanged. The new equilibrium requires both suppliers' optimality and that total clearing condition. The extension changes a market, not merely an accounting label.

For a>c first solve the baseline, obtaining price R₀. If R₀≤h, alternative supply is zero and the baseline remains an equilibrium. If R₀>h, both suppliers use their increasing branches and

H=1+b/γ+bκ;
R=(a−bD+bc/γ+bκh)/H;
x=D+(R−c)/γ,   z=κ(R−h),   Q=(a−R)/b=x+z.

Branch and uniqueness proof. At R=h, alternative supply is zero and demand exceeds incumbent supply because the baseline clearing price is higher. At R=R₀, positive alternative supply makes total supply exceed demand. Between these prices, demand strictly decreases and total supply strictly increases continuously. There is exactly one clearing price in (h,R₀); since h≥c it indeed lies on both increasing branches. Substituting these branches into (a−R)/b=D+(R−c)/γ+κ(R−h) and collecting coefficients gives the displayed formula. When the alternative is inactive, the baseline proof applies, including its supply interval at c. For a≤c, no trade still clears at R in [a,c] because h≥c. Thus a formula derived for active replacement lending is never used to produce negative z. The restriction h≥c simplifies the exhaustive branch classification; changing that restriction requires a new classification.

While both branches are active, differentiating the clearing equation gives ∂R/∂D=−b/H, ∂x/∂D=(1+bκ)/H, ∂z/∂D=−bκ/H and ∂Q/∂D=1/H. The derivatives add correctly: ∂x/∂D+∂z/∂D=∂Q/∂D. Since κ>0, the total-credit response is smaller than the baseline response 1/(1+b/γ). An incumbent funding contraction increases alternative lending and partly offsets the incumbent's fall. This conclusion is local to the stated regimes and common homogeneous borrower market. It does not assume every real borrower can switch lenders.

Worked case 2. A complete borrower-substitution extension

Keep the baseline inputs and set (2,1) for (h,κ). Baseline R₀=5 exceeds h=2, so both suppliers are active. H=3 and R=(10−2+2+2)/3=4. Thus (4,4,2,6) gives (R,x,z,Q). Borrower demand is six, and incumbent four plus alternative two clears it. The extension predicts more total credit and a lower price than the original five-unit equilibrium, conditional on the new technology being available.

Now reduce D from two to zero. The new (R,x,z,Q) tuple is (14/3,8/3,8/3,16/3). The changes (Δx,Δz,ΔQ) are (-4/3,2/3,-2/3). Incumbent lending falls by twice as much as total borrower credit. Both prices exceed h=c, so using the active formulas is justified throughout this experiment. The derivative vector (∂R/∂D,∂x/∂D,∂z/∂D,∂Q/∂D) is (-1/3,2/3,-1/3,1/3).

Here is the completed empirical bridge: measure incumbent funding capacity before the shock, incumbent lending x, alternative lending z and borrower total Q with compatible units and borrower identifiers. A pure capacity loss predicts x down, z up and Q down less than x, conditional on the maintained demand and cost schedules. Matched credit totals are required; a file containing only incumbent accounts cannot test replacement. A rise in alternative lending is compatible with this mechanism but does not identify it, since changing demand or alternative costs may also move z. The C05 distinction between lender and borrower outcomes survives the theory exercise.

As a regime check, replace h=2 with h=6. The baseline price five is below six, so the correct solution is (5,5,0,5) for (R,x,z,Q). Blindly applying the active formula produces R=16/3<6 and z=−2/3, violating z≥0. That candidate must be rejected, not interpreted as lending withdrawals or short credit. The correct corner is an economic change in participation.

4. Contrast mechanisms without pretending to identify them

Test whether price taking is essential by returning to a single supplier and giving it market power. This is a separate extension, not an unannounced combination with alternative lending. A monopolist chooses quantity q anticipating R(q)=a−bq, with the original cost. Its profit is π(q)=(a−c)q−bq²−γ(q−D)+²/2. The feasible domain is 0≤q≤a/b so the inverse-demand price is nonnegative. A monopoly allocation is its profit-maximizing quantity together with the price that clears borrower demand; no price-taking optimality is attributed to the supplier.

Solution and proof. For a≤c, π′(0)≤0 and strict concavity gives q=0. For a>c, π′(q)=θ−2bq−γ(q−D)+ strictly decreases from a positive number to a negative number by q=a/b, so its unique zero is the unique global maximum in the feasible interval. With q̄M=θ/(2b), the solution is qM=q̄M if D≥q̄M, and qM=(θ+γD)/(2b+γ) if D<q̄M. Set RM=a−bqM. In the binding branch ∂qM/∂D=γ/(2b+γ), smaller than the competitive binding derivative. In the slack branch it is zero. Comparisons require checking each model's branch; its funding threshold is different.

For identical primitives with a>c, monopoly quantity is strictly below competitive quantity. Indeed, at the positive competitive optimum S′(Q)=0 while π′(Q)=S′(Q)−bQ<0. Since π′ starts positive and strictly decreases, its zero occurs before Q; inverse demand then gives a higher monopoly price. This proof works even when the two optima lie in different financing branches. The mechanism is marginal revenue below price, not an extra resource cost. Total surplus remains B−C under the maintained welfare definition; monopoly profit and total surplus are distinct objects.

Worked case 3. Market power and an observationally equivalent rival

Using the baseline primitives, q̄M=4>D=2. The monopoly (q,R,e) tuple is (10/3,20/3,4/3). Profit is 8(10/3)−(10/3)²−(4/3)²/2=44/3. Borrower surplus is bq²/2=50/9; total surplus is 182/9. Compared with competitive surplus 23, the loss is 25/9. In order, (profit,borrower surplus,total surplus,loss) is (44/3,50/9,182/9,25/9). Because both quantities exceed D, surplus here is a quadratic with curvature −(b+γ); the independent loss calculation (b+γ)(5−10/3)²/2 also gives 25/9. This is an allocative comparison holding primitives fixed, not a measured cost of real banking concentration.

Could observing Q=5,R=5 establish competition? No. A monopoly with (6,1/5,1,1,2) for (a,b,c,γ,D) also produces (5,5,3) for (q,R,e). Here marginal revenue is 6−2(1/5)5=4 and marginal cost is 1+(5−2)=4; inverse demand is 6−(1/5)5=5. The original competitive model and this different monopoly parameterization therefore fit the same single price–quantity observation. They need not agree on shocks. The monopoly's binding D slope is 5/7, versus the baseline competitive slope 1/2. Those numbers are (5/7,1/2); their difference is not an identified empirical contrast unless the shift and maintained primitives are independently justified.

The completed model-comparison memo is: “Price and quantity levels alone leave demand and cost primitives, hence conduct, unresolved. If demand slope and financing cost are independently restricted, compare the price-taking margin R=C′ with the monopoly margin R−bq=C′. If an exogenous capacity shift can be measured with stable demand and cost schedules, examine the different predicted responses. Do not choose the mechanism by whichever calibration matches one point.” Do not compare welfare across the two observationally equivalent parameterizations as though preferences and costs were unchanged.

5. Make a result useful to research

A model disciplines a claim by showing which assumptions jointly imply it. A successful prediction does not verify every assumption; competing structures may share it. A failed prediction rejects the joint maintained description plus its measurement bridge, not necessarily the preferred single assumption. Preserve this asymmetry in writing. “The model predicts replacement credit when both lenders are active” is supported by the proof. “Our market's replacement credit is caused by this mechanism” additionally needs evidence, a design and an account of rival explanations.

Develop a baseline by solving the smallest coherent economy that contains the proposed margin. Check dimensions, budgets, feasibility and corners before decorating it. Write a claim with explicit quantifiers and conditions; prove it and search deliberately for a boundary that breaks a stronger claim. Translate endogenous objects into observable definitions, naming which primitives must be held fixed. Then choose an extension because it changes that bridge: alternative lenders distinguish institution from borrower outcomes; market power changes the margin determining price. Re-solve equilibrium after the change. A sensitivity exercise that keeps the old choice rule despite new incentives is not a solved extension.

A usable final memo for the funding question states: “Under homogeneous borrowing, price taking and binding convex external-finance costs, lower internal capacity raises loan price and reduces lending. Alternative active supply attenuates the total-credit loss through replacement. Slack funding removes the capacity effect; unmeasured demand changes can confound the price–quantity pattern. Our proposed evidence must match borrower totals across lenders and justify stable demand and financing costs. These static results do not establish macroeconomic propagation, default risk or a policy welfare ranking.” Each sentence has a reason in the solved cases. A future dynamic or heterogeneous-borrower model should be motivated by one of these unresolved objects, with new state variables and evidence requirements stated before adding algebra.

Complete solutions

1. Model memo

The question is the equilibrium response to exogenous D holding a,b,c,γ fixed. Q,q,D are loan units; R,a,c are numeraire per loan; b,γ are price per quantity; B,C are numeraire. Domains are a,c,D≥0 and b,γ>0; choices Q,q≥0 and R≥0. The borrower maximizes aQ−bQ²/2−RQ, the sector maximizes Rq−cq−γ(q−D)+²/2, and Q=q clears. Known primitives precede simultaneous price-taking choices. Missing default and income feedback preclude claims about risky loan pricing or aggregate propagation. D is modeled capacity; a recorded deposit balance may respond to lending opportunities and need not equal accessible internal funding. A complete answer must connect units, choices and clearing, not merely list symbols.

2. Boundary and equilibrium

The rows are (4,6,4),(8,2,0),(8,2,0). For θ>0, the continuous strictly decreasing derivative θ−bQ−γ(Q−D)+ has exactly one zero: θ/b when that value is at most D, otherwise (θ+γD)/(b+γ). Setting price to marginal benefit gives c in the first branch and c+γ(Q−D) in the second, satisfying both agents' global choice conditions. Any positive equilibrium must solve this same derivative equation, proving uniqueness. Starting at D=2, the slope is 1/2 until D=8, so Q rises by three to eight; it is then zero until D=10. Multiplying 1/2 by the full change eight would incorrectly predict Q=9. At the threshold the one-sided derivatives differ.

3. Sign restrictions

Differentiate Q=(a−c+γD)/(b+γ) and R=a−bQ. This gives QD=γ/(b+γ), RD=−bγ/(b+γ), Qa=1/(b+γ), Ra=γ/(b+γ). At the baseline all magnitudes are 1/2. D=1 gives (Q,R)=(9/2,11/2); a=9 gives (9/2,9/2). Both remain binding. A joint decline rejects a pure binding D reduction with other primitives fixed and correctly measured prices/quantities. It does not reject every funding explanation or prove demand caused the observation; simultaneous primitive changes or changing loan composition can invalidate the maintained bridge.

4. Friction sensitivity

The quotient rule gives Qγ=[D(b+γ)−(θ+γD)]/(b+γ)²=(bD−θ)/(b+γ)²<0 under the stated binding condition. As γ tends to zero from above, Q tends to θ/b=q̄ and R tends to c. This is a limit of equilibria, not permission to divide by γ=0 in the original supply formula. In the slack branch Q=q̄ regardless of positive γ, so the derivative is zero. With baseline a,b,c,D and γ=3, Q=7/2 and R=13/2; higher friction reduces credit relative to Q=5 at γ=1.

5. Replacement calculation

Clearing (a−R)/b=D+(R−c)/γ+κ(R−h) yields HR=a−bD+bc/γ+bκh. Hence RD=−b/H, xD=1+RD/γ=(1+bκ)/H, zD=κRD=−bκ/H and QD=−RD/b=1/H. H=3 gives (−1/3,2/3,−1/3,1/3). From D=2 to zero the before tuple (R,x,z,Q)=(4,4,2,6) becomes (14/3,8/3,8/3,16/3). Thus Δx=−4/3, Δz=2/3 and ΔQ=−2/3; adding the first two gives the third. Both prices exceed two, so the assumed active branches hold. Total credit falls less than incumbent lending because borrowers substitute toward the alternative.

6. Reject an infeasible branch

The active formula gives R=(10−2+2+6)/3=16/3, x=16/3, z=−2/3, Q=14/3. Although this algebra clears its unconstrained equation, z is negative and R<h, violating the active-supply domain. The correct tuple is R=5,x=5,z=0,Q=5. At this price alternative profit is (5−6)z−z²/2, strictly decreasing for z≥0, so z=0 is its unique optimum. Incumbent and borrower choices are the baseline optima at five. This verifies individual optimality and clearing, not just the nonnegativity of the proposed total.

7. Conduct and surplus

For a>c, the strictly decreasing derivative θ−2bq−γ(q−D)+ starts positive and crosses zero before a/b. Its root is θ/(2b) when D is at least that value, otherwise (θ+γD)/(2b+γ); strict concavity makes it the global maximum. At the competitive positive optimum π′(Q)=S′(Q)−bQ=−bQ<0, so the monopoly root is smaller, and inverse demand implies a higher price. Baseline q=10/3,R=20/3,e=4/3 gives profit 44/3. Borrower surplus bq²/2=50/9; their sum is 182/9. Competitive surplus 23 minus 182/9 is 25/9. This comparison holds the same benefit/cost schedules fixed and excludes the cost of acquiring D.

8. Same levels, different structures

The rival's slack threshold is (6−1)/(2/5)=25/2, exceeding D=2, so q=(6−1+2)/(2/5+1)=5. R=6−5/5=5 and e=3. Marginal revenue and cost both equal four, verifying the optimum. Its binding D slope is 1/(2/5+1)=5/7; the original competitive slope is 1/(1+1)=1/2. Observing (5,5) cannot reveal conduct when demand and costs can differ this way. Independently justified demand slopes, cost schedules and a capacity shift that leaves them stable could make a comparison informative. A before–after difference without those restrictions is not that experiment. The illustrative rival is a counterexample to identification from one point, not an estimate.

9. Allocation versus price uniqueness

At a=1<c=2, Q=q=0 with any R in [1,2]. Below one demand is positive but supply is zero; above two supply is positive while demand is zero; inside the interval both may choose zero, including the endpoint supply correspondence at two. No positive trade can occur because marginal willingness to pay 1−Q is below marginal cost 2+(Q−2)+ for every Q>0. At a=c=2 only R=2 clears with zero quantity; the same strict inequality holds for positive Q. Quantity uniqueness survives both examples, but price uniqueness fails in the strict no-trade interval. Selecting one endpoint is a normalization, not a proved unique price.

10. Proposed-study memo

One complete answer is: “I seek the effect of an incumbent capacity contraction on the same borrowers' total homogeneous credit over a specified one-period window. I need pre-shock capacity, incumbent and alternative loan amounts, common borrower identifiers and comparable prices; exits and coverage changes must be reconciled. With both suppliers active and fixed demand/cost schedules, the model predicts incumbent credit down, alternative credit up and total credit down by less than the incumbent decline. Falling borrower demand is a competing mechanism; it can reduce total credit and price. A measured capacity loss with stable maintained primitives but rising total credit would contradict the predicted local total-credit sign. I stop this interpretation if lender coverage cannot reconcile borrower totals or the active-branch assumptions cannot be supported. The result would concern this credit allocation; it would not establish investment effects or welfare gains.” Grade the answer on defined target/units, the solved substitution mechanism, feasible observations, a genuine rival, a conditional contradictory pattern, a practical stop rule and an explicit claim ceiling. Other memos are acceptable if all seven elements are coherent; claiming that a confirming sign proves the assumptions fails the argument.

Teach-back and delayed retention

Teach-back. In three minutes explain why a first-order condition is not an equilibrium, why internal funding can have a zero marginal effect, and why a fall in one lender's credit need not equal the fall in borrower credit. Answer guide: specify global feasible choices and clearing; identify the slack funding branch; add alternative lending and reconcile x+z=Q. Do not replace these mechanisms with a list of derivatives.

After one week, without notes: derive the baseline piecewise solution, classify h=6 at the baseline, and produce a reason that observing Q=5,R=5 does not identify conduct. Answer guide: use the strictly decreasing surplus derivative and verify choices; h=6 is inactive because baseline price is five; the rival monopoly (6,1/5,1,1,2) fits the same levels. Re-read the branch proof if you get a negative supply, and the model-comparison case if you treat calibration as identification. The next research task is to connect these model objects to data construction, ethical access and a credible analysis plan.
