Capability and prerequisites

After this chapter, you can reconcile employment, unemployment and participation; derive unemployment dynamics from feasible transitions; connect recruiting and wage bargaining to job finding; explain why a firm might pay a wage above a worker's immediate outside option; and distinguish slow recovery from history-dependent long-run unemployment. You can also say what additional evidence a policy claim requires.

Read Consumption, saving and investment. We use difference equations, discounted Bellman reasoning, conditional probability and incentive constraints. Every numerical economy below is synthetic. A period has a fixed length throughout each model. Transition probabilities, wages and discount factors must refer to that same length. The matching and effort models are separate environments, with their own wage equations.

1. A lower unemployment rate can accompany fewer jobs

Let P be the eligible population, E employed people, U unemployed people and N people outside the labor force. These are mutually exclusive states at a reference date. Let L = E + U be the labor force. Then

P = E + U + N; u = U/L; participation ℓ = L/P;
employment–population ratio eP = E/P = (1 − u)ℓ.

The denominators matter. With P = 1,000, E = 600 and U = 100, unemployment is 1/7 and participation is 7/10. If 20 unemployed people stop satisfying the search requirement while nobody gains or loses employment, U becomes 80 and L becomes 680. Unemployment falls to 2/17, while employment–population remains 3/5. The change is real in the classification system, but it does not establish stronger hiring.

For the United States CPS, the base population is civilian, noninstitutional and age 16 or older. Employment includes qualifying work for pay or profit and certain temporary absences; unpaid family work has a separate hours threshold. A jobless person generally counts as unemployed when available and actively searching within the last four weeks; temporary layoff with expected recall is an alternative. Wanting work alone does not establish unemployment, and receiving unemployment benefits does not determine the classification. These are scoped survey definitions, not judgments about a person's effort or economic value. Consult the official definitions before classifying an unusual case.

A full-time student, retiree or caregiver may be in E, U or N depending on the actual criteria. People holding several jobs still count once in a person-based employment stock. Hours, desired hours, pay, job security and participation provide additional information that a single unemployment rate omits. A fall in participation may reflect schooling, retirement, care demands, discouragement or other changes; the identity alone cannot distinguish them.

Stocks change through gross flows

Write Fij,t for people in state i at the beginning of the interval and state j at its end. Each row must sum to the opening stock and each column to the closing stock, assuming a closed, unchanged eligible population. A person who changes status repeatedly inside the interval appears once in this endpoint table; such a table does not count every intervening event. With pij = Fij/i, each nonempty row contains nonnegative conditional probabilities summing to one.

Uₜ₊₁ − Uₜ = FEU,t + FNU,t − FUE,t − FUN,t.

Migration, aging into eligibility and death require extra population flows in actual data. Survey weights, sampling error and inconsistent responses also matter when estimating transitions. The exact accounting below concerns the stated synthetic population.

2. Derive persistence before explaining its cause

Temporarily fix L and exclude N. Let s be the probability an initially employed worker is unemployed next period, and f the probability an initially unemployed worker is employed next period. Everyone has the same probabilities, constant over time. The elementary model allows one transition per period; new separations do not join that period's matching pool. Thus s and f lie in [0,1] and

Uₜ₊₁ = (1 − f)Uₜ + s(L − Uₜ),
uₜ₊₁ = s + (1 − s − f)uₜ.

This map sends [0,1] into [0,1]: it interpolates between endpoint values s and 1 − f. Setting the change to zero gives u* = s/(s + f) when s + f > 0. Subtraction gives an exact solution, not an approximation:

uₜ − u* = ρᵗ(u₀ − u*), where ρ = 1 − s − f.

If 0 < s + f < 2, then |ρ| < 1 and every initial state converges to u*. When ρ is nonnegative convergence is monotone; a negative ρ creates alternating deviations. If s = f = 1, the economy switches u to 1 − u every period and generally never converges. If s = f = 0, every initial state is stationary. If f = 0 < s, the unique steady state is all unemployment; if s = 0 < f, it is zero unemployment. Zero unemployment in the latter case does not imply frictionless hiring: there are simply no new separations in the stationary economy.

For s,f > 0, ∂u*/∂s = f/(s + f)² > 0 and ∂u*/∂f = −s/(s + f)² < 0. These are partial comparisons holding the other probability fixed. A policy that changes both needs their joint response. The same u* can result from rapid turnover or very slow transitions.

Incidence, duration and the observation window

For a newly unemployed worker, let D count periods until the first exit into E. Under the constant, independent conditional hazard f > 0, Pr(D > d) = (1 − f)d for integer d ≥ 0. Summing the tail probabilities gives E[D] = Σd≥0(1 − f)d = 1/f. This is a completed-spell expectation under the model. At f = 0 it is infinite. The inflow into unemployment per labor-force member is s(1 − u), not s: s is conditional on being employed.

If an unemployed person can also leave the labor force with probability g, the probabilities must satisfy f + g ≤ 1. When f + g > 0, mean time until either exit is 1/(f + g). The probability that the eventual exit is a job is f/(f + g), obtained by summing Σ(1 − f − g)df. If f = g = 0, nobody exits: duration is infinite and the destination ratio is undefined. Counting every exit as job finding would overstate f. With varying duration-specific hazards, survival is a product of the successive survival probabilities, and the simple geometric formula no longer applies. In the CPS, reported unemployment duration describes spells still in progress; it is not the completed time to a new job. See the duration section of the official definitions.

3. Recruiting, congestion and the value of a match

Why does f change? A matching function represents the number of new worker–vacancy pairs formed from opening unemployment U and vacancies V. Use the original discrete-period specification

M = μ√(UV), θ = V/U,
f = M/U = μ√θ, h = M/V = μ/√θ.

Here μ > 0 summarizes the matching technology, θ is labor-market tightness, f is a worker's job-finding probability, and h is a vacancy's filling probability. Increasing vacancies per searching worker helps workers find jobs and makes each vacancy harder to fill. Doubling both pools doubles matches; this constant-returns assumption is maintained, not inferred from the formula's convenience.

In discrete time M cannot exceed either U or V. On positive pools the specification is therefore valid only when μ ≤ 1 and μ² ≤ θ ≤ 1/μ². At U = 0 or V = 0 set M = 0 and avoid undefined ratios. Outside this domain one must change the matching technology, for example by explicitly introducing a cap and rederiving its branches. Reporting a probability of two is not an acceptable approximation. Continuous-time arrival intensities are different objects and need not obey the same upper bound.

At a stationary unemployment stock, s(1 − u) = μ√(uv), where v = V/L. Squaring gives the Beveridge locus

v = (s²/μ²)(1 − u)²/u, for u > 0.

For positive s,μ and 0 < u < 1 its slope is (s²/μ²)(1 − 1/u²) < 0. This locus lists stationary combinations under fixed parameters and feasible matching probabilities. An observed economy can be off it during adjustment. A change in separations, participation, composition or measurement can move the observed unemployment–vacancy relation; a shift alone does not identify a deterioration in matching technology.

A complete stationary wage and vacancy system

Consider risk-neutral workers and firms discounting next-period real payoffs by β ∈ (0,1). Each filled job produces z and pays real wage w each period, then separates with probability s ∈ (0,1). An unemployed worker receives real flow payoff b, including any modeled nonwork value, and starts a job next period with probability f. Assume z > b ≥ 0. A vacancy costs κ > 0 now, is filled for next period with probability h, and otherwise expires. Free entry permits new vacancies each period. All values refer to the beginning of a period and all parameters are stationary. No worker searches while employed.

Let J be the value of a filled job to its firm, VE and VU the worker's values, and S = VE − VU the worker's gain from employment. Define A = 1 − β(1 − s) > 0. The dated value equations are

J = z − w + β(1 − s)J = (z − w)/A,
VE = w + β[(1 − s)VE + sVU],
VU = b + β[fVE + (1 − f)VU],
S = (w − b)/(A + βf).

For a particular pair bargaining over wᵢ, the market outside value VU stays fixed: one pair cannot change the entire labor market. Its worker surplus is [wᵢ − (1 − β)VU]/A, and its employer surplus is (z − wᵢ)/A. With worker bargaining weight η ∈ (0,1), maximize their product SᵢηJᵢ1−η over nonnegative surpluses. A positive joint surplus makes the optimum interior. The log objective has negative second derivative and its derivative gives η/Sᵢ = (1 − η)/Jᵢ. Hence S = ηJ/(1 − η) in the symmetric stationary allocation. The pair's outside option was held fixed while differentiating; substituting an economy-wide VU(w) before taking that derivative would solve a different bargaining problem.

A vacancy's expected value is −κ + βhJ. With positive entry this is zero. Combining bargaining, values and free entry yields

J = (1 − η)(z − b)/(A + ηβf),
κ = βhJ,
w = (1 − η)b + η(z + κθ).

The last term connects wages to recruiting conditions: a worker's outside opportunities depend on the same market that firms face. It is not legitimate to use a one-period surplus formula while ignoring the continuation value of a job lasting many periods.

Set x = √θ. The remaining equilibrium condition is a scalar quadratic:

κηβμx² + κAx − βμ(1 − η)(z − b) = 0.

The left side starts negative at zero and is strictly increasing for x ≥ 0, becoming positive eventually. There is one positive algebraic root. A feasible positive-entry equilibrium in this maintained specification additionally requires μ ≤ x ≤ 1/μ. Once checked, set θ = x², f = μx, h = μ/x, u = s/(s + f), v = θu, J = κ/(βh), and w = z − AJ. A root outside the probability domain does not establish an equilibrium of this restricted technology. With η at an endpoint or z ≤ b the positive-surplus interior derivation must be reconsidered.

The MIT search and matching lectures supply the primary route into matching, flow balance, vacancy values and bargaining. They use continuous-time arrival rates for the equilibrium search model. Our dated probabilities, expiring vacancies, discounting and numerical cases above form a separately derived discrete-time teaching model.

Worked example 2 — Wage bargaining and recruiting jointly determine job finding

Take β = 4/5, s = 1/4, μ = 1/2, η = 1/2, z = 12, b = 4 and κ = 8/3. Then A = 2/5. Substitution into the entry equation gives (8/15)x² + (16/15)x − 8/5 = 0, or x² + 2x − 3 = 0. The positive root is x = 1, inside [1/2,2]. Thus θ = 1 and f = h = 1/2.

The job value is J = (8/3)/[(4/5)(1/2)] = 20/3. The wage is 12 − (2/5)(20/3) = 28/3; bargaining also gives S = 20/3. From the unemployment value equation, (1 − β)VU = b + βfS = 20/3, so VU = 100/3 and VE = 40. Substitution verifies both worker Bellman equations. The vacancy cost 8/3 exactly equals discounted expected job value (4/5)(1/2)(20/3).

Stationary u = (1/4)/(3/4) = 1/3 and v = 1/3. Separations and hires each equal 1/6 of the labor force per period. These deliberately large synthetic probabilities simplify arithmetic; they are not estimates for a country or a month.

Now halve μ to 1/4, holding the other primitives fixed. The new quadratic is x² + 4x − 3 = 0, so x = √7 − 2 ≈ 0.645751, within [1/4,4]. Job finding falls to (√7 − 2)/4 ≈ 0.161438 and the vacancy-filling probability falls to approximately 0.387146. Tightness is 11 − 4√7 ≈ 0.416995. Unemployment becomes (√7 + 1)/6 ≈ 0.607625, vacancies per labor-force member approximately 0.253377, and the wage approximately 8.555993. Both matching sides find partners less often here. The conclusion follows from this change in μ with all stated primitives fixed; an empirical fall in vacancies and rise in unemployment does not by itself identify μ.

4. Efficiency wages: paying for a future loss that matters

A worker may accept a job yet have weak incentives to perform its required effort. This section uses a separate model: there is no Nash bargaining or vacancy-cost equation. Workers are risk neutral with discount β ∈ (0,1). An employed worker receives w now, chooses effort at cost e > 0 or shirks at zero effort cost, and may lose the job at the end of the period. Exogenous separation has probability s < 1. Conditional on no exogenous separation, shirking is detected and punished by dismissal with probability q ∈ (0,1]. Thus shirking increases job-loss probability by (1 − s)q, not q + s without correcting overlap.

Individual effort is not directly contractible or perfectly inferable from aggregate production. The firm cannot demand a bond or impose a monetary fine. Monitoring and dismissal are credible, wages are stationary, and an unemployed worker receives b and finds a job for the next period with probability f ∈ [0,1]. These assumptions make future job loss the available incentive. Different contracts or information change the result.

Under a candidate policy of always working, define values VE, VU and D = VE − VU. Then

VE = w − e + β[(1 − s)VE + sVU],
VU = b + β[fVE + (1 − f)VU],
D = (w − e − b)/[1 − β(1 − s − f)].

Shirking once saves e now and loses expected discounted value β(1 − s)qD. Therefore working is incentive compatible exactly when

e ≤ β(1 − s)qD,
w ≥ wNS = b + e + e[1 − β(1 − s − f)]/[β(1 − s)q].

The denominator defining D is positive because 1 − s − f ≤ 1 and β < 1. At the minimum incentive wage D is strictly positive, so accepting employment also beats remaining unemployed at the decision date. At equality assume the worker chooses effort when indifferent; a small wage premium gives a strict incentive with the outside environment fixed. A model relying on a tie should disclose it.

The calculation is a global policy certificate, not merely a comparison with one arbitrarily selected lifetime strategy. The candidate values satisfy the Bellman equalities for work and inequalities against shirking and voluntary immediate exit. Iterating those inequalities bounds the value of any sequence of deviations by the candidate value plus a discounted terminal remainder. Payoffs are bounded at fixed parameters and βT tends to zero, so that remainder vanishes. Equivalently, the finite-state discounted Bellman operator is a contraction. This establishes optimality against repeated as well as one-period deviations.

At fixed s,β,e,b,q, a higher f raises the required wage: finding a replacement job more easily reduces the penalty from dismissal. A higher q lowers the required rent, but monitoring costs and privacy or institutional constraints have not been modeled. If β = 0, q = 0 or s = 1, dismissal cannot induce costly effort through future job loss under these rules. One cannot obtain a finite incentive wage by dividing by zero. Calling a wage an efficiency wage describes an incentive mechanism; it does not establish that the resulting allocation is socially efficient.

The MIT efficiency-wage lectures develop the moral-hazard and employment-rent mechanism in continuous time. Our discrete timing explicitly separates exogenous separation from conditional detection and derives the corresponding incentive bound. Its numerical coefficients should not be substituted into a continuous-time formula.

Worked example 3 — An incentive wage and an unemployment equilibrium

Let β = 4/5, s = 1/4, f = 1/4, q = 1/2, e = 1 and b = 2. Then 1 − β(1 − s − f) = 3/5 and β(1 − s)q = 3/10, giving wNS = 5. The value difference is D = (5 − 1 − 2)/(3/5) = 10/3. The unemployment equation gives VU = 40/3 and therefore VE = 50/3. Shirking saves one now and costs (3/10)(10/3) = 1 in expected discounted value. Employment is valuable even though the worker is just indifferent between the two effort choices.

To connect incentives to aggregate employment, add a specific closure. Normalize the fixed labor force to one. Assume aggregate real production from workers exerting effort is F(E) = 7E − 2E² for 0 ≤ E ≤ 1, competitive marginal-product labor demand is w = F′(E) = 7 − 4E, and firms pay the least common wage inducing effort. This is a maintained aggregate labor-demand closure, with no additional vacancy-cost restriction. Stationary flows require f = s(1 − u)/u. Its probability bound requires u ≥ 1/5.

Substitution into the incentive bound gives wNS(u) = 11/3 + 2/(3u). Labor demand gives w = 3 + 4u. Equating them yields 12u² − 2u − 2 = 0, with roots 1/2 and −1/3. Only u = 1/2 is feasible. It gives E = 1/2, f = 1/4 and w = 5, exactly the individual values already calculated. The incentive schedule decreases strictly with u while the wage on labor demand increases with u, so this feasible intersection is unique.

All workers prefer having a job at this wage, yet the specified incentive and labor-demand conditions do not support employment of everyone. The result is conditional on the contracting and production assumptions. It does not show that every unemployment spell is caused by shirking incentives, or that creating unemployment is a desirable policy. Benefits, output, monitoring costs, distribution and alternative institutions all belong in a welfare comparison.

5. Institutions change mechanisms, not only a label on a curve

Search assistance can change the matching process; a bargaining rule can change η; unemployment support can change resources and an outside option; a dismissal rule can change separations and the cost of forming jobs. A wage floor may bind the wage decision, but the resulting employment effect depends on the rest of the market. The competitive labor-demand model, a bargaining model with free entry and a labor market with employer wage-setting power impose different constraints. The models in market power and contracts provide the relevant comparisons. Do not apply one model's sign to every institution.

A static fall in a firm's hiring cost need not equal the eventual aggregate increase in jobs. Entry, wages, search behavior, participation and congestion may adjust. Likewise, an intervention can improve measured job finding for its participants while displacing other applicants. Specify the population, outcome, horizon, budget and counterfactual before drawing a policy conclusion. Benefits also insure income risk and affect consumption; the earlier consumption chapter explains why a welfare assessment cannot be reduced to the unemployment rate.

6. Slow recovery and strict hysteresis are different claims

With fixed positive s and f and |ρ| < 1, a temporary disturbance to u eventually disappears. A ρ close to one can make this recovery extremely slow, but the long-run limit remains the same. In strict hysteresis, a temporary disturbance changes which long-run state is reached even after the original external disturbance has ended. The mechanism must retain history through a state, changed behavior or a changed feasible set; saying “unemployment was high for years” does not supply that mechanism.

Blanchard and Summers' 1986 paper distinguishes a strict history-dependent equilibrium concept from broader long-lasting effects. It examines membership in wage setting and differences between short- and long-duration unemployment as possible mechanisms, and discusses the limits of those arguments. We use that conceptual distinction, without importing its historical observations as current evidence. The bounded feedback example below is our own assumed law, not a parameter estimate or a reproduction of their model.

Worked example 4 — A temporary shock changes the limiting state

Keep s = 1/20 and a fixed labor force. Suppose job finding depends on the current unemployment state:

f(u) = 2/5 if u < 1/5; f(u) = 1/10 if u ≥ 1/5.
T(u) = 1/20 + (11/20)u below the threshold;
T(u) = 1/20 + (17/20)u at or above it.

This assumed rule could represent a reduced-form loss of contacts or effective employability associated with a weak labor market. It does not derive those individual mechanisms. Both probabilities are feasible and both transition branches preserve [0,1]. Below 1/5, T(u) < 4/25 < 1/5, so that region is invariant. At or above 1/5, T(u) ≥ 11/50 > 1/5, so the upper region is invariant too. The lower branch has fixed point uL = 1/9 and slope 11/20. The upper has fixed point uH = 1/3 and slope 17/20. Both are attracting within their regions.

Start at 1/9. Without a disturbance it stays there. Let a one-time external job-loss event move unemployment to 1/4. After that event ends, restore exactly the same T. Numbering the post-shock state as t = 0 gives uₜ = 1/3 + (17/20)t(1/4 − 1/3), which stays in the upper region and converges to 1/3. The outside shock has ended, but its effect on the limiting state remains.

A temporary intervention that actually moved u strictly below 1/5 would switch the basin in this example. Whether any feasible intervention can accomplish that, at what cost and with what consequences, is outside the assumed law. A smoother feedback model may have different thresholds or equilibria. The example establishes logical possibility; it is not evidence that a particular country possesses this threshold.

  3. Matching domains and stationary vacancies. Take U = 80, V = 20 and μ = 1/2. Compute matches, tightness and both probabilities.   5. Monitoring and incentive compatibility. Use Worked example 3's individual environment, holding f = 1/4 fixed, but increase detection q to one. What fails if q = 0?   7. History-dependent limits. Let s = 1/25. Set f = 9/25 when u < 1/4 and f = 3/50 when u ≥ 1/4. Derive both maps, prove their regions invariant and find their fixed points.   8. Diagnosing a recovery claim. In a synthetic population, unemployment falls from 10% to 8% while employment–population falls from 60% to 59.8%. Derive both participation rates. A new aggregate equilibrium must solve the changed incentive schedule jointly with labor demand and flow balance; retaining the old f without checking that system is unjustified.

    When q = 0, shirking saves e without any extra chance of dismissal; no finite wage supplies this incentive under the assumed instruments. With job finding 1/5 and labor-force exit 1/10, the total exit probability is 3/10, mean duration until either exit is 10/3 and eventual employment-exit probability is (1/5)/(3/10) = 2/3. The competing exit removes some people before they find work. It is not the no-other-exit model with completed mean time 5 to a job, nor does it determine a future reentry-and-job history.

  6. New labor demand is w = 8 − 4E = 4 + 4u. The unchanged incentive schedule is 11/3 + 2/(3u). Equality gives 12u² + u − 2 = 0, so the positive root is u = (√97 − 1)/24 ≈ 0.368702. The other root is negative. Since 1/5 < u < 1/2, the implied f = (1 − u)/(4u) is between 1/4 and one and therefore feasible. Rationalizing gives f = (√97 − 3)/16 ≈ 0.428054. The wage is 4 + 4u = (23 + √97)/6 ≈ 5.474810.

    The increasing labor-demand wage in u and decreasing incentive wage ensure only one positive intersection. Compared with the original u = 1/2, w = 5 and f = 1/4, the stronger production schedule raises the wage and job finding and lowers unemployment. The numerical production and incentive parameters are assumptions; an actual intervention may change monitoring, separations, participation, costs or the distribution of productivity. No observed policy variation or causal identification has been supplied. A valid answer names at least two such limitations rather than claiming this synthetic comparative static is an estimate.

  7. Below 1/4, T(u) = 1/25 + (3/5)u, with fixed point 1/10. It sends that region below 1/25 + 3/20 = 19/100 < 1/4. At or above 1/4, T(u) = 1/25 + (9/10)u, with fixed point 2/5. The minimum image there is 1/25 + 9/40 = 53/200 > 1/4; its maximum is 47/50 ≤ 1. Thus both regions are invariant, and their slopes have absolute value less than one.

    After the shock, uₜ = 2/5 − (1/10)(9/10)t, converging to 2/5. Resetting to exactly 1/4 selects the upper branch and still converges to 2/5; a reset strictly below is required for the low basin. With f = 3/50 everywhere, the same upper affine map applies globally and all initial states converge to 2/5. That model can be persistent but has no different long-run limits selected by the initial unemployment state.

  8. Use ℓ = eP/(1 − u). Initially ℓ = 0.60/0.90 = 2/3; finally ℓ = 0.598/0.92 = 0.65. The unemployment decline accompanies a participation decline and a slightly smaller employment share. There is no logical requirement of stronger hiring. One compatible explanation is fewer searches and more exits into N despite weak employment. Another is simultaneous hiring and separations with a shift toward schooling, retirement or care; the endpoint ratios leave all gross flows underdetermined. Improved matching can coexist with adverse changes elsewhere, but it is not established by these ratios.

    An illustrative evaluation randomly assigns a well-defined training offer among eligible applicants, follows all assigned people for a predeclared period, and compares employment, earnings and participation by assignment. Estimate the effect of the offer unless additional assumptions justify a participation effect. Record baseline status, take-up and losses to follow-up. Random assignment must remain valid; differential missing outcomes require bounds or a justified missingness strategy. Treatment may divert vacancies from untreated applicants, so a participant comparison need not measure aggregate job creation; variation across markets or saturation levels, with a stated interference model, would address that question more directly. Track program resource costs and consumption or earnings risk as well as employment. Grade for a clear estimand, comparison, timing, assumption, concrete threat and welfare measure; alternative designs can satisfy these criteria.

    Finally, a slowly decaying deviation under a unique fixed point and a history-dependent limiting state can look similar in a short sample. Evidence on mechanisms and responses after the original disturbance ends is needed. Long persistence alone does not distinguish them.

Teach-back and delayed check

Teach-back: explain to a colleague how unemployment can fall without any new jobs. Draw a three-state flow table, derive the two-state steady state, and then explain one reason job finding and one reason wages can change. Finally, contrast a slow return to one fixed point with two history-dependent limits. Name what each model holds fixed.

Teach-back checklist

A complete explanation identifies U/(E + U), shows that U→N can reduce it with E unchanged, and distinguishes gross from net flows. Flow balance s(1 − u) = fu gives the two-state steady state under fixed participation. Recruiting depends on both matching pools and entry incentives; an efficiency wage instead creates a value loss large enough to deter a deviation under restricted contracts. Those wage models have different assumptions and should not be stacked without rebuilding their joint equilibrium. Geometric persistence with |ρ| < 1 erases the initial disturbance; the threshold example preserves it through the selected invariant region. Observing an aggregate correlation does not identify which mechanism generated it.

Delayed check: without notes, take s = 2/25, f = 8/25 and u₀ = 3/10. Find u*, the next state and the completed mean duration. Explain the difference if exits to N occur too. State the efficiency-wage incentive inequality before solving for w, and identify which side of the threshold u = 1/5 belongs to in Worked example 4.

Delayed-check answer

Here u* = 1/5 and ρ = 3/5, so u₁ = 1/5 + (3/5)(1/10) = 13/50. Completed mean duration to a job is 25/8 periods in the no-other-exit homogeneous model. Additional N exits change both the endpoint accounting and the competing-exit duration; the total exit hazard cannot be labeled job finding. The effort inequality is e ≤ β(1 − s)q(VE − VU). The threshold itself belongs to the upper region and maps to 11/50, then converges to 1/3.
