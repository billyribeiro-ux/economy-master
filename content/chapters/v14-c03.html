Volume 14 · Chapter 3

Incomplete markets and heterogeneous agents

Two households can face the same wage and interest rate but make different saving decisions because one has little wealth and an uncertain paycheck. A population can have constant aggregate capital while its members continually move between wealth states. A representative household suppresses precisely the distribution needed to study these questions.

Capability. Derive constrained saving under uninsured income risk, propagate a wealth-income distribution, construct stationary exchange and production equilibria, and audit a numerical approximation without confusing a solved grid economy with a continuous one. Five worked cases and twelve original solved exercises develop the proofs and their limits.

Prerequisites. Dynamic optimization and recursive methods supplies finite Bellman contraction, policy evaluation and Euler perturbations. Stochastic growth and real business cycles distinguishes an individual’s assets from aggregate capital and establishes ownership-consistent equilibrium accounting. Probability, conditional expectation, concavity and finite linear systems come from Volumes 2–3. We prove the distribution tools needed here rather than assuming that every Markov chain converges.

All numerical economies and targets below are original teaching constructions. No historical calibration is replicated. Exact results for restricted finite asset sets are explicitly distinguished from the continuously divisible asset models that motivate them.

1. Income risk, debt limits and the household problem

A household enters date t with assets at, observes its labor efficiency et in a finite positive set E, and chooses consumption and next assets before et+1 is revealed. The transition matrix Π has nonnegative rows summing to one; Πee′ is the probability of tomorrow’s efficiency conditional on today’s. At constant wage w>0 and gross asset return R>0, the saving convention is

ct+at+1=R at+w et,   ct>0,   at+1≥a̲,   E∑t≥0βtu(ct),   0<β<1.

Assets are goods invested at the preceding date; R includes principal. A negative a is debt. Efficiency is supplied inelastically, so earnings are we, not a choice of hours in this chapter. The only saving instrument has the same return in every individual income state; no contract directly insures the next income realization. Assume u′>0 and u″<0 where differentiating. Log utility is used in the numerical cases. The state is (a,e), not just a when income is persistent.

A fixed borrowing limit a̲ is a primitive. It must permit at least one feasible action in every state being solved. A natural debt limit instead reflects repayment from the worst admissible future income path. For R>1 and a possible permanently low income ymin=wemin, its safe bound is a≥−ymin/(R−1). To see the units, multiply the worst-path budget by R−t−1, sum through T and impose liminf aT+1/RT+1≥0. Nonnegative consumption requires a0+∑t≥0ymin/Rt+1≥0. At equality, maintaining that debt on the worst path permits zero consumption, so strictly positive consumption requires additional slack. This calculation is a robust worst-path bound; if the low state cannot persist, the appropriate bound depends on admissible income histories. For R≤1 the displayed finite perpetuity formula is unavailable. One cannot divide by R−1 and call the resulting positive number a debt limit.

For a well-defined continuous problem, the formal Bellman equation is

V(a,e)=supa′≥a̲, a′<Ra+we{u(Ra+we−a′)+β∑e′Πee′V(a′,e′)}.

An unbounded asset domain with log or CRRA utility needs appropriate existence, integrability and terminal arguments. We do not infer them from a bounded finite-state theorem. The exact infinite-horizon verification below is for a finite asset set with feasible positive consumption, where rewards really are bounded. Continuous Euler conditions are derived separately and used only on their stated domain.

The borrowing-limit Euler condition. Increase at+1 by a small d, lower current consumption by d, and on each successor increase next consumption by Rd while keeping at+2 fixed. At a finite-value optimum with positive consumption and feasible two-sided perturbations,

u′(ct)=βR Etu′(ct+1).

At a′=a̲ only d≥0 is allowed, giving u′(ct)≥βR Etu′(ct+1). Write λt=u′(ct)−βR Etu′(ct+1)≥0 and λt(at+1−a̲)=0. These are the lower-bound complementary-slackness conditions. An artificial upper asset bound can instead produce the opposite inequality. On a discrete asset grid there is no arbitrarily small d, so action-value comparisons, not continuous Euler equalities, certify grid optimality.

One useful stationary implication is conditional and precise. Suppose a continuous-choice stationary equilibrium has no upper constraint, E u′(c) is finite and positive, and λ is integrable. Integrating the Euler inequality under the invariant distribution gives (1−βR)E u′(c)=Eλ≥0. Thus βR≤1; if λ>0 on positive population mass, then βR<1. The derivation uses stationarity of the whole consumption process. It does not establish existence, prove that the lower bound must bind, or apply automatically when the marginal-utility expectation is infinite or actions are discrete.

2. What precautionary saving actually proves

Consider two dates with cash x today, saving s in a common interval, and future consumption Rs+Y. The derivative of u(x−s)+βE u(Rs+Y) with respect to s is F(s)=−u′(x−s)+βR E u′(Rs+Y). It decreases strictly because F′=u″(x−s)+βR²E u″(Rs+Y)<0. If u′ is convex, a mean-preserving spread in Y weakly raises E u′(Rs+Y) for every feasible s. It therefore weakly raises the unique optimal saving choice: the zero of a decreasing derivative moves right, with endpoint choices handled by its one-sided signs. Assume the same feasible interval and positive consumption for both income laws; otherwise changing solvency restrictions can itself change the answer. Strict convexity, nondegenerate risk and an interior comparison yield a strict increase.

For three-times differentiable utility, u‴≥0 makes marginal utility convex. Risk aversion u″<0 alone does not establish this comparison. Nor does this two-date fixed-price result prove a universal increase in stationary aggregate wealth: prices, borrowing limits, earnings persistence, policy curvature and the endogenous distribution can all change. The mechanism is higher expected marginal value of resources in bad future states, not a desire to maximize asset holdings.

Worked case 1. Risk raises saving, but a poor household still hits its constraint

Use two dates, log utility, β=R=1, cash 4, saving s≥0, and future income Y∈{1,3} with equal probabilities. The no-risk comparison replaces Y by its mean 2. With certain income, equality of marginal utilities gives scertain=1.

Under risk, −1/(4−s)+(1/2)/(s+1)+(1/2)/(s+3)=0 is equivalent to 2s²+2s−5=0. The unique feasible root is s*=(√11−1)/2=1.1583123952. Current and the two future consumptions are (2.8416876048,2.1583123952,4.1583123952). Strict concavity plus derivative signs at zero and as s approaches 4 prove global optimality. The negative quadratic root is outside the feasible domain.

At general cash x>0, the derivative at s=0 is −1/x+2/3. Thus the lower bound binds exactly for x≤3/2; at equality the optimum is still s=0, with a zero multiplier. At x=1 the borrowing multiplier is 1/3. The household would like to borrow but cannot. Risk can increase desired saving while leaving the actual saving of a constrained household unchanged at zero.

3. A finite household problem that can be certified completely

Fix a finite ordered asset set A and finite E. In the saving convention, feasible next assets are Γ(a,e)={b∈A:Ra+we−b>0}; require Γ nonempty. There are finitely many feasible consumptions and all are positive, so log rewards have a finite maximum absolute value M. This finiteness, not a general bounded-log claim, justifies the following theorem.

For a vector v indexed by S=A×E, define Tv(a,e)=maxb∈Γ(a,e){u(Ra+we−b)+β∑Πee′v(b,e′)}. For two vectors, comparing the same feasible action in both maxima gives ‖Tv−Tz‖∞≤β‖v−z‖∞. Iteration is Cauchy because successive differences decay geometrically; its limit V satisfies TV=V by continuity. If V and W are fixed points, their distance is at most β times itself, hence zero. A maximizing action exists because Γ is finite.

Choose any maximizing policy g. Along its path the Bellman equality telescopes to E∑t=0Tβtu(ct)+βT+1EV(sT+1)=V(s0). Bounded V makes the terminal term vanish. Every other feasible history-dependent or randomized plan satisfies the corresponding Bellman inequality, so its expected payoff is no larger. This proves global optimality for the finite-action economy. Randomization cannot improve on a maximum, although it may mix tied maximizing actions.

Certificates. If an approximate vector v has Bellman residual ε=‖Tv−v‖∞, then ‖V−v‖∞≤ε/(1−β): add and subtract Tv and rearrange. For a proposed policy g, solve (I−βQg)vg=rg, or bound its fixed-policy evaluation error. The inverse exists as ∑j≥0βjQgj. Check every unused feasible action against vg; nonpositive improvement proves optimality. A small residual in a different reward, transition or feasible-action model certifies that different model only.

4. From individual decisions to population distributions

Let μ(a,e) be population mass at a state, nonnegative and summing to one. The continuum economy here assumes that its cross-sectional transition follows the specified deterministic law; this is a modeling convention about aggregation of idiosyncratic risk, not an application of an elementary finite-sample law of large numbers to an unexamined continuum construction. With deterministic policy g,

Q(a,e),(b,e′)=1{b=g(a,e)}Πee′,   μ′=μQ.

Every row of Q sums to one. Therefore μ′ has nonnegative entries and preserves mass. The income marginal evolves by Π, but the wealth-income joint law generally contains dependence that the separate marginals miss. With an actual randomized optimal policy, replace the indicator by its conditional action probabilities; this changes individual risk and must be specified, not silently confused with numerical interpolation.

A stationary distribution π satisfies πQ=π. Every finite stochastic Q has at least one. Starting from any probability vector μ, the Cesàro averages νN=N−1∑t=0N−1μQt lie in a compact simplex, so a subsequence converges. Since νNQ−νN=(μQN−μ)/N has norm at most 2/N, its limit is invariant. This argument does not imply uniqueness or convergence of μQt.

A sufficient mixing condition with a proof. Suppose for some integer m≥1, probability vector ν and ε∈(0,1], every row satisfies Qm(s,·)≥εν(·). For ε<1 write Qm=ε1ν+(1−ε)P̃ with P̃ stochastic. A signed row vector d obeys ‖dP̃‖1≤∑j,i|di|P̃ij=‖d‖1. For probability vectors μ,η, their difference has zero sum, so

‖μQm−ηQm‖1≤(1−ε)‖μ−η‖1.

The simplex is complete, and geometric contraction gives a unique fixed point π of the m-step map. Then πQ is also fixed by that map because Q commutes with its powers, so uniqueness implies πQ=π. For t=jm+r, stochastic nonexpansion gives ‖μQt−π‖1≤2(1−ε)j. If ε=1 all rows of Qm equal ν, giving exact convergence after m steps and the same invariance argument. A positive matrix power supplies this condition by taking ν uniform and ε equal to the number of states times the smallest entry. Checking such a power is one explicit alternative to invoking an irreducibility-and-aperiodicity theorem without verifying its hypotheses.

For contrast, Q=[[0,1],[1,0]] has the unique invariant distribution (1/2,1/2) but oscillates from a point mass. Q=I has every probability vector invariant. Even if every entry of the exogenous income matrix Π is positive, a policy that preserves each asset level makes separate closed wealth classes, so Q can still have multiple invariant distributions.

A distribution certificate. Under the displayed m-step condition, any candidate μ satisfies ‖μ−π‖1≤‖μQm−μ‖1/ε≤m‖μQ−μ‖1/ε. The second inequality telescopes m one-step differences and uses stochastic nonexpansion. For a bounded statistic f, |∑f(μ−π)|≤(max f−min f)‖μ−π‖1/2, because positive and negative signed masses are equal. A tiny stationary residual without a mixing bound need not imply an accurate distribution.

5. Huggett exchange: bonds must sum to zero

In a pure-exchange economy, income e is an endowment of the perishable good. Use a payoff-unit bond b: one unit delivers one good today, and one unit of b′ costs q>0 today and delivers one good tomorrow. Its budget is c+qb′=b+e, with b′≥b̲. Gross return is 1/q. This differs from the saving-unit convention a′ costing one good; with constant q the relation is a=qb and R=1/q. Mixing the two conventions creates an erroneous extra return or bond price.

A stationary Huggett equilibrium consists of a price q, optimal household policies c,g at that price, and an invariant unit-mass distribution μ for their transition. The asset is in zero net supply: ∑bμ=0 and, by stationarity, ∑gμ=0. Aggregating budgets gives C+q∑gμ=∑bμ+E, hence C=E. Bond claims of creditors are liabilities of debtors; there is no physical capital and no firm whose capital demand absorbs positive net desired saving. Goods clearing is an implication only after accounting and asset clearing are correct.

Worked case 2. A complete four-state exchange equilibrium

Restrict payoff-unit bonds to (-1/2,1/2), endowment to (1,4), and let each next endowment be equally likely independently of the current state. Set 3/4 for β, q=1, and u=log c. Order the states as (−1/2,1),(−1/2,4),(1/2,1),(1/2,4). This is an exact restricted-asset economy; the upper bound and missing interior bond choices are part of its primitives.

Consider g=(-1/2,1/2,−1/2,1/2): choose the low asset after low income and the high asset after high income. Its consumption vector is (1,3,2,4). Let W− and W+ be the averages of the two value entries at each asset level before the next income is drawn. Policy evaluation gives W−=(log1+log3)/2+(β/2)(W−+W+) and W+=(log2+log4)/2+(β/2)(W−+W+). Therefore D=W+−W−=(1/2)log(8/3), and the four policy values are (2.1996348878,3.6660581464,2.8927820684,3.9537402188).

At the first state, high saving consumes zero and is infeasible. At the second and fourth states, switching from low to high saving costs current utility log(4/3) and log(5/4), respectively, and gains βD. Both gains exceed the costs. At the third, that cost is log2>βD, so low saving is optimal. The three chosen-action advantages over their feasible alternatives are (0.0801288974,0.3253362107,0.1446674186), in states two, three and four. Their signs can also be checked without decimal rounding: (8/3)³ exceeds both (4/3)⁸ and (5/4)⁸, while it is below 2⁸. Taking logs and dividing by eight gives the three comparisons. Policy evaluation plus all-action comparisons proves unique optimal actions on every state; the Bellman theorem extends this to all feasible histories.

Q = [[1/2,1/2,0,0], [0,0,1/2,1/2], [1/2,1/2,0,0], [0,0,1/2,1/2]].

Direct multiplication gives Q² with every row (1/4,1/4,1/4,1/4). Thus μ=(1/4,1/4,1/4,1/4) is unique and every initial distribution reaches it after two steps. Aggregate inherited and next bonds are both 0. Aggregate consumption and endowment are (5/2,5/2). Households borrow and lend while the economy’s net bond position remains zero. Aggregate constancy does not mean each person’s consumption or wealth is constant.

We have constructed one equilibrium at q=1, not proved uniqueness of its price or existence in the continuous-asset Huggett model. Since actions are discrete, a continuous Euler residual can be nonzero even when the complete grid Bellman certificate is exact.

The distinction between a payoff-unit claim, a borrowing limit, optimal household choices, zero aggregate credit and an invariant distribution follows the model structure in Huggett, The Risk-Free Rate in Heterogeneous-Agent, Incomplete-Insurance Economies—Revised, working paper 91-0155, printed pages 2–4. The author’s research page warns that the 1993 published article mistakenly contains tables and figures for another model and directs readers to the working-paper version. We use the inspected working-paper model description, not the affected historical numerical tables.

6. Aiyagari production: household wealth supplies physical capital

Now let aggregate production be F(K,L)=ZKαL1−α, with 0<α<1, Z>0 and depreciation δ∈(0,1]. Labor efficiency is inelastic and has stationary marginal ν, so L=∑eν(e). A competitive firm rents capital and efficiency units at r=FK and w=FL; concavity supports its optimum and constant returns imply zero profits. Household wealth is ownership of physical capital and earns R=1−δ+r. There is no separate positive-supply bond in this baseline.

A stationary production equilibrium requires (i) household optimality at w,R; (ii) μ=μQ for those policies and unit mass; (iii) K=∑aμ=∑gμ; (iv) the firm’s factor prices and labor clearing L=∑eμ. Integrating household budgets gives C+K=RK+wL. Since rK+wL=F(K,L), this is C+δK=F(K,L), the goods-market condition. Ownership matters: one must not add dividends after distributing the entire constant-returns product to labor and capital.

The finite-grid algorithm at a trial K>0 computes w(K),R(K), solves the household problem, constructs a stationary μ if a selection is well defined, and returns Ks(K)=∑aμ. An equilibrium requires Ks(K)−K=0. A household optimum and an invariant distribution at an arbitrary trial price do not establish this fixed point. On a deterministic grid, policy switches or changes in closed classes can make the selected capital supply discontinuous or multivalued. Do not apply an intermediate-value argument without its continuity premises.

Worked case 3. A complete finite production equilibrium

Use saving-unit assets (0,1), efficiency (1/2,3/2), iid equally likely shocks, β=4/5, and log utility. Choose production parameters α=1/10, δ=4/9 and Z=(20/9)21/10. We construct the candidate K=1/2, L=1. Production, the rental price, wage and gross return are (20/9,4/9,2,1). In particular, the quoted household prices come from the stated technology.

At w=2,R=1, the policy g=(0,1,0,1) has consumption (1,2,2,3). Its continuation-value difference is D=(1/2)log3. As before, the low-wealth/low-income state cannot save one while maintaining positive consumption. At low-wealth/high-income, high-wealth/low-income and high-wealth/high-income, the chosen-action advantages are respectively βD−log(3/2), log2−βD and βD−log(4/3), numerically (0.0339798074,0.2537022651,0.1517628430). They are all positive: equivalently 3² exceeds (3/2)⁵ and (4/3)⁵, while 3² is below 2⁵. These rational-power inequalities establish the signs independently of rounded logarithms. The four policy values are (2.2651841921,3.3977762881,2.9583313726,3.8032413962). This establishes household optimality over the entire finite feasible set.

The transition matrix is the same displayed Q as in case 2; hence μ is uniform. Its aggregates are (K,L,C,δK)=(1/2,1,2,2/9). Therefore asset supply equals the candidate capital used to price factors, labor clears, and C+δK=20/9 equals output. This is a fully solved stationary production equilibrium of the restricted finite economy. It is not a numerical solution of Aiyagari’s historical calibration, a proof of continuous-asset equilibrium existence, or evidence that the asset ceiling does not matter.

Aiyagari’s production framework distinguishes individual income and wealth dynamics from stationary aggregates and closes household saving with capital demand and factor prices. That distinction motivates the equilibrium architecture above. The article’s continuous income-fluctuation problem requires conditions beyond our finite theorem; its quantitative findings are not imported into these examples. See Aiyagari, Uninsured Idiosyncratic Risk and Aggregate Saving, Quarterly Journal of Economics 109(3), 1994, printed pages 664–667.

7. Why the mean is generally not an aggregate state

With fixed prices and no aggregate uncertainty, an invariant distribution is a constant equilibrium object. Outside stationarity, one must propagate μt+1=μtQg. With aggregate shocks and changing prices, the policy generally depends on individual (a,e), aggregate productivity and the distribution μ; tomorrow’s distribution is induced by those policies and shocks. A proposed approximation based on mean wealth must demonstrate that its omitted distributional information has a small effect for the question being asked.

Worked case 4. Equal mean wealth, different next aggregate saving

Take a two-date log household with β=R=1, current endowment 1, certain next endowment 3, assets a≥0, and next assets s≥0. It chooses log(a+1−s)+log(s+3). Strict concavity and the derivative at zero give g(a)=max{(a−2)/2,0}. Consider population A with every household at a=2, and population B with half at a=0 and half at a=4. Both have current mean assets 2 and identical income.

Population A’s mean saving and consumption are (0,3). Population B’s are (1/2,5/2): its wealthy half saves one and consumes four, while its poor half saves zero and consumes one. Both obey C+S=3, but their next mean wealth differs. Therefore current mean assets and common income cannot determine next aggregate saving on this population class. The result comes from the kink at the borrowing limit, not an aggregate shock. It is a two-date nonaggregation example, not a claimed stationary equilibrium.

The distribution need not always be carried in full. An exactly affine saving policy g(a,e)=γa+η(e) aggregates as E g=γE a+Eη(e). But nonlinearities, constraints and the correlation of wealth with persistent income can destroy such a finite-moment closure. An accurate reduced distribution rule is an approximation to be tested, not an identity obtained by replacing every household with its mean.

8. Price search and numerical error are distinct problems

Worked case 5. A sign change without a deterministic grid equilibrium

Consider a separate two-date exchange market with a unit mass of identical households, endowment 4 at each date, no inherited bond, and utility log c0+βlog c1 with β=1/2. Each household can choose only a bond d∈(-1,2), priced q∈(0,2), giving c0=4−qd and c1=4+d. All these choices are feasible.

Holding d=2 instead of d=−1 is preferred exactly when log[(4−2q)/(4+q)]+(1/2)log2>0. Its left side is strictly decreasing in q. Equality occurs at q*=4(√2−1)/(1+2√2)=0.4327767502. Below q* every household demands 2; above it every household demands −1. At q* both are optimal. A common deterministic rule for this single household state selects one or the other, so its aggregate bond position is never zero. A bisection interval can shrink to q* while the absolute clearing error stays at least 1. Thus this restricted common-deterministic-policy economy has no clearing equilibrium, despite a sign change in demand. This is not a nonexistence claim about the continuous-asset economy.

If heterogeneous choices among indifferent households are admitted, let mass 1/3 choose d=2 and the remaining 2/3 choose d=−1 at q*. Net bonds then equal zero and aggregate consumption equals (4,4) across the two dates. Each household’s realized consumption respects its own budget. The same allocation may be described with a public lottery selecting optimal actions before consumption, provided that lottery is part of the arrangement. Simply linearly interpolating the reported aggregate demand without specifying implementable household choices is not this argument.

A related infinite-horizon selection failure can occur even when an equilibrium exists within the original finite economy. Let u(c)=c, β=3/4, fixed income 4 and bond assets {−1,2}, with c=b+4−qb′ and q near 3/4. Every choice is strictly feasible. A value ansatz V(b)=b+C gives V(b)=b+4+βC+maxb′(β−q)b′. For q<β all states choose 2; for q>β all choose −1. A tie rule choosing the smaller action at q=β produces selected stationary demand 2 below the threshold and −1 at and above it, with no root. At q=β, however, every action is optimal. The identity policy g(b)=b and invariant masses (2/3,1/3) clear bonds. The algorithm’s no-root result is a failure of its selection, not a proof that the economy has no equilibrium.

A practical numerical sequence. At each trial price, check positive consumption and nonempty action sets; solve and certify the household finite problem; build Q and verify row sums; establish how stationary distributions are selected and whether iteration actually converges; compute aggregate assets, labor and goods; then examine price clearing. Keep these residuals separate. A policy can be optimal at a trial price while aggregate markets fail badly.

For a continuous target economy, refine the asset mesh and expand its upper truncation separately. Check mass and policy choices near both boundaries, evaluate consumption and Euler inequalities between grid nodes, and compare the invariant distribution, aggregate assets and cleared prices across refinements. A coarse grid can give an exact Bellman residual for the wrong feasible set. An inactive upper grid endpoint in a few simulations is weaker evidence than a proved invariant bound or systematic tail checks. Euler equalities are inappropriate at a binding lower constraint; complementarity or one-sided errors are needed.

A useful conditional error result separates discretization from iteration. Suppose two contraction operators T,T̃ on the same space have modulus β and fixed points V,Ṽ, and ‖TṼ−T̃Ṽ‖∞≤η. Then ‖V−Ṽ‖∞≤β‖V−Ṽ‖∞+η, hence at most η/(1−β). A bound on one-step maximization loss can contribute to η: if the maximand is uniformly L-Lipschitz in next assets and a feasible grid point lies within h of each candidate, the maximization loss is at most Lh. Continuation interpolation and truncation errors must also be bounded, and positive consumption or other regularity is needed for finite L. Comparing two meshes is a diagnostic; it does not by itself establish this uniform η.

Distribution errors can amplify policy or transition errors. If Q satisfies the m-step minorization with ε and Q̃ has an invariant distribution π̃, then ‖π−π̃‖1≤mδ/ε, where δ=maxs∑j|Qsj−Q̃sj|. Indeed, insert π̃Qm between πQm and π̃Q̃m, use the contraction for their first difference, and telescope Qm−Q̃m as a sum of m single-step replacements; each has norm at most δ after multiplication by stochastic matrices. Small errors need not remain small when ε is near zero. A tiny change in a continuous policy also need not make δ small under hard nearest-grid assignment.

Evidence and welfare. A fitted wealth distribution is a conditional model outcome. Income-process estimates, borrowing constraints, preference heterogeneity, mortality, transfers and asset measurement can all affect it. Reserve moments not used in calibration, assess uncertainty and compare mechanisms. A transition after a reform changes both prices and households’ positions; comparing only two stationary average consumptions omits transitional welfare and distributional incidence. The representative-planner welfare equivalence from the preceding chapter does not automatically survive uninsured household risk.

Give an explicit five-part grading rubric with one empirical test and one distributional welfare requirement.
Complete solutions and critique rubrics

1. More current cash

Under certainty, 5−s=s+2 gives s=3/2 and both consumptions 7/2. Under risk, (s+1)(s+3)=(5−s)(s+2) gives 2s²+s−7=0. The positive root is (√57−1)/4=1.6374586088; the risky consumption vector is (3.3625413912,2.6374586088,4.6374586088). The other root is negative. The derivative at zero is −1/5+2/3>0 and tends to −∞ as s↑5; the objective has strictly negative second derivative throughout 0≤s<5. Hence this is the unique global optimum. Comparing feasible roots alone would not supply the concavity and boundary argument.

2. Debt units

The saving-unit natural bound is −(1/2)/(1/10)=-5. The imposed bound −2 is tighter. At a=−5 on the permanently low-income path, maintaining the debt consumes zero, which gives −∞ log utility, so it is not a positive-consumption allocation. Since q=1/R=10/11 and a=qb, b=Ra; the natural payoff-unit bound is -11/2. At that bound, e+(1−q)b=1/2−1/2=0, confirming the units. With R≤1 the worst-income perpetuity is not a finite amount of this form; the solvency problem and any imposed credit rule must be specified separately.

3. Stationary marginal utility

Integrate u′(c)=βR E[u′(c′)|a,e]+λ over the invariant law. Iterated expectations and stationarity make E u′(c′)=E u′(c), so (1−βR)E u′(c)=Eλ≥0. Finite positive E u′(c) permits division. If λ>0 on positive mass, nonnegativity and integrability give Eλ>0, hence strict inequality. Discrete actions, a binding upper asset cap, a nonstationary distribution or infinite marginal-utility expectation each invalidate a premise. Three correctly explained examples suffice. Numerically Eλ=(1−4/5)2=2/5.

4. Changed patience in exchange

The same policy has D=(1/2)log(8/3), independent of β because the shared continuation term cancels in W+−W−. At β=2/3, the values are (1.4255553730,2.8511107460,2.1187025536,3.1387928185). Its three alternative-action gaps are (0.0392610119,0.3662040962,0.1037995330), all positive. The first state still has only one feasible action. Thus the policy remains optimal; Q and its unique uniform μ are unchanged. Mean inherited and next bonds are zero and mean consumption=endowment=5/2. This verifies another exact restricted-asset equilibrium at q=1, not a continuous-asset equilibrium or price-uniqueness theorem.

5. Off-stationary distributions

Starting at μ0=(1,0,0,0), μ1=(1/2,1/2,0,0) and μ2=(1/4,1/4,1/4,1/4). At date 0 inherited bonds and chosen next bonds are (−1/2,−1/2); consumption and endowment are (1,1). At date 1 inherited bonds and chosen next bonds are (−1/2,0), while consumption and endowment are (2,5/2). These four date-1 values are (-1/2,0,2,5/2). The integrated budget C+qB′=B+E holds at both dates, but the inherited distribution initially has nonzero net debt and is inconsistent with this closed zero-supply exchange economy. The fixed-price household/distribution transition is a valid mathematical experiment; it is not an equilibrium path from an admissible balanced initial ownership allocation. A stationary equilibrium does not make arbitrary initial distributions market clearing.

6. Distinct wealth classes

In state order (0,L),(0,H),(1,L),(1,H), Q has rows (1/2,1/2,0,0),(1/2,1/2,0,0),(0,0,1/2,1/2),(0,0,1/2,1/2). Every invariant distribution is (t/2,t/2,(1−t)/2,(1−t)/2), 0≤t≤1: stationarity equalizes the two income masses within each wealth class but imposes no transfer between classes. Starting with wealth all zero or all one leaves disjoint wealth support forever. Positive entries of Π do not connect wealth classes. For the flip matrix, solving πQ=π forces (1/2,1/2), yet (1,0) alternates with (0,1); uniqueness alone does not imply convergence of the raw iterates.

7. Accounts cannot replace incentives

The proposed policy still has continuation gap D=(1/2)log3. At β=3/4 its low-wealth/high-income advantage is (3/8)log3−log(3/2)=0.0065145001, which remains positive. Thus this change does not destroy the certificate: the other gaps log2−(3/8)log3 and (3/8)log3−log(4/3) are also positive, and the same finite equilibrium remains valid. A claim that this particular decrease necessarily makes the household abandon saving would be false. More generally, the first gap changes sign at β=2log(3/2)/log3; below it the proposed policy fails, even though mechanically retaining Q still produces the old stationary and goods accounts. Household optimality is a separate equilibrium condition.

8. A right mean with a wrong distribution

μQ=(1/4,1/4,1/4,1/4), hence μQ² is the same invariant vector. The exact initial L1 distance is 3/5. The one-step-residual bound m‖μQ−μ‖1/ε gives 6/5; the direct two-step bound gives the exact 3/5. Both μ and π put half the mass on each bond level, so mean bonds are zero. Nevertheless μ has a different wealth-income association, with more mass on the diagonal states. A correct mean cannot certify the full joint distribution.

9. Three equal means

Population C also has mean assets 2. Its saving at assets 1 and 3 is 0 and 1/2, so its mean saving and consumption are (1/4,11/4). Thus A, C and B have saving 0,1/4,1/2 despite equal wealth means and common income. If g(a,e)=γa+η(e), linearity of expectation gives E g=γE a+Eη(e), with no independence assumption required because the expression is additive. Our actual g=max{(a−2)/2,0} has different slopes below and above 2 and is not a single affine function across the combined support. Replacing it by its value at the mean discards the mass on each side of the borrowing kink.

10. Continuous bond clearing

For real d with positive consumption, the derivative is −q/(4−qd)+(1/2)/(4+d). Strict concavity gives d(q)=[2−4q]/[(3/2)q]. Demand is zero exactly at q=1/2, yielding consumption (4,4). The derivative equation gives an interior optimum for every q>0 because the implied consumptions are positive; the utility tends to −∞ at the two feasibility boundaries. The continuous clearing price 1/2 differs from the coarse-grid indifference price approximately0.432777. A mixture of −1 and 2 has mean zero but gives dispersed consumption, whereas a continuously feasible zero bond delivers (4,4) to every household. Strict concavity makes averaging allocations different from averaging utilities. Grid lotteries are an arrangement or approximation requiring explicit interpretation, not proof that the original continuous policy has been solved.

11. Separate certificates

The iteration value error is 0.0002/(1−0.9)=0.002. The same-domain discretization error is 0.003/(1−0.9)=0.03, so the triangle inequality bounds total value error by 0.032. This addition is valid only when both error statements apply to compatible representations of the same target, including interpolation/truncation. The stationary-distribution bound is 4(0.0001)/0.2=0.002. A span of10 bounds mean-asset error by (10/2)0.002=0.01. A sign change in selected asset demand still does not give a zero if the map jumps; one must report the actual clearing residual and, if necessary, justify a feasible equilibrium selection.

12. Ten-point referee rubric

Distribution theorem (2 points). Income irreducibility does not establish joint wealth-income irreducibility or convergence. Supply the induced Q, closed classes and either a verified minorization/mixing bound or an appropriate alternative invariant-distribution analysis. The identity saving counterexample defeats the stated inference.

Aggregation and evidence (2 points). A mean match does not identify the joint distribution or mechanism. Reserve a wealth-income covariance, borrowing-bound mass or consumption response by liquid wealth as an out-of-fit test, with data definitions, measurement error and uncertainty. An empirical test must not be a statistic used to set the same parameter.

Numerical target (2 points). Small Bellman errors certify the stated grid problem. Add asset-mesh and tail refinement, consumption feasibility, between-grid checks and a proved error bound if claiming certification of a continuous target. Keep optimization, discretization, distribution and market-clearing errors distinct.

Equilibrium (2 points). A discontinuous selected demand can change sign without attaining zero. Report household optimality, stationary mass, actual bond/capital and goods residuals and the treatment of indifference. In production, factor prices must correspond to the aggregate capital actually supplied.

Welfare (2 points). Greater aggregate saving does not imply each household gains: preferences, prices, risk and transition paths determine welfare. Require household or type-specific expected discounted welfare from a stated initial distribution and feasible policy financing, including losses and gains across wealth-income states. A permissible conclusion is conditional numerical evidence for selected equilibrium moments under stated primitives, not a universal welfare or identification theorem.

10. Teach-back and delayed retention

Teach-back. Explain why households can accumulate positive physical capital in an Aiyagari economy while bonds must net to zero in a Huggett economy; why positive income-transition probabilities do not ensure unique wealth distributions; and why exact Bellman arithmetic need not imply exact equilibrium.

Benchmark answer. Physical capital is a positive-supply productive asset owned by households and priced through firms’ marginal products. Huggett credit is a claim on other households, so aggregate creditor assets cancel debtor liabilities. A policy can trap each wealth level in a separate class even when income mixes freely. Bellman arithmetic certifies optimization at supplied prices and allowed actions; equilibrium additionally needs invariant-distribution selection, ownership-consistent mass and goods accounts, and exact asset or capital clearing. A coarse asset grid may change all of these objects.

Delayed retention, one week later. (1) Write the borrowing-limit Euler inequality in saving units. (2) Give a chain with an invariant distribution but nonconvergent pointwise iteration. (3) State the missing premise in “asset demand changes sign, therefore equilibrium exists.” (4) Give two populations with equal mean wealth but different saving from this chapter.

(2) The two-state flip chain has invariant (1/2,1/2) but alternates from a point mass. (3) Continuity of a suitable single-valued selection, or another valid correspondence/existence argument, is missing; actual clearing must be checked. (4) All households at a=2 save zero in case 4; half at0 and half at4 have the same mean2 but save1/2 on average.
