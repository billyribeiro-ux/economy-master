Capability and prerequisites

After this chapter, you can construct a dated incremental project cash-flow schedule, calculate net present value and an internal rate of return when appropriate, and explain why a value-creating proposal may still be infeasible. You can compare mutually exclusive choices without confusing a percentage return with value added, and identify when unequal project lives require an explicit service or replacement plan.

Complete Volumes 3, 5 and 7. In particular, retain dated value and compounding, opportunity costs and feasible alternatives, cash and financing schedules, and cash-versus-profit diagnosis. No cost-of-capital estimation is assumed here: the rate is supplied. Chapters 2–4 of this volume will distinguish cash-flow claims, continuation values and risk-matched rates.

A manager offers two arguments for a new line: “Its accounting profit is positive” and “Its percentage return is the highest.” Neither answers how much value the line adds relative to a feasible alternative. Nor does either establish that the company can pay for it today. Capital budgeting connects the operating decision, its full cash consequences and their dates.

1. Define the decision before constructing the cash flow

Capital budgeting evaluates commitments of resources whose consequences extend across dates. Begin with an actual comparison: install versus do not install, replace versus retain, or one design versus another. The without-project case is what would realistically happen if this proposal were rejected. It is not necessarily today's business repeated unchanged. Demand, competitors, maintenance and existing contracts can change in both cases.

Incremental cash at date t: Ct = firm cash with the decision at t − firm cash without it at t.

The boundary is the affected business as a whole. A transfer between its divisions is not a receipt from an outside customer. New-product sales that displace existing contribution can reduce the increment. Conversely, a project can benefit existing products. Record only the change relative to the same baseline. If an existing product would lose some customers to a competitor anyway, attributing that entire decline to the proposed launch exaggerates the launch's cost; assuming all losses would occur anyway without evidence is equally weak.

All numerical cases below are original, in thousands of currency units (kCU), with annual end dates unless stated otherwise. They are teaching models, not observations about a company or tax jurisdiction.

2. Net present value measures value at one date

A discount factor dt converts one unit of the stated date-t cash-flow claim into its date-0 value; d0 = 1. Factors must match the cash flow's currency, timing and risk. For a constant appropriate annual rate r > −1, dt = (1 + r)−t. This chapter supplies that rate instead of estimating it. A loan coupon, an accounting return or the company's historical growth rate is not automatically the appropriate r.

NPV = Σt=0…T dtCt.
At a constant rate: NPV(r) = C0 + C1/(1+r) + … + CT/(1+r)T.

Each term expresses a consequence in the same date-0 units. If the only initial flow is an investment I > 0, then C0 = −I: NPV is the value of future net cash less the required initial resources. Under the stated valuation and feasibility assumptions, positive NPV means the proposal adds value relative to the specified alternative. Zero means financial indifference in that model, not certainty about the forecast. A project can have large future receipts and negative NPV if their value is smaller than their cost.

The OpenStax NPV section supplies an introductory timeline-and-discounting route. The timeline matters as much as the arithmetic: moving an amount to another date changes what is being valued.

Value additivity follows directly when the same valid linear valuation factors apply to the streams being combined:

V(C + G) = Σ dt(Ct + Gt) = V(C) + V(G).
Hence NPV(B − A) = NPV(B) − NPV(A), under those same factors.

This is a distributive-law result, not permission to use one arbitrary rate for all risks. If projects need different risk adjustments, value the relevant claims consistently rather than subtracting them and discounting the difference at an invented common rate. If choosing both changes operating cash, first construct the combined case: synergy or congestion means CA+B need not equal CA + CB.

For independent feasible projects with additive values and adequate funding, each positive NPV contributes to total value. For mutually exclusive projects, compare complete alternatives and choose the highest positive NPV, including rejection with NPV zero when available. With a hard resource constraint, select the feasible combination with the greatest total NPV. Do not replace that constrained problem with the slogan “take every positive-NPV project.” Financial value also does not waive safety, legal or other binding requirements or capture every external effect on other people.

3. Build the operating-to-investment bridge

For the following deliberately simple tax model, let Mt be incremental revenue minus incremental cash operating costs, including lost contribution and foregone taxable rent. Let Dt be tax depreciation, τ the tax rate, and Wt the incremental noncash operating working-capital balance after date-t movements. Borrowing and cash itself are excluded from W. Suppose all incremental taxable income and deductions affect cash tax at that date, including immediately usable losses. Then:

Operating tax = τ(Mt − Dt).
Operating cash before working capital = Mt − τ(Mt − Dt)
= (Mt − Dt)(1 − τ) + Dt
= Mt(1 − τ) + τDt.
Project cash = operating cash − additional equipment cash − ΔW + after-tax disposal cash.

The term τD is the depreciation tax shield under these assumptions. It is tax saved relative to otherwise identical taxable cash margin, not a second payment from the equipment. Deduct equipment cash once when paid. Add depreciation back only if the bridge began with profit after depreciation. Book depreciation and tax depreciation need not coincide in practice; if they differ, use the actual tax schedule and a reconciled tax-cash bridge. If deductions are delayed or unusable, τD is not an immediate receipt.

If disposal proceeds are S and remaining tax basis is B, a stipulated symmetric disposal-tax rule gives tax τ(S − B) and after-tax proceeds S − τ(S − B). A loss produces a contemporaneous tax benefit only under the expressly supplied usability assumption. Neither the book gain nor a book loss is another sale receipt. Terminal working-capital release is separate from disposal proceeds. Claim full recovery only when the case supports collection, sale and settlement at those amounts; a forecast ending is not evidence that all balances become cash.

The MIT capital-budgeting notes connect incremental after-tax cash, working capital and opportunity cost to NPV. Here the tax rules and dates are stipulated so the arithmetic is determinate. No claim about a current country's deduction, tax rate or reporting rule follows.

This operating investment comparison contains neither loan proceeds nor interest and principal distributions to funders. Its supplied discount rate belongs to that stream. Subtracting interest while using a rate meant for cash before financing can mismatch claims or count financing consequences twice. This does not assert that financing is irrelevant: availability, tax effects, fees and distress can matter. Their consistent valuation and separate treasury schedules follow in later chapters.

4. IRR is a root of a cash-flow equation

The internal rate of return is a rate ρ > −1 satisfying NPV(ρ) = 0 for the stated periodic cash-flow sequence. It is computed from the sequence, while the opportunity rate is an externally justified input to valuation. Do not choose the discount rate merely to make a desired project pass.

For a conventional investment, C0 < 0, every later flow is nonnegative and at least one is positive. Its NPV is continuous and strictly decreasing over r > −1: increasing r strictly reduces at least one positive discounted receipt and increases none. As r approaches −1 from above, a positive future term grows without bound; as r grows without bound, the future terms approach zero and NPV approaches the negative initial flow. The intermediate-value argument therefore gives exactly one IRR on r > −1. The root is positive only when NPV(0) > 0, zero when the undiscounted sum is zero, and negative otherwise.

For this conventional case and one applicable constant opportunity rate k > −1, monotonicity proves NPV(k) > 0 exactly when IRR > k. This is an accept/reject equivalence for that stream. It does not prove that the highest IRR creates the most value among exclusive alternatives, or that one IRR can be compared with a changing term structure as though there were a unique hurdle rate.

Compute a conventional root by bracketing rates whose NPVs have opposite signs and repeatedly halving the bracket. For Kestrel, NPV at 10% is positive and at 20% is negative; bisection gives IRR ≈ 13.357868%. Retain precision and verify the residual, rather than treating the calculator's final display as evidence that all cash dates and signs were correct. The lab searches in x = 1 + r > 0, so it can also locate a negative conventional IRR.

With later outflows, NPV need not decrease, and a root finder can find one of several roots or fail because none exists. Repeated sign changes warn of a possible problem; they do not by themselves tell you the number of admissible roots. A generic software “IRR” output without a domain and completeness check is not a decision rule.

Worked example 2 — Two roots, no roots, and a borrowing reversal

A separate project has C = (−100, 240, −143) at dates 0, 1 and 2. The final obligation is real and cannot be discarded. Put x = 1 + ρ > 0 and multiply the zero-NPV equation by x²:

−100x² + 240x − 143 = −100(x − 1.1)(x − 1.3) = 0.

Both 10% and 30% are IRRs. At a required rate of 20%, NPV is −100 + 240/1.2 − 143/1.2² = 25/36 ≈ 0.694444. At zero it is −3; at 40% it is approximately −1.530612. Choosing whichever root favors approval is meaningless. Value the complete stream at the justified rate. The positive NPV between the roots does not eliminate the final cash obligation or its funding requirement.

For C = (−100, 180, −90), the numerator after multiplying by x² is −100x² + 180x − 90 = −100(x − 0.9)² − 9, strictly negative for every positive x. There is no IRR on the admissible domain, and NPV is negative at every such rate. Failure to return an IRR is not itself proof that the input was mistyped.

Finally, receiving 80 now and repaying 96 next year has an IRR of 20%, but the receiver's NPV at 10% is 80 − 96/1.1 = −80/11 ≈ −7.272727. This is borrowing, with the opposite cash orientation to investing −80 and receiving 96. A borrowing rate above the alternative funding rate is costly, not a reason to apply the conventional “IRR above hurdle” acceptance rule. The financial claim and the perspective must be stated.

5. Compare value across exclusive choices

Mutually exclusive projects cannot both be selected. The relevant comparison is their whole dated difference, including any remaining cash or subsequent opportunity implied by the choice. Holding unused funds at the applicable opportunity return adds zero NPV; it is not a reason to prefer a smaller project with a higher percentage. Scale and cash timing can both change rankings.

Worked example 3 — Scale and timing give different rankings

Two one-year designs perform the same exclusive commercial role. Small costs 40 now and pays 56 next year; Large costs 100 and pays 130. They have the same supplied 10% rate, and sufficient funding exists for either. Small has IRR 40% and NPV −40 + 56/1.1 = 120/11 ≈ 10.909091. Large has IRR 30% and NPV 200/11 ≈ 18.181818. Choose Large for its greater value under these conditions.

Large minus Small is (−60, 74). Its NPV is 80/11 ≈ 7.272727, exactly the difference between the alternatives' NPVs. The extra investment has conventional IRR 74/60 − 1 = 23⅓%, exceeding 10%; this incremental check agrees. There are no intermediate distributions in this one-year example. A story about reinvesting intermediate receipts therefore cannot explain the ranking conflict: the percentage and value measures answer different questions.

Now compare separate two-year alternatives Early = (−100, 130, 0) and Late = (−100, 0, 150), with no replacement opportunities, no other cash and sufficient funding. Early's IRR is 30%; Late's is √1.5 − 1 ≈ 22.474487%. At 10%, Early's NPV is 18.181818 while Late's is 23.966942. At 20%, Early's NPV is 8.333333 and Late's is 4.166667. Their ranking changes with the justified opportunity rate.

NPV(Late − Early) = −130/(1+r) + 150/(1+r)²
= [150 − 130(1+r)]/(1+r)².
Equality occurs at r = 150/130 − 1 = 2/13 ≈ 15.384615%.

The denominator is positive for r > −1, so the numerator determines the sign. Below the crossover, Late adds more value; above it, Early does, subject to checking the chosen alternative against rejection. The crossover is the IRR of this particular difference stream after its initial zero, not a universal extra hurdle rate. No new reinvestment project is part of either cash sequence. If the decision instead requires an accumulated balance at a future date, any actual reinvestment opportunities and rates must be specified and modeled separately.

Simple payback asks when cumulative undiscounted receipts cover the initial outlay. For Kestrel, the cumulative totals are −105, −74.75, −33 and 33.75, so recovery occurs at the third supplied end date. A fractional “2.49 years” would assume cash arrives smoothly during Year 3, contrary to this end-date convention. Payback ignores the opportunity rate and cash after the recovery date; discounted payback still ignores later cash. Neither establishes value or replaces the dated financing schedule.

For one initial outflow I and later nonnegative cash, a profitability index may be defined as PV of future cash divided by I, equal to 1 + NPV/I. An index above one then agrees with positive NPV for that project. Its ranking need not solve an indivisible budget problem: selecting a high ratio can leave unusable funding while a different combination adds more total value. Exercise 7 demonstrates this with an exhaustive feasible-set comparison.

6. Unequal lives require an actual horizon

Different lives do not make present values incomparable: both are date-0 values. The missing issue is often a mismatched obligation. A one-year machine does not supply two years of required service unless the plan says how the second year is provided. Conversely, an unrepeatable short project should not be silently replicated forever. Specify the service period, replacement availability, changing costs, quality, residual value and any opportunity after a project ends.

Under a common constant rate, identical repeatable service and cost cycles, an equivalent annual cost can summarize the comparison. If P is the present cost of one n-year cycle, its equivalent end-year amount a satisfies P = aΣt=1…n(1+r)−t. Thus a equals P divided by that annuity factor. At r = 0 the factor is n. This is a restatement of a specified cost stream, not evidence that replacement prices or technology will remain unchanged.

The scoped equivalent-annuity and horizon discussion makes replacement assumptions visible. We use a finite matched service contract below, so the comparison can also be checked by listing every payment.

Worked example 4 — Two years of the same service

A no-tax service must be delivered for exactly two years, after which no service or resale value remains. Short costs 24 now, lasts one year and costs 6 to operate at its year-end. An identical replacement is contractually available at date 1 for the same 24 and performs the second year at operating cost 6. Long costs 42 now, lasts both years and costs 5 at each year-end. Quality and capacity are identical, both plans are feasible, and r = 10%.

Short's complete two-year costs are (24, 30, 6): date 1 includes the first operating payment and replacement purchase. Its present cost is 56.231405. Long's costs (42, 5, 5) have present cost 50.677686. Long saves 5.553719 in present value. Comparing only Short's first cycle, present cost 24 + 6/1.1 ≈ 29.454545, with Long's two-year cost would omit a year of service.

Short's equivalent annual cost is (24 + 6/1.1)/(1/1.1) = 32.4. Long's is (42 + 5/1.1 + 5/1.1²)/(1/1.1 + 1/1.1²) = 29.2. Both annual amounts multiplied by the same two-year annuity factor reproduce their matched-horizon costs. The agreement follows from the supplied repetition assumptions. If service were needed for only one year, Long's date-1 resale or other continuing value would be required for a new comparison.

Download the original inputs, standard-library Python benchmark, preserved results and run guide. Save them together and run python3 v08-c01-analysis.py. The program derives Kestrel's margin, tax, working capital and terminal cash; discounts the stream; brackets its conventional IRR; solves the two-period nonconventional examples; constructs both service schedules; and enumerates the small capital budget in Exercise 7. Exact rational values accompany rounded displays. The tax model explicitly permits immediate use of every incremental loss; it is not a general tax engine.

First reproduce the main stream without consulting the results. Then change only the sunk-study amount: the NPV should not move. Change the cash foregone from an alternative use: the NPV should move. Add four to both intermediate working-capital balances, keeping the terminal zero: the capital is recovered later, but its delay has a cost. Finally, reduce committed time-0 funding: the valuation need not change even as feasibility worsens. Each intervention changes only its stated assumption; a real project change may require several linked inputs to change.

A decision record should state the feasible alternatives and rejection case, dated incremental cash, rate rationale, constraints and a result-changing uncertainty. Ask why an attractive margin would persist and what commercial evidence supports it. Favor a transparent conditional recommendation over a false certainty. Expected or forecast NPV does not identify the probability of loss, authenticate a forecast or prove that a bad state can be funded; those are separate analyses. Preserve the distinction between a useful valuation and authorization to spend.

Teach-back and delayed check

Teach-back. In three minutes, explain to an operating manager why accounting profit, project NPV, IRR and available cash answer different questions. Use one dated cash bridge and one exclusive-project comparison. Then explain what must be documented before a positive-NPV proposal can start.
