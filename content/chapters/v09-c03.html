Choose a portfolio, then inspect what made it optimal

Capability. Derive minimum-variance and efficient portfolios, allocate between risky assets and a safe asset with the correct sign conditions, certify a constrained optimum, and explain how estimated inputs can change the answer. Prerequisites are Portfolio mathematics and diversification, the return conventions in Returns, payoffs and investment opportunity sets, and the linear-system and convex-optimality tools of linear algebra and convexity, duality and sensitivity.

A mandate asks for a 10% expected annual return with the least possible variance. A spreadsheet produces a negative weight. Replacing it by zero and rescaling the other weights feels harmless, but can violate the return target. A second analyst changes an expected return by twenty basis points and obtains a large short position. Neither result can be judged by the word “optimal.” We need the objective, feasible trades, moments, numerical certificate and uncertainty surrounding the inputs.

Every numerical asset and return model below is original and synthetic. Returns are one-year simple total returns in a common currency, expressed as decimals: 0.08 means 8%. Trades occur at date 0 and are held to date 1, with no interim rebalancing, tax, transaction cost or short fee. Borrowing and short positions are permitted only where stated. These cases establish conditional model results, not current expected returns or investment recommendations.

1. What mean-variance efficiency does and does not mean

Let R be an n-vector of risky returns, μ = E[R], and Σ = Cov(R), all for the same horizon and information set. For beginning-wealth weights w with 1ᵀw = 1, expected return is m(w) = μᵀw and variance is v(w) = wᵀΣw. An admissible portfolio is mean-variance efficient if no other admissible portfolio has at least as high a mean and at most as large a variance, with one strict improvement. This is a two-moment comparison on a declared feasible set. It does not order every pair of return distributions or every investor's preferences.

A useful stipulated preference is J(w) = μᵀw − (γ/2)wᵀΣw, where γ > 0 measures aversion to variance in the chosen return units. Its indifference curves prefer a higher mean and lower variance. If returns are re-expressed in percentage points, their means multiply by 100 and variances by 10,000; γ must divide by 100 to preserve the ranking after scaling J by 100. Treating an 8% mean as 8 alongside decimal variances changes the problem.

There is an exact expected-utility interpretation in a special model. If a portfolio return is normal with mean m and variance v, exponential utility −exp(−γR) has expected value −exp(−γm + γ²v/2), by completing the square in the normal density. Maximizing it is equivalent to maximizing m − γv/2. This requires the normal model and the stated exponential utility; γ incorporates any fixed initial-wealth scaling. Normal simple returns have an unbounded lower tail, so they cannot literally describe every limited-liability asset whose return is bounded below by −1. The exact mathematical benchmark and an approximation to actual returns are different claims.

Quadratic expected utility also depends on the first two moments, but E[aR − bR²] = am − b(m² + v), not automatically m − γv/2 with a constant γ for every m. Quadratic utility can stop increasing above a threshold. For general distributions and preferences, skewness, loss probabilities, liabilities, liquidity and catastrophic tails can matter beyond mean and variance. Exercise 6 constructs equal-moment returns with different loss probabilities and different exponential expected utilities. Our finite-state lab simply stipulates J as its criterion; it does not label its bounded distributions normal.

The MIT portfolio-theory lecture supplies a primary teaching route for the mean-variance criterion, frontier and safe-asset combinations. The proofs, sign qualifications and numerical cases here are developed explicitly rather than importing historical stock examples or a universal recommendation to hold one fund.

2. The global minimum-variance portfolio

For the first derivation, allow every real w satisfying 1ᵀw = 1, including short positions, and assume Σ is symmetric positive definite (SPD): zᵀΣz > 0 for every nonzero z. The previous chapter established positive semidefiniteness for any valid covariance matrix. SPD is stronger: no nonzero risky-asset combination has zero variance. Redundant claims such as an asset exactly equal to a combination of the others violate it. Remove the redundancy or formulate the lower-dimensional problem before using an inverse. A covariance matrix estimated from too few common-date observations may also be singular.

Minimize ½wᵀΣw subject to 1ᵀw = 1. With L = ½wᵀΣw − λ(1ᵀw − 1), stationarity is Σw = λ1. Define

a = Σ⁻¹1,   A = 1ᵀΣ⁻¹1 > 0.
wG = a/A,   vG = 1/A,   mG = μᵀa/A.

This stationary point is the unique global minimum, not merely a solver candidate. Any other budget-feasible w can be written wG + z with 1ᵀz = 0. Since ΣwG = 1/A, the cross term vanishes:

wᵀΣw = 1/A + zᵀΣz ≥ 1/A,

with equality only at z = 0. SPD also makes the quadratic grow as weights become arbitrarily large, so a finite minimum exists on the nonempty closed budget plane. The GMV weights do not depend on μ. This protects that particular optimization from direct mean-estimation errors, but not from covariance error, costs or an unsuitable variance objective.

3. Add a target return and derive the whole boundary

Impose μᵀw = m as well as 1ᵀw = 1. Assume μ is not a constant multiple of 1, so the two equalities are independent and every real target m is feasible when shorting is unrestricted. Define b = Σ⁻¹μ, B = 1ᵀΣ⁻¹μ and C = μᵀΣ⁻¹μ. The number Δ = AC − B² is positive: use the inner product ⟨u,v⟩ = uᵀΣ⁻¹v and the strict Cauchy–Schwarz inequality for the independent vectors 1 and μ.

The Lagrangian ½wᵀΣw − λ(1ᵀw − 1) − η(μᵀw − m) gives Σw = λ1 + ημ. Applying both constraints yields Aλ + Bη = 1 and Bλ + Cη = m. Therefore

λ(m) = (C − Bm)/Δ,   η(m) = (Am − B)/Δ,
w(m) = [(C − Bm)a + (Am − B)b]/Δ,
v(m) = (Am² − 2Bm + C)/Δ
= 1/A + (A/Δ)(m − B/A)².

To verify sufficiency without trusting the first-order equations, write any alternative with the same budget and target as w(m) + z. Both 1ᵀz and μᵀz are zero. Thus zᵀΣw(m) = 0 and its variance is v(m) + zᵀΣz. SPD proves the claimed unique minimum. Multiplying Σw = λ1 + ημ by wᵀ also gives v(m) = λ + ηm, an independent arithmetic check.

The minimum-variance boundary includes targets on both sides of mG = B/A. The efficient frontier is its branch m ≥ mG. A target below mG has the same minimum variance as the reflected target 2mG − m, whose mean is higher. On the upper branch, a strictly higher mean requires higher minimum variance; no portfolio can dominate its boundary point. A portfolio above the minimum variance at its own target is already dominated by that target's minimizer.

The weights are affine in m. Consequently any two distinct boundary portfolios span all the boundary portfolios through combinations whose coefficients sum to one; those coefficients need not be positive. This is the algebra behind two-fund separation in the unrestricted risky-asset model. Restrictions can change active assets as the target moves, so this global affine formula need not survive. If μ = c1, Δ = 0: only m = c is feasible, and the GMV problem remains well defined. Dividing by Δ in that case is an error.

Worked example 1 — A return model that supports its covariance matrix

For assets A, B and C, let Z0, ZA, ZB, ZC be independent signs, each ±1 with probability ½. Thus there are sixteen equally likely physical states. Set RA = 0.04 + 0.10Z0 + 0.10ZA, RB = 0.08 + 0.10Z0 + 0.20ZB, and RC = 0.12 + 0.10Z0 + 0.30ZC. All individual simple returns exceed −1. Their moments are

μ = (0.04, 0.08, 0.12)ᵀ,
Σ = (1/100) [[2,1,1], [1,5,1], [1,1,10]]
= 0.01·11ᵀ + diag(0.01,0.04,0.09).

For any nonzero z, zᵀΣz = 0.01(Σzi)² + 0.01zA² + 0.04zB² + 0.09zC² > 0. Solving the two linear systems gives a = (720,180,80)/17 and b = (76,104,84)/85. Hence A = 980/17, B = 264/85, C = 536/2125 and Δ = 416/85.

The GMV portfolio is (36,9,4)/49, with mean 66/1225 ≈ 5.3878% and variance 17/980 ≈ 0.01734694, giving volatility about 13.1708%. Its common-factor variance is 0.01 because the weights sum to one; its idiosyncratic variance is 9/1225. Buying more assets cannot remove that stipulated common exposure while retaining this budget.

At target m = 8%, λ = 1/1300 and η = 4/13. The minimizing weights are (4,5,4)/13, with variance 33/1300 ≈ 0.02538462 and volatility about 15.9326%. Both the budget and weighted mean check directly. At m = 10%, weights become (−1,28,25)/52 and variance 441/10400 ≈ 0.04240385. This last solution is feasible only under the unrestricted-short mandate. These population inputs are supplied by the fictional state law, not estimated from sixteen observed years.

4. A safe asset, risky demand and tangency signs

Now introduce a safe simple return rf, with unrestricted lending and borrowing at that same rate. Let x be risky exposures per unit of initial wealth; cash/borrowing weight is 1 − 1ᵀx. Unlike w above, x need not sum to one. The total return is rf + xᵀ(R − rf1). With q = μ − rf1, its mean is rf + qᵀx and variance xᵀΣx.

For the stipulated preference J(x) = rf + qᵀx − (γ/2)xᵀΣx, complete the square:

x* = Σ⁻¹q/γ,
J(x) = rf + qᵀΣ⁻¹q/(2γ) − (γ/2)(x − x*)ᵀΣ(x − x*).

SPD and γ > 0 prove a unique optimum for any q. The budget is completed by cash; a negative cash weight means borrowing. Higher γ scales the risky vector down but does not change its direction in this unrestricted model. A borrowing-rate spread, leverage ceiling, short restriction or investor-specific liability changes the problem and can destroy this separation.

For a nonzero risky vector x, define its total portfolio's excess-return-to-volatility ratio as qᵀx/√(xᵀΣx). Cauchy–Schwarz applied to Σ1/2x and Σ−1/2q gives

qᵀx/√(xᵀΣx) ≤ √(qᵀΣ⁻¹q).

If q ≠ 0, equality with the positive upper bound requires x = cΣ⁻¹q for c > 0. A negative c reverses the mean premium and gives the negative of that ratio. This sign matters: maximizing a squared ratio alone loses it. The safe asset itself has zero variance and no defined Sharpe ratio, though its mean and value remain well defined.

Let v = Σ⁻¹q and s = 1ᵀv = B − rfA. If s > 0, define the fully invested risky tangency fund t = v/s. Then 1ᵀt = 1, its premium qᵀt = qᵀΣ⁻¹q/s is positive, and a positive amount of this fund achieves the upper bound. The optimal allocation is x* = yt with y = s/γ; its safe weight is 1 − y. For y ≥ 0, total mean is rf + y(μᵀt − rf) and volatility is y√(tᵀΣt). This produces the upper straight line in mean/volatility space.

If s = 0 and q ≠ 0, v is a nonzero zero-net-investment risky direction and cannot be normalized to a risky fund with weights summing to one. If s < 0, dividing by s flips the direction: the resulting fully invested fund has a negative premium and is not the positive-premium tangency fund just derived. In either case x* remains valid under the unrestricted cash-and-risky model. If q = 0, the unique mean-variance optimum is all safe cash; normalization and the positive-premium construction are unnecessary. Exercise 4 makes these boundaries concrete. Optimizing a portfolio does not prove an equilibrium pricing relation; the CAPM chapter adds common beliefs, market clearing and other assumptions.

Worked example 2 — How much of the risky fund?

Use the three-asset law above with rf = 2% and γ = 4. Here q = (0.02,0.06,0.10), v = (4,86,76)/85 and s = 166/85 > 0. The risky tangency fund is t = (2,43,38)/83. Its mean is 202/2075 ≈ 9.7349%; variance is 5457/137780 ≈ 0.03960662. Its squared Sharpe ratio is 321/2125 ≈ 0.15105882, so its Sharpe ratio is about 0.3887.

The investor holds y = s/4 = 83/170 of initial wealth in that fund. Actual risky exposures are (1/85,43/170,19/85); cash is 87/170. The total mean is 0.02 + (321/2125)/4 = 491/8500 ≈ 5.7765%, and total variance is 321/34000 ≈ 0.00944118. These are the total portfolio's moments, not the risky fund's moments. At γ = 1 the risky exposures quadruple, their sum becomes 166/85 and cash becomes −81/85. That is a leveraged policy requiring the explicitly allowed funding contract, not four times as much unfinanced wealth.

5. Constraints change the feasible frontier

For a fully invested long-only portfolio, impose w ≥ 0. The feasible set is the simplex: its expected return must lie between the smallest and largest asset means. A target outside that interval is infeasible regardless of how attractive the unconstrained formula looks. Caps, minimum holdings, turnover limits and funding rules can narrow the set further. With a nonempty compact convex feasible set and SPD Σ, the minimum variance exists and is unique. The frontier is generally piecewise rather than a single unrestricted quadratic curve.

For an equality target, the long-only KKT conditions under our sign convention are

Σw − λ1 − ημ − ν = 0,
1ᵀw = 1,   μᵀw = m,   w ≥ 0,   ν ≥ 0,   νiwi = 0.

The objective is ½wᵀΣw; η is unrestricted for an equality target. For a minimum-return inequality μᵀw ≥ m, write m − μᵀw ≤ 0 and require η ≥ 0 as well as complementarity. A cap wi ≤ ui contributes a nonnegative multiplier times wi − ui to the Lagrangian, reversing its stationarity sign relative to a lower bound.

A feasible KKT tuple is a global certificate. For any alternative feasible z with the same target, expand the variance difference: 2(z − w)ᵀΣw + (z − w)ᵀΣ(z − w). The equality terms vanish and the first term becomes 2νᵀz ≥ 0 because νᵀw = 0. The second is nonnegative and strictly positive for z ≠ w. The analogous inequality/cap terms have the appropriate nonnegative signs. This certificate is stronger than reporting that a numerical routine stopped.

In a small problem, enumerate nonempty subsets of assets, solve the equality-constrained problem on each candidate face, discard negative or target-infeasible weights, and compare their variances. Every feasible optimum lies on some face, so complete enumeration is exact for the small case. It is not a claim that enumerating 2ⁿ − 1 subsets is an efficient large-universe method. The lab restricts this procedure to three assets.

Worked example 3 — A binding short-sale restriction

Return to the 10% target. The budget and target imply w = (t − ½, 3/2 − 2t, t), where t = wC. Long-only feasibility requires ½ ≤ t ≤ ¾. On that interval,

v(t) = 41/400 − t/4 + 13t²/50,
v′(t) = −1/4 + 13t/25.

The unrestricted minimizer t = 25/52 is below ½. Since the derivative is positive from ½ onward, the long-only solution is (0,½,½), with variance 17/400 = 0.0425. The restriction raises minimum variance by 1/10400. A KKT certificate is λ = −1/50, η = 5/8, ν = (1/200,0,0): Σw = (0.01,0.03,0.055), and all stationarity, feasibility and complementarity conditions hold.

Clipping the unrestricted negative weight and renormalizing instead gives (0,28/53,25/53). Its mean is 131/1325 ≈ 9.8868%, below the promised 10%. It is not a solution to the same mandate. A 13% target is impossible for a long-only portfolio of means 4%, 8% and 12%; allowing a target with no feasible allocation is not an optimization failure to fix with more iterations.

6. Estimated inputs and the inverse amplify each other

The formulas so far use supplied population moments. With estimates μ̂ and Σ̂, the optimizer is conditional on those estimates. It does not know which favorable-looking mean or low-variance spread is genuine. For risky demand x* = Σ⁻¹q/γ, a change δμ at fixed Σ and rf gives the exact response δx = Σ⁻¹δμ/γ. Hence ‖δx‖₂ ≤ ‖δμ‖₂/(γλmin(Σ)). A small positive eigenvalue permits a large response in its direction.

For a small covariance perturbation H, differentiate Σx = q/γ to obtain dx = Σ⁻¹dμ/γ − Σ⁻¹H x. Equivalently, the exact inverse identity (Σ + H)⁻¹ − Σ⁻¹ = −(Σ + H)⁻¹HΣ⁻¹ shows why both matrices must remain invertible. This is a sensitivity calculation, not a sampling distribution or a confidence interval. For fully invested mean-variance choice, adding the same constant to every mean adds a constant to the objective and leaves the optimizer unchanged; uncertain relative means can still induce large offsetting trades.

Covariance shrinkage replaces an estimated matrix S by Σα = (1 − α)S + αT for 0 ≤ α ≤ 1 and a declared target T. A transparent target is τI with τ = tr(S)/n. If S is PSD, τ > 0 and α > 0, every nonzero z satisfies zᵀΣαz ≥ ατ‖z‖² > 0. The eigenvalues are (1 − α)λi(S) + ατ, so very small estimated eigenvalues move away from zero. If τ = 0, this target supplies no positive curvature. Shrinkage can stabilize inversion while biasing genuine covariance structure; it does not automatically correct the means.

A separate mean shrinkage rule is μκ = (1 − κ)μ̂ + κμ̄1, where μ̄ is the cross-sectional average supplied estimate. It reduces differences between the estimated means. These are model choices: declare the target, intensity, loss criterion and information used to choose them. Selecting α or κ after observing the evaluation outcomes contaminates a claimed forecast test. A fixed illustrative intensity is not an estimated optimal coefficient.

Ledoit and Wolf's original shrinkage manuscript, sections 3.1–3.3, discusses combining covariance estimates with a structured target and choosing an intensity. Their inspected manuscript uses a constant-correlation target. Our identity target, fixed intensities and synthetic audit are separate teaching choices; we do not import its historical performance results or claim that every shrinkage rule dominates every alternative.

Worked example 4 — A twenty-basis-point change, inspected against a known synthetic audit

A separate two-asset forecast has equal variances 0.04 and covariance 0.0396, so S = [[0.04,0.0396],[0.0396,0.04]]. Its eigenvalues are 0.0796 and 0.0004; the ratio is 199. The lab supplies a finite independent-sign construction for these moments. For this example all wealth must be in these two risky assets, shorts are allowed, and γ = 4. Initially both estimated means are 8%, yielding (½,½). Change only the second estimate to 8.2%.

Write w = (½ − d, ½ + d), with common variance v and covariance c. Then portfolio variance is (v + c)/2 + 2(v − c)d², and mean is (μ̂1 + μ̂2)/2 + d(μ̂2 − μ̂1). Differentiating the strictly concave objective gives d* = (μ̂2 − μ̂1)/[2γ(v − c)]. Here d* = 5/8, producing (−1/8,9/8). The mean difference changed by 0.002, but a 62.5-percentage-point shift was encouraged by the tiny estimated variance of the relative trade.

With α = ½ and T = 0.04I, off-diagonal covariance becomes 0.0198. The eigenvalues are 0.0598 and 0.0202, ratio 299/101 ≈ 2.9604. Keeping the perturbed means gives d = 5/404 and weights (197,207)/404. Also shrink those means halfway toward their common average 8.1%: they become 8.05% and 8.15%, d becomes 5/808, and weights become (399,409)/808.

For a transparent audit only, suppose a separately disclosed synthetic population has both true means 8%, both variances 0.04 and covariance 0.02. Its finite-sign construction is in the inputs; the forecast rules above do not use these true parameters. Evaluate every decision on that same population:

7. Reproduce a decision and its certificate

The offline lab includes synthetic inputs, standard-library analysis, complete reference output, four-file manifest and instructions and limits. It reconstructs population moments from independent signs, solves linear systems exactly, enumerates the seven faces of the three-asset long-only problem, reports cash-inclusive demand separately from a normalized tangency fund, and evaluates four shrinkage decisions against a common synthetic audit law. Rational results are preserved as strings; displayed square-root volatilities are labeled approximations.

First reconcile a target's budget, mean, covariance risk and optimality certificate. Then vary the risk-aversion coefficient, target or shrinkage intensity within the documented domain and explain which constraint or normalization changes. Report an infeasible target as infeasible. The named fixture rejects contradictory return/funding/policy labels instead of silently calculating under another convention. A successful calculation does not validate a forecast or guarantee that its assumed shorting and funding contracts exist.

    For an exact comparison multiply the positive expected exponential costs by e. P gives cosh(1), while Q gives 3/4 + cosh(2)/4 = 1/2 + cosh²(1)/2. Q minus P is ½[cosh(1) − 1]² > 0. The distributions are discrete and not normal; equality of two moments does not determine exponential expected utility here. A stipulated mean-variance objective would instead call them tied.

  7. The covariance becomes 0.75×0.0396 = 0.0297, with unchanged diagonal 0.04. Its eigenvalues are 0.0697 and 0.0103, ratio 697/103 ≈ 6.7670. Shrunk means are (0.0805,0.0815), so d = 0.001/[2×4×0.0103] = 5/412 and weights are (201,211)/412. Under the disclosed equal-mean audit law, variance is 0.03 + 0.04(5/412)² = 0.0300058912 approximately, and J is 0.0199882176 approximately.

    In the alternative population where μ = (0.08,0.082) and covariance is the raw S, the unique optimum has d* = 5/8. The objective's quadratic term is −γ(0.04 − 0.0396)d², so completing the square gives J(d*) − J(d) = γ(0.04 − 0.0396)(d − d*)². It is strictly positive for d = 5/412. Thus this population favors its own raw optimum. Keep the objective coefficient consistent with γ/2 times variance.

    A valid empirical comparison would predefine a historical information set, target family, objective and cost/constraint model; choose intensities on training or earlier validation data; then freeze them for later unseen evaluation periods, using common candidate universes and dates. Report uncertainty, turnover and regime sensitivity, and retain unsuccessful alternatives. Equivalent designs respecting the information boundary earn credit. The invented audit table alone does not estimate real forecasting performance.

  8. If μ = c1 and Σ is SPD, b = ca, B = cA and C = c²A, so Δ = 0. Every budget-feasible portfolio has mean c. A target m ≠ c is infeasible; at m = c the extra equality is redundant and the unique GMV weights a/A solve the remaining problem. The target-frontier formula requiring Δ > 0 must not be used.

    For the separate redundant pair, any weights (u,1 − u) produce exactly the same random return, mean 6% and variance 0.04. The direction (1,−1) lies in the covariance nullspace; infinitely many weights represent the same exposure. Shrinking toward a positive diagonal would assign positive variance to that genuinely zero-risk difference. It is a changed risk model, not discovery of an unobserved independent risk. Remove a duplicate asset or represent the lower-dimensional exposure explicitly; if the singularity instead reflects a short estimation sample, justify a separate regularization model and disclose its uncertainty. The cause of singularity determines the appropriate response.

Scope and deeper study

This chapter proves static SPD mean-variance solutions, their sign and degeneracy boundaries, and small convex constraint certificates. It does not solve dynamic trading, liability hedging, investor-specific taxes, estimation of expected returns, nonlinear transaction costs or robust optimization over an uncertainty set.
