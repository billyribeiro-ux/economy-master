Capability and prerequisites

You can trace contributions, profits, dividends and share repurchases through equity; distinguish percentage ownership from control; and build a small consolidated group without counting internal claims or internal profit twice. Use double-entry and closing, the three statements, inventory and receivables, and financing obligations. The acquisition example supplies its asset measurements; advanced valuation is not a hidden prerequisite.

A parent buys 75% of another business. Should it report 75% of the subsidiary's revenue, all its cash, or just the investment purchase price? Those are answers to different questions. We must identify the reporting entity, the rights held, the measurement convention and the date before calculating a number.

1. Equity records claims, not a separate cash reserve

For the stipulated ordinary shares in this chapter, equity is the residual book claim E = A − L. Creditors' contractual claims and shareholders' residual claims differ; a legal label such as “share” does not settle accounting classification. The IFRS Foundation's IAS 32 overview emphasizes contractual payment obligations and contains important exceptions and own-share settlement distinctions. Redeemable, preferred and compound instruments need their actual terms and applicable rules. Here every common share is expressly residual equity, with no mandatory redemption or promised dividend.

A company can increase equity by selling shares or by earning profit, but only the second is performance. A dividend distributes an existing claim; it is not an operating expense. Under our absence of other comprehensive income, revaluations and other equity adjustments, the total-equity identity is

E₁ = E₀ + profit + owner contributions − owner distributions.

Classifying a cash contribution as revenue would preserve A = L + E yet overstate performance. Recording a dividend as expense would make the opposite error. Retained earnings is cumulative retained accounting profit, with specified adjustments where relevant; it is not an earmarked bank balance. A firm may have positive retained earnings but little cash, or cash raised from borrowing and no accumulated profit.

A cash dividend declared before payment records Dr Dividends, Cr Dividends payable; payment records Dr Payable, Cr Cash. Closing transfers the dividend debit to RE. Our cases declare and pay on the same date, allowing the two cash entries to be combined. Actual authorization, solvency and distribution restrictions depend on governing law and contracts; an accounting balance alone is not legal permission to pay.

2. Issued, outstanding and treasury shares

Issued counts shares issued and not retired. Outstanding counts issued shares held outside the issuer: issued minus treasury shares. Authorized shares are a legal ceiling, distinct from both; we stipulate adequate authorization. In this one-class model, only outstanding shares vote and receive distributions. A repurchase for treasury reduces cash and equity but leaves issued capital unchanged. Retirement is a different event, excluded here.

We use the cost-method teaching mechanics described by OpenStax. Repurchasing q shares at price b records Dr Treasury shares qb, Cr Cash qb. On reissue, remove their recorded cost. Proceeds above that cost credit treasury APIC rather than profit. Proceeds below cost first debit the accumulated treasury APIC credit, then RE for any remaining deficiency. This allocation is a specified instructional convention, not a universal rule about every jurisdiction's reserves. Average repurchase cost is used if several lots are supplied to the program; the main case has one lot.

3. Dilution, transfers of value and buyback claims

Distinguish percentage dilution, a falling ownership fraction, from a reduction in wealth. Also distinguish book equity per share E/N from market value per share V/N. Book amounts result from recognition and measurement conventions; market value incorporates expectations and required returns. We supply V below to isolate arithmetic, not to estimate intrinsic value.

Suppose old market equity is V, old shares N and the company issues n new shares at p. Assume issue cash adds np to value, with no fees, information effects, taxes or change in the value of the pre-existing business. The new price is P′ = (V+np)/(N+n). Old owners retain N shares, so their wealth change is

NP′ − V = n(Np−V)/(N+n).

This follows by putting N(V+np)/(N+n) − V over a common denominator. At p = V/N the change is zero even though N/(N+n) < 1. An issue below that supplied fair price transfers value from old to new owners under these assumptions. Valuable investment opportunities, costs or information can change the result; the identity alone does not identify those effects from an observed announcement return.

Worked example 2 — A lower percentage need not mean lower wealth

This is a separate market-value experiment, not Juniper's book ledger. Set V = 300 kCU and N = 100 thousand shares, so the initial price is 3 CU. Issue n = 25 thousand shares at 2 CU: cash raised is 50, new value is 350, and price becomes 350/125 = 2.8. Old owners' wealth is 280, a loss of 20; new shares are worth 70 after a payment of 50, a gain of 20. The cash raised is real, but it does not prevent a transfer between investor groups.

If the issue price is instead 3, new value is 375, price stays 3 and old owners keep wealth 300. Their collective percentage still falls from 100% to 80%. The unchanged 40-thousand-share investor falls from 40% to 32% yet retains wealth 120 in the fair-price issue.

For a separate buyback at fair price 3, repurchasing 20 of the original 100 shares costs 60: remaining value is 240 and shares 80, leaving price 3. The sellers receive 60 and remaining owners hold 240. If next-period profit were held fixed at 30, profit divided by shares would rise from 0.30 to 0.375. That denominator effect does not create value. Cash no longer earns a return, borrowing may add interest, and operating choices may change profit. These are prospective simple per-share comparisons, not a computation of reported basic or diluted EPS with weighted-average shares and options.

Book equity can move differently. Just before Juniper's buyback, equity is 190 and outstanding shares 125, giving book value 1.52 CU per share. After paying 60 for 20 shares, it is 130/105 ≈ 1.2381. Buying above book value lowers book value per remaining share in this case; that does not establish overpayment relative to economic value. Future valuation and payout policy require the tools of Volume 8.

4. Economic ownership, votes and control

Ownership percentage measures a particular claim, such as the share of ordinary dividends. Voting percentage measures specified decision rights. Control concerns the ability to direct relevant activities and affect returns. Multiple share classes, contracts, delegated authority and rights held by others can make those measures diverge.

The IFRS Foundation's May 2011 educational presentation explains the control model through power linked to variable returns, including control possible below a majority and the significance of contractual and agency relationships. The accompanying public IFRS 10 overview identifies control as the consolidation basis and notes an investment-entity exception. Neither a large investment nor exposure to losses alone proves power.

For this chapter, ask three connected questions: who can currently direct the activities that matter for returns; who receives variable returns; and can that decision-maker use the power to affect its own returns? Inspect substantive rights, restrictions and whether a manager acts as an agent. A 60% economic stake consisting of nonvoting shares does not, by itself, answer those questions. Conversely, a 40% shareholder with separately supplied substantive exclusive appointment and decision rights may control. These are reasoning cases, not automatic percentage rules or a complete legal control opinion.

When control is established and no applicable exception intervenes, consolidated statements depict the parent and subsidiary as one economic reporting entity. Each legal company still keeps its own ledger and obligations. External owners of the subsidiary hold a noncontrolling interest (NCI), historically called a minority interest. Our ordinary NCI is an equity claim, not a debt or operating expense. A consolidated cash total does not establish that every unit is freely transferable to the parent: subsidiary creditors, minority rights, contractual restrictions and distribution law remain relevant.

Other interests require other analysis. A passive investment is not automatically consolidated. Significant influence without control can lead to an equity-method investment: in a simplified no-adjustment case its carrying amount grows by the investor's share of investee profit and falls by dividends received, with one income line rather than the investee's individual revenues. Joint control requires a separate rights assessment. MIT's historical acquisition notes help explain the reporting-entity distinction; their old percentage shortcuts and presentation alternatives are not current decision rules here.

5. Acquiring control: remove the investment and identify the claims

Our original group comprises Cedar Parent (P) and Brook Subsidiary (S). At 1 January X1, P buys 75% of S's existing ordinary shares from outside owners for cash 150 kCU. S is expressly a business, not merely an asset collection. P acquires substantive control on that date through enforceable rights to direct its relevant activities, receives variable returns, and can use those rights to affect its returns. This conclusion is supplied separately from α = 3/4. There is no consolidation exception.

Identifiable acquisition-date fair values equal the balances below. No omitted intangible assets, contingent liabilities, deferred tax, transaction costs, prior stake or contingent consideration exist in this case. Dates and policies match. Land is nondepreciable; no goodwill amortization, impairment, FX, tax or other comprehensive income arises in X1. These simplifications permit a complete calculation; actual acquisition accounting requires evidence for recognition and measurement. The IFRS 3 public overview supplies the acquisition framework, not a valuation of these invented businesses.

Define N as S's identifiable net assets at acquisition, C as consideration and α as P's economic ownership. We stipulate the proportionate-NCI measurement method:

Under a full fair-value NCI alternative, use separately supplied NCI fair value F and G = C + F − N. The IFRS Foundation's dated Module 19 comparison explains this distinction and also demonstrates why later goodwill treatment cannot be carried indiscriminately across frameworks. We use it as historical educational support, not the latest complete SME standard. Our positive-goodwill benchmark excludes bargain purchases, which require reassessment rather than silently treating a negative residual as an ordinary asset.

Worked example 3 — Acquisition-date consolidation

Here N = 200 − 40 = 160. Proportionate NCI is 0.25 × 160 = 40; goodwill is 150 − 0.75 × 160 = 30. The consolidation worksheet removes S's pre-acquisition equity and P's investment, while introducing goodwill and the outside equity claim:

Dr S share capital 100; Dr S pre-acquisition RE 60; Dr Goodwill 30;

Debits and credits both total 190. The parent investment represents a claim on the same subsidiary resources that the group now includes directly. Keeping both would count those resources twice. S's old RE is part of what was purchased; it is not income earned by the new group during X1. These are worksheet adjustments, not entries erasing the subsidiary's legal share capital.

Group assets are cash 150 + 40 = 190, receivables 20, inventory 40, land 300 and goodwill 30: total 580. External liabilities are 140; parent owners' equity remains 400 and NCI is 40. Thus 580 = 140 + 400 + 40. S assets and liabilities enter at 100%, while the outside residual claim is displayed separately.

Immediately before control, the parent's reporting perimeter contains cash 300. Paying 150 and bringing in S's cash 40 gives 190. The acquisition cash outflow net of acquired cash is 110. Calling the change simply “cash down 150” while comparing a parent-only opening total to a group closing total would miss the acquired 40. Purchase cash goes to former S owners; it does not finance new operations within S.

6. Post-acquisition profit: eliminate internal activity before attributing it

All following events occur after control. Old S receivables 20, old inventory 40, both land balances and external liabilities remain unchanged. External liabilities carry no interest in this case. P first earns cash service revenue 80 and pays operating expense 50. It then buys one batch externally for cash 40 and sells that whole batch on credit to S for 60. S later sells half to outsiders for cash 100, pays other operating expense 20 and settles 45 of its payable to P. S declares and pays dividends 16; P receives 12 and outside S owners receive 4. Finally P declares and pays its own shareholders 20. The dated sequence is in the downloadable input.

P records sale 60 and cost 40; S records a purchase 60, then outside revenue 100 and cost 30 for half the batch. The remaining half carries 30 in S's separate inventory but cost the group only 20. The group has neither sold that remainder externally nor earned its embedded margin 10. Calling the transfer “a sale” does not make it an external group performance event.

Let T be the whole-batch transfer price, c its external cost and z the fraction sold outside. For nonnegative markup T−c and no impairment, the unrealized margin is U = (T−c)(1−z). Combined company cost of sales is c + zT. Remove the whole internal revenue T and its matching cost component, then restore the cost of unsold internal profit:

Group cost of sales = c + zT − T + (T−c)(1−z) = zc.

The equality proves that outsiders' sales carry their share of external cost. Eliminating only the unpaid invoice would not do this: revenue and profit elimination depend on the transaction and remaining goods, not on whether S has paid P. When z = 1 no internal profit remains in inventory; when z = 0 all markup remains unrealized. The supplied cash schedule must still be feasible at either boundary.

7. Attribution, measurement and analytical boundaries

An upstream transaction has S as seller. With no other adjustments, reduce S profit by its unrealized margin U before sharing it: NCI profit = (1−α)(S profit−U). A downstream U instead belongs entirely to parent owners. Both directions remove all U from group profit. If goods are later sold externally without further value changes, the previous deferral reverses into the appropriate later cost/profit calculation. A transfer loss may indicate a real impairment and cannot safely be handled by assuming that every loss is artificial.

In our downstream case, NCI closing equity equals 25% of S closing net assets, 0.25 × 194 = 48.5. That convenient shortcut need not survive upstream profit adjustments, acquisition fair-value differences, a different NCI measurement or other comprehensive income. Derive the rollforward for the stated case instead of memorizing a percentage times a convenient balance.

Consolidation neither guarantees parent access to cash nor turns all subsidiary obligations into direct parent contractual debt. Read separate-company disclosures and guarantees along with the group totals. Similarly, the parent's market capitalization is the price of its own traded claims, not total group book equity including NCI. Comparing those quantities without adjusting the claim definition can distort valuation ratios.

A credible review asks what establishes control, what constitutes the acquired business, how identifiable values were obtained, which interests NCI represents, what related transactions occurred and whether cash can move between entities. This chapter supplies those facts to teach the mechanics. It does not estimate acquisition synergies, certify a control assessment, value an unobserved NCI stake, or derive taxes, complex group reorganizations, ownership changes, investment-entity exceptions or impairment testing. Those extensions need additional facts and specialist guidance.

Teach-back and delayed retrieval

Teach-back. Explain to a new analyst why Cedar's group reports all Brook's external sales, eliminates a legally valid internal invoice, and then gives only part of group profit to Cedar's owners. Use the numbers 100, 15, 10 and 12.5 in a two-minute explanation.

Teach-back criteria and illustrative answer

Control defines the economic reporting perimeter, so all 100 of Brook's outside sales enter. The invoice's unpaid 15 is a valid claim in separate ledgers but vanishes against the group liability to itself. Ten of the parent's transfer margin remains in unsold goods, so it is deferred from group profit. After those adjustments, total profit is 90; outside Brook shareholders receive attribution of 12.5 and parent owners 77.5.
