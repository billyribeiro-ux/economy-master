A bank can create a deposit when it grants a loan, yet still need funding to settle the borrower's payment. A central bank can supply reserves without supplying bank capital. Monetary policy becomes easier to understand once we separate these balance-sheet operations from the interest rates and spending decisions they may influence.

Money is a claim with particular uses

Money provides a unit of account in which prices and debts are quoted, a medium of exchange accepted in payment, and a store of value for transferring purchasing power. The last function need not be performed perfectly: inflation can erode purchasing power. An asset can store value without being readily accepted at a checkout. A payment card is an instruction or credit arrangement, not an additional money balance on top of the deposit used to settle it.

Currency held by the public is distinct from commercial-bank deposits. A deposit is the customer's asset and the bank's liability. A reserve balance is the commercial bank's asset and the central bank's liability, used for settlement among eligible account holders. An ordinary household in our model cannot hold a central-bank reserve account. Paying another household normally transfers deposits and prompts settlement between their banks; it does not hand the household reserve balances.

For our closed synthetic ledger, define broad transaction money M = currency held by the nonbank public + its bank deposits. Define the monetary base H = that currency + banks' reserve balances. No vault cash, foreign money, nonbank central-bank accounts or other monetary liabilities appear in this simplified economy. Actual monetary aggregates have jurisdiction-specific holder and instrument boundaries, so our M and H are definitions for this ledger, not a claim to reproduce M1, M2 or a particular official series.

Money, credit and wealth are different objects. A newly granted loan can increase both a borrower's deposit asset and loan liability without increasing its net worth at origination. A loan can finance buying an existing asset rather than producing anything new. Conversely, issuing bank equity can finance the bank and strengthen its loss buffer without creating new customer credit. Balance-sheet identities establish which claims change; behavioral and institutional assumptions explain why.

The inspected Bank of England article by McLeay, Radia and Thomas, Money creation in the modern economy, 2014 Q1, pages 14–19, distinguishes loan-created deposits, interbank settlement and constraints on lending. The original examples below specify their own accounts and rules; the article's historical institutional description is not presented as an October 2026 operating manual.

A bounded demand for liquid balances

Let Pt > 0 be currency units per consumption good. Holding nominal transaction balances Mt gives real balances m = Mt/Pt, measured in date-t goods. Inflation over the next interval is πt+1 = Pt+1/Pt − 1, so 1+πt+1 > 0. All returns in a calculation refer to this same interval.

Consider a household with given real financial wealth w ≥ 0 at date t. It allocates m ∈ [0,w] to a liquid deposit earning nominal net rate im, and the remainder to a safe but less convenient bill earning ib. The assets mature together; neither defaults; future prices are known in this model. The deposit provides transaction convenience during the interval. Its net benefit, including balance-handling costs, is stipulated to be B(m) = vm − hm²/2 in date-(t+1) consumption-good equivalents, with v ≥ 0 and h > 0. This is a reduced-form willingness to pay for convenience, not an extra item of measured output.

The bill would turn one date-t good of wealth into (1+ib)/(1+πt+1) future goods. Putting that same amount into the deposit yields (1+im)/(1+πt+1). Their difference is the real opportunity cost per current real balance unit,

q = (ib − im)/(1+πt+1).

The full payoff is the all-bill future wealth plus B(m) − qm. The all-bill term is constant in m, so choose m to maximize (v−q)m − hm²/2 on [0,w]. Here v and q are future goods per current real balance unit; h converts squared current balance units into future goods. Suppose ib ≥ im ≥ 0 for the numerical demand cases, so q ≥ 0. The optimization itself also works for negative q if a different rate ordering is explicitly allowed.

m* = min{w, max{0, (v−q)/h}}.

To prove globality, the second derivative is −h < 0. Completing the square also writes the objective as a constant minus (h/2)[m−(v−q)/h]²; the feasible point closest to the unconstrained peak is the displayed clipped choice. Thus q ≥ v gives m* = 0, q ≤ v−hw gives m* = w, and the intervening region is interior. Equality at a boundary still yields a unique optimum when w > 0. If w = 0 the sole feasible choice is zero. In the interior, ∂m*/∂q = −1/h; at a strictly binding endpoint a small rate change need not alter the choice.

The opportunity cost is a spread, not automatically the headline policy rate. Holding w,v,h and the inflation factor fixed, equal increases in deposit and bill rates leave q and this model's m* unchanged. This conclusion uses the model's linear valuation of future wealth: a more general income effect or changed transaction needs could alter it. A rise in the price level with real wealth and real desired balances unchanged requires a proportional rise in nominal M = Pm; nominal money growth alone does not reveal increased real liquidity.

Worked example 1: the relevant spread and the price unit

Let w = 30, v = 2/25 = 0.08, h = 1/500 = 0.002, Pt = Pt+1 = 2, ib = 3/50 = 6% and im = 1/50 = 2% per teaching interval. Then q = 1/25 = 0.04 and m* = (0.08−0.04)/0.002 = 20, strictly between zero and 30. Nominal balances are M* = 2·20 = 40 currency units. The variable payoff is (0.04)20 − (0.002/2)20² = 2/5 future goods.

If only the bill rate rises to 8%, q becomes 0.06, m* = 10 and M* = 20. If instead both original rates rise by two percentage points, their spread stays four percentage points and m* stays 20. If the spread reaches eight percentage points, m* is zero. At a zero spread the unconstrained peak is 40, but wealth caps the choice at 30.

Finally double both dated price levels from two to four while holding real w and the stated real benefit function fixed. Inflation remains zero and q is unchanged. Desired real balances remain 20, while nominal balances become 80. This experiment scales nominal wealth as well; it does not silently halve the household's real resources.

Loan creation, settlement and consolidation

Every bank balance sheet satisfies assets = liabilities + equity. Equity is the residual claim that absorbs recognized asset losses before unchanged creditors bear them; it is not a segregated pile of reserves. Liquidity is the ability to make payments when due. An institution can have positive equity yet lack immediately usable settlement assets. Conversely, borrowing reserves can solve a payment shortfall while leaving losses and inadequate equity unresolved.

All entries in the next example are nominal currency units at a common instant. Existing securities and loans are initially carried at the stated values. There are no fees, interest accruals, taxes, revaluations or defaults during these transactions. Deposits belong to nonbanks; interbank claims are separately identified. The government is outside the consolidation boundary. A central-bank advance is a secured loan: the collateral remains the borrower's asset, marked as pledged, rather than also becoming an outright asset of the central bank.

Other transactions have different counterparts

Principal repayment: paying a bank loan from a deposit reduces the loan asset and a customer deposit liability. Across two banks, reserve settlement accompanies this destruction of deposits; total system reserves need not change. Interest is not principal repayment: it enters bank income and subsequent costs or distributions, so the principal-only entry must not be used for every payment.

Credit loss: recognizing that 12 of a bank's loans cannot be collected reduces its loan carrying value and equity by 12. Deposits held by other customers do not disappear merely because a loan has lost value. A bank write-down also need not legally cancel the borrower's obligation; valuation and legal forgiveness are separate matters.

Currency withdrawal: if a customer converts x of deposits into freshly delivered currency, with no change in bank vault cash, deposits fall x, bank reserves fall x and currency held by the public rises x. M is unchanged, as is H: the public and central-bank liability composition has changed. This requires the bank to obtain and deliver the cash; our entry describes the completed transaction.

Central-bank asset purchase: buying a security outright from a bank raises that bank's reserves and reduces its securities by the same purchase value; it need not create a customer deposit. Buying from a nonbank through its bank instead credits the seller's deposit and that bank's reserves, while the seller gives up the security. These are immediate entries at the transaction price, not predictions of subsequent lending or portfolio behavior. In neither case is creating reserves itself creating new bank equity. Repayment of a central-bank advance reduces the advance asset and reserve liabilities, reversing reserve creation if no other operation offsets it.

Why deposit creation is not unlimited lending

A bank must find borrowers willing and able to accept its terms, earn enough expected revenue to cover funding, operating and credit costs, meet payment needs, and satisfy its capital and other constraints. The deposit created at origination is an obligation to the customer. If it leaves the bank, the bank needs settlement funding. An available interbank or central-bank facility can supply that funding only at its price and under its access, collateral and quantity conditions.

For a transparent toy capital rule, suppose equity E must satisfy E ≥ κL, where L is the carrying value of customer loans and κ ∈ (0,1]. Reserves and the specified government securities have zero weight; all these customer loans have weight one. This is not a description of a complete regulatory capital framework. Before losses, A after its 40-unit loan has E = 20 and L = 190. With κ = 1/10, its remaining capital-based capacity for additional loans is E/κ − L = 10.

Under the earlier opening accounts, E/κ−L was 50. If all new loan proceeds leave A, its initial reserves are 30, its available interbank funding is at most 15 and it must retain a reserve buffer of five, its liquidity-based limit is 30+15−5 = 40. With fixed equity, no other operations and willing profitable borrowers, the feasible extra loan interval is therefore [0,min{50,40}] = [0,40]. Both inequalities matter. This is a global feasibility bound, not an assertion that the bank necessarily wants to choose its upper endpoint.

Following a recognized loss of 12 from the post-refinancing A balance sheet, E = 8 and L = 178. Its minimum under the toy rule is 17.8, so the shortfall is 9.8 despite positive book equity. No nonnegative extra lending makes that existing violation compliant. One must not clip negative headroom to zero and call the bank adequately capitalized. A fresh equity issue, retained earnings, a genuine loss transfer or an appropriate balance-sheet contraction changes the capital position. Borrowing another 12 of reserves raises an asset and a debt equally; it does not add 12 to E. Positive accounting equity also does not guarantee economic solvency if the remaining assets are overvalued.

A fixed relation D = R/ρ can be an implication of a deliberately restricted textbook model if reserves R are fixed, a ratio R ≥ ρD binds exactly, currency leakage and other constraints are excluded, and borrowers and banks willingly expand to the bound. Without binding equality, D ≤ R/ρ is only an upper bound in that model. It does not explain loan demand, risk, funding costs or an interest-rate-targeting central bank's response. In the reconciled example, deposits and reserves changed in different steps; multiplying a reserve injection by a fixed number would miss the transactions actually performed.

From reserve conditions to a policy rate

A policy target, an administered rate and a market rate are distinct. A central bank can announce a desired market-rate level or range. It sets rates on specified facilities or eligible balances. Market participants then trade under those outside options and the available supply of reserves. The relevant marginal funding rate affects other rates through competition, risk and expectations; a central bank does not directly set every loan contract.

Consider a deliberately frictionless one-period reserve-market model. Let iD be the return on eligible reserve holdings and iF ≥ iD the rate on an accessible central-bank lending facility, with both gross rates positive. Suppose all participating banks can use both options, collateral is sufficient for the required facility borrowing, maturities and risks are comparable, and no balance-sheet cost, stigma or market segmentation prevents arbitrage. Only those reserve-market outside options are modeled; an additional cash/storage opportunity would require checking the demand schedule again, especially at negative administered rates. Under these assumptions a bank need not lend reserves below its deposit-facility alternative, and can borrow from the central bank rather than pay more than iF.

Specify the inverse demand for reserves as

i(R) = iD + b max{R̄−R,0}, R ≥ 0, b > 0, R̄ > 0.

R is a nominal reserve stock; b has units of rate fraction per reserve unit. The additional term is a stipulated marginal liquidity-service value, declining to zero at the satiation quantity R̄. Beyond R̄, eligible holders are willing to absorb balances at iD, so supply determines their actual holdings along the flat segment. This schedule describes aggregate reserve-market conditions, not households' deposit demand from the earlier example.

Let R0 ≥ 0 be reserves supplied before use of the lending facility. To keep the marginal rate no higher than iF, total reserves must reach at least

Rc = max{0, R̄−(iF−iD)/b}.

Specify that the facility supplies the minimum amount needed to enforce the ceiling: z = max{Rc−R0,0}. Then R = R0+z and the resulting rate is

i* = min{iF, iD+b max{R̄−R0,0}}.

This solves every domain branch. Below Rc, facility supply makes up the shortfall and the rate is at the ceiling when that branch exists. Between Rc and R̄, no facility borrowing is needed and an extra reserve unit lowers the rate by b. At or above R̄, additional reserves leave the rate at the floor. The function is continuous and nonincreasing in R0, and lies in [iD,iF]. If iD = iF, the corridor collapses and the stipulated minimum-supply rule fills any shortfall to R̄; the common rate alone would not select a unique volume of costless extra borrowing and redepositing. If the ceiling is so high that Rc = 0, it never binds for positive supplied reserves. A quoted marginal rate need not imply positive trading volume at every endpoint.

This model's ceiling relies on sufficient access and collateral. It must not be combined silently with the finite pledged-collateral limit in example 2. A borrower unable to obtain more facility credit may pay above the quoted facility rate. Likewise, lenders ineligible to earn iD, or intermediaries facing costs, can trade below that rate. Actual systems can have a nearly flat rather than exactly flat reserve-demand region.

Transmission requires a sequence of conditional links

Nominal to real. A nominal contract set at t pays gross factor 1+it at t+1. Its realized purchasing-power factor is

1+rt+1 = (1+it)/(1+πt+1).

With known inflation this is the exact real factor used in the dated decisions of Chapter 4. With uncertain inflation, its conditional expected value is Et[(1+it)/(1+πt+1)], generally not (1+it)/(1+Etπt+1). The familiar it−Etπt+1 is a small-rate approximation to the expected real net return. A risky consumption Euler equation also involves the joint distribution of returns and marginal utility, not merely an arithmetic expected return.

Across contracts and maturities. A policy-rate change can change banks' funding alternatives and eventually their deposit and lending terms. Pass-through depends on contract reset dates, competition, funding mix and expected losses. Medium- and long-term required returns depend partly on expected future policy and compensation for risk; today's overnight rate is not every maturity's discount rate. Announcements can alter those expectations before implementation. If an action was already fully anticipated, its announcement or execution may contain little new information.

Asset prices and collateral. For a certain positive nominal payoff F at maturity T, discounted at a constant required yield y > −1, price Q = F/(1+y)T and ∂Q/∂y = −TF/(1+y)T+1 < 0 for T > 0. A lower required yield raises this fixed-payoff price. A policy cut need not lower every required yield or preserve every risky payoff forecast. If collateral prices rise, a borrowing rule tied to collateral can relax; if bank asset values fall, its equity buffer can contract. These channels require the relevant balance-sheet exposure and lending rule.

Spending and credit. Changed real financing costs affect the dated household and investment choices already derived. Borrowing constraints, existing fixed-rate contracts and income effects can weaken or reverse a particular household's response. The sign of a consumption or saving response is not a universal consequence of the Euler equation. Bank losses or tighter risk assessments can also make lending expensive or unavailable even after a policy-rate cut.

The ECB's inspected transmission-mechanism explanation organizes channels through market and bank rates, expectations, asset values, collateral and credit. It also emphasizes uncertain timing. We use it for this mechanism map, not for a numerical effect or a present policy setting.

Worked example 4: a funding-rate cut that does not lower the loan rate

A risk-neutral lender finances one currency unit for one interval at nominal net rate if ≥ 0 and pays operating cost c ≥ 0 at the final date. A borrower repays promised gross amount 1+iloan with probability 1−p; on default it pays only recovery λ of principal, with 0 ≤ p < 1 and 0 ≤ λ ≤ 1. Probabilities and recovery are stipulated and do not vary with the offered rate in this example. Ignore capital charges and other risk premia; suppose a willing borrower exists at the calculated quote.

The expected receipt is (1−p)(1+iloan)+pλ. Covering funding and operations requires it to be at least 1+if+c. Since 1−p > 0, the smallest break-even quote is uniquely

iloan = [1+if+c−pλ]/(1−p) − 1.

This is an exact expected-cash-flow bound, not an approximate addition of spreads. With if = 2%, c = 1%, p = 1/20 and λ = 2/5, the gross quote is (1.03−0.02)/0.95 = 101/95, so iloan = 6/95 ≈ 6.316%. Cutting funding to 1%, with risk unchanged, lowers the gross quote to 100/95 = 20/19, or net 1/19 ≈ 5.263%.

If default probability instead rises simultaneously to 1/10, the new gross quote is (1.02−0.04)/0.90 = 49/45, or net 4/45 ≈ 8.889%. Lower funding cost and worse credit risk operate in opposite directions; here the latter dominates. At p = 1 a promise of a larger success payment cannot help because success never occurs. Expected recovery λ must itself cover 1+if+c; otherwise no finite quote breaks even. When p < 1 the formula supplies a quote, but if no borrower accepts it or a capital/payment constraint binds, it does not establish a positive quantity of lending.

The example holds behavior and probabilities fixed to isolate channels. In a richer lending problem, a higher quote can change borrower selection, repayment incentives and default risk. An observed increase in loan rates after a policy cut does not alone identify which channel changed.

Bank credit, household expenditure, firms' investment and employment then interact with production, wage setting and prices. Monetary policy is often chosen in response to the same economic news that moves those outcomes. A before/after correlation therefore mixes policy transmission with the reason policy changed. The next chapter develops inflation and expectations; the subsequent stabilization models state the additional equations needed to close the spending-output-price system.

The consolidated institution has securities 160 + customer loans 330 = 490, against deposits 420 + currency 20 + equity 50 = 490. The extra securities entered from outside the consolidation boundary. The seller loses securities worth 20 and gains a deposit worth 20, so its net worth is unchanged at the given prices. There is no new customer loan and no bank equity gain in either branch. Subsequent lending or asset-price changes are separate questions.

  5. Restoring the existing portfolio requires 8+x ≥ (1/10)178 = 89/5, hence x ≥ 49/5 = 9.8. Supporting a further 20 on capital grounds requires 8+x ≥ (1/10)198 = 99/5, hence x ≥ 59/5 = 11.8. At either subscription, A receives reserves x and credits new equity x, leaving deposits and loans unchanged. Its post-loss assets rise from 203 to 203+x and equity from eight to 8+x. B debits the subscriber's deposit x and transfers reserves x: assets fall from 240 to 240−x and deposits from 220 to 220−x, with B's equity 20 unchanged. Aggregate reserves and public currency do not change; deposits fall x as the subscriber exchanges a money claim for bank equity.

    At the smaller subscription A has reserves 74/5, loans 178, securities 20 and equity 89/5; B reserves are 251/5 and deposits 1051/5. At the larger, A has reserves 84/5 and equity 99/5; B reserves are 241/5 and deposits 1041/5. These are separate recapitalizations, not cumulative ones. Buying an existing share pays its former owner and supplies no new equity to A. Borrowing reserves raises a bank asset and a debt together, leaving equity unchanged. Even after the larger issue, A has only 16.8 reserves; a further loan whose entire 20 leaves the bank would need additional settlement funding, especially if the five-unit buffer remains. Its already pledged securities do not automatically support an extra central-bank advance.

  6. Rc = 80−(0.05−0.02)/0.0005 = 20. For R0 = 10, borrow z = 10, total R = 20 and i* = 5%. At 40, z = 0, R = 40 and i* = 2% + 0.0005·40 = 4%. At 100, z = 0, R = 100 and i* = 2%. Raising both administered rates by one percentage point leaves the corridor spread and Rc unchanged; at R0 = 100 the new floor and market rate are 3%.

    With initial reserves 10 and accessible borrowing capped at five, total reserves are only 15. The stipulated inverse demand gives i = 0.02 + 0.0005(80−15) = 0.0525 = 5.25%. The unavailable extra facility borrowing prevents the arbitrage that enforced the earlier ceiling. A lender ineligible for reserve remuneration may accept a rate below that quoted remuneration rate; intermediary balance-sheet costs or other segmentation can also weaken the floor. Those cases require modifying the frictionless assumptions, not pretending the original bound still applies.

  7. The two real gross returns are 21/20 and (21/20)/(11/10) = 21/22. Their equally weighted mean is (21/20+21/22)/2 = 441/440, so the expected real net return is 1/440, approximately 0.2273%. Expected inflation is 5%, making the ratio of gross factors (21/20)/(21/20) = 1. Averaging a reciprocal and taking the reciprocal of an average differ. The small-rate approximation i−Eπ is zero here; it is not the exact expectation.

    The fixed-payoff prices are 110/(11/10) = 100 and 110/(21/20) = 2200/21 ≈ 104.762. The price rises if this asset's required yield falls while its payoff remains fixed. For the loan, expected break-even requires (9/10)(1+iloan) + (1/10)(2/5) = 1+1/100+1/100 = 51/50. Therefore 1+iloan = 49/45 and iloan = 4/45. Expected inflation, maturity-specific required yields, credit probabilities, recovery, costs and contract constraints are additional inputs. A policy cut alone fixes none of all these objects, so its effects must be traced conditionally.

  8. A complete critique begins with counterparts. Reserves could have increased because the central bank advanced funds against collateral, buying a bank debt claim, or bought securities outright. A purchase from a bank need not create a customer deposit; a purchase from a nonbank normally credits one in this ledger. A reserve increase therefore does not reveal a particular deposit or lending sequence. Interbank lending alone reallocates reserves and cannot explain a change in their aggregate stock within the stated boundary.

    Weak loan growth can reflect weak borrower demand, expected losses, low bank equity, constrained settlement or collateral access, repayment of old loans, or changed credit terms. Give at least two channels and identify which side of a bank or borrower decision they affect. For example, firms expecting poor sales may reject borrowing even at a lower quote, while an undercapitalized bank may be unable to expand despite abundant reserves. Net loan growth also subtracts repayments and recognized losses; it is not gross loan origination.

    Falling employment may have prompted the easing, may reflect an adverse shock that outweighed its effects, or may precede any substantial response. A credible evaluation needs dated policy news and expectations, reserve-operation counterparts, loan applications/originations/repayments, bank constraints and exposure, and an employment counterfactual justified by an identification design. Relevant comparison units, spillovers, anticipation and other simultaneous policies must be addressed. Employment can fall yet fall less than without easing; it can also fall with little policy benefit. The three observations establish neither that policy was ineffective nor that it succeeded. Evaluate answers for a reconciled mechanism, a specified counterfactual and concrete threats, not a preferred policy conclusion.

Teach-back

Explain a new bank loan, its payment to another bank, and central-bank refinancing using three separate sets of entries. Then explain why ample reserves can coexist with a positive policy rate and weak credit growth.

Successful explanation

Origination adds a bank loan asset and customer deposit liability. A cross-bank payment shifts deposits and reserves between banks; interbank funding creates an internal asset/liability pair without changing total reserves. A central-bank advance creates reserves against a debt owed to the central bank, not new bank equity. In a floor model with eligible remunerated reserves, administered returns supply an opportunity-cost anchor even when reserves are abundant. Credit still depends on borrowers, expected losses, funding terms, equity and other constraints. Neither balance-sheet arithmetic nor a fixed reserve multiplier establishes the spending response.

Delayed check

After several days, a bank has reserves eight, loans 72, deposits 70 and equity 10. It grants a loan of 12 whose deposit initially stays at the same bank. Then the borrower pays seven to another bank. Show this bank's accounts after each step. Under the toy rule E ≥ L/10, find remaining loan headroom. If the central bank advances five, does headroom change? Finally compute the certain real net return when nominal i = 6% and inflation is 2%.

Delayed-check answer

Initially assets 80 equal deposits 70 plus equity 10. Origination raises loans to 84 and deposits to 82, giving assets and financing of 92, with reserves still eight. The payment reduces reserves to one and deposits to 75, so assets 85 = deposits 75 + equity 10. The other bank receives seven reserves and credits seven deposits. Capital headroom is 10/(1/10)−84 = 16, a capital-only bound. A central-bank advance raises reserves to six and adds debt five: assets 90 = deposits 75 + central-bank debt five + equity 10. Headroom is still 16. The certain real gross return is (53/50)/(51/50) = 53/51, so the net return is 2/51 ≈ 3.9216%, not exactly 4%.

Reproduce and extend

The standard-library ledger program and README reproduce the original transaction stages and independent branches using exact fractions. Run python3 v06-c06-ledger.py for JSON output. The fixture reports every institution and both consolidation boundaries. Its entries are given transactions, not an estimated behavioral banking model.

More complete monetary analysis adds heterogeneous banks and nonbanks, risk-sensitive capital rules, collateral valuation, money-market frictions, risky intertemporal pricing and an equilibrium account of output and prices. The next chapter, Inflation, expectations and nominal rigidity, develops the price-setting and expectations side; accounting identities alone cannot close that system.
