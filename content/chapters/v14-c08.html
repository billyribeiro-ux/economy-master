Volume 14 · Chapter 8

Investment, debt and financial frictions

Capability. Derive an investment rule from a dated firm problem, establish how financing restrictions change its feasible set, and trace a balance-sheet shock through subsequent investment without losing the creditor’s side of the accounts. Five worked cases and twelve solved exercises separate adjustment costs, hidden effort, limited pledgeability, old-debt incentives and dynamic net-worth propagation.

Prerequisites. Dynamic optimization, concavity and equilibrium accounting from Chapters 1–3; fiscal/default contract timing from Chapter 6; stochastic discounting from Chapter 7; agency and contract incentives from Volume 13. All figures are original synthetic examples. The chapter proves finite models with explicit restrictions; it does not present a fitted general-equilibrium business-cycle model.

A profitable machine may not be installed. The firm may face costly adjustment, insufficient pledgeable revenue or an old creditor who captures its gains. Those explanations differ: a transfer of wealth can repair one constraint while leaving another intact. Begin with the frictionless investment benchmark, then identify exactly which assumption changes.

1. Marginal q is a dated shadow value

Dates are t=0,…,T. Initial physical capital K0≥0 is given. At each t<T the firm receives operating profit πt(Kt), invests It∈[0,Ī] at unit purchase price, and pays adjustment cost Φt(It). Capital becomes Kt+1=(1−δ)Kt+It, with δ∈[0,1]. Terminal salvage is S(KT). Let π and S be continuous concave functions and Φ continuous convex. All payoffs are real goods. A constant gross discount return R>0 gives β=1/R in this finite problem; it need not be a household preference parameter.

max Σt=0T−1βt[πt(Kt)−It−Φt(It)]+βTS(KT).

The finite product of investment intervals is compact. The capital sequence is a continuous affine function of investments, so a maximum exists. The objective is concave in the entire investment vector. This is an unconstrained-finance benchmark: owners can fund the stated investment costs, and financing does not itself change the objective or feasible set. The upper bound Ī is a physical compactness restriction, not a disguised credit constraint.

At a differentiable optimum define qt as the value, in date-t goods, of one extra installed unit of Kt+1 while subsequent choices are optimized. A backward costate calculation gives

qT−1=βS′(KT),   qt−1=β[π′t(Kt)+(1−δ)qt],   t=1,…,T−1.

The first unit is installed next period, so its shadow value already discounts the next-period payoff. At an interior investment, 1+Φ′t(It)=qt. At I=0, q≤1+Φ′(0); at I=Ī, q≥1+Φ′(Ī). These signs follow from the permitted directional change in investment. A large marginal q can coexist with low investment when adjustment costs or a binding cap make expansion costly.

Why these conditions suffice. For a candidate feasible path, concavity bounds any objective improvement by its tangent in changes ΔK and ΔI. Substitute ΔKt+1=(1−δ)ΔKt+ΔIt, with ΔK0=0. Backward use of the terminal and costate equations cancels every capital term, leaving Σβt[qt−1−Φ′t(It)]ΔIt. At an interior optimum each coefficient is zero. At a lower bound it is nonpositive and ΔI≥0; at an upper bound it is nonnegative and ΔI≤0. Thus the tangent is at most zero for every feasible path, proving global optimality. Strict convex adjustment costs make the objective strictly concave in investment and give a unique investment vector. Kinks require supporting subgradients or direct concave optimization, not an invented derivative.

With uncertainty and a valid positive shareholder SDF m, the shadow condition becomes qt=Et[mt+1(π′t+1+(1−δ)qt+1)], with the appropriate terminal salvage derivative. This is a valuation condition under stated shareholder preferences and feasible trading. It does not make a constant discount rate valid for arbitrary risky profits. Capital-dependent adjustment costs would add their derivative to the costate; the simpler Φ(I) assumption here is explicit.

Average Q divides the value of a specified installed-capital claim by its replacement cost. Marginal q differentiates that value with respect to another unit. Equality requires suitable homogeneity and price-taking assumptions. Fixed rents, market power, nonlinear technology, adjustment costs that are not homogeneous, growth options or a mismatch between the measured firm value and capital can break it. A high market-to-book ratio is not automatically the marginal return to a particular investment.

Worked case 1: Solve investment and distinguish average value

Take one investment date with K0=1,δ=0.1,R=1.05,π0=0,Ī=2, Φ(I)=I² and S(K)=3K−K²/2. The objective is −I−I²+S(0.9+I)/1.05. The interior derivative condition gives 1+2I=(3−0.9−I)/1.05, so I*=21/62, about0.33870968. Capital is 192/155 and marginal q=52/31, about1.67741935. The second derivative is −2−1/1.05<0, and the solution lies between zero and two, certifying the global optimum.

The value of the terminal installed-capital claim, excluding the already paid investment/adjustment costs, is S(K)/R. Its value per installed unit is (3−K/2)/R=492/217, about2.26728111. It exceeds the marginal value (3−K)/R because S is strictly concave. The numerator is specified deliberately: adding cash or subtracting outstanding debt would measure a different claim. If an investment ceiling were0.2, the same concavity proof selects I=0.2 with positive marginal incentive to expand.

The inspected investment section of Malmendier’s 2007 Berkeley lecture develops adjustment-cost q theory and the restrictive average-versus-marginal distinction. The finite proof and numerical economy above are original and retain an explicit terminal payoff.

2. A positive project value need not be pledgeable

Consider a two-date indivisible project costing I at date0 and yielding Y>0 on success and zero on failure at date1. A risk-neutral entrepreneur has initial liquid wealth n≤I. Competitive lenders require gross expected return R. High effort gives success probability pH; shirking gives pL<pH plus private consumption-equivalent benefit B>0. Effort is hidden, outcomes are observable and contracts can condition only on success/failure. Limited liability prevents negative payments by an entrepreneur with no other pledgeable assets. There are no failure-state resources and no outside subsidy, so a feasible contract pays the entrepreneur y∈[0,Y] on success and zero on failure.

High effort is incentive compatible exactly when (pH−pL)y≥B. Thus an implementing contract must leave at least y̲=B/(pH−pL) to the entrepreneur. If y̲>Y, high effort cannot be induced in this contract class. Otherwise the maximum expected revenue pledgeable to lenders is pH(Y−y̲), and break-even requires

R(I−n)≤pH[Y−B/(pH−pL)].

This is a financing condition derived from incentives and limited liability. It does not follow merely from pHY>RI, which says the high-effort project has positive total surplus at the opportunity cost R. The entrepreneur must also prefer investing to retaining n at gross return R. At a lender break-even contract, expected entrepreneurial payoff is pHY−R(I−n); participation becomes pHY≥RI. Hence positive total surplus plus pledgeability suffice for high-effort financing in this precisely restricted two-outcome model. At an incentive equality, select high effort among tied actions, or require a strict inequality if robust strict implementation is desired.

The proof covers the stated outcome-contingent contract class: failure cannot pay without resources; reducing the entrepreneur’s success payment below y̲ violates incentives, while any larger payment reduces pledgeability. Monitoring, collateral, reputation, multiple financiers or richer signals change that class and require a new derivation. A loan interest quote is an expected-payoff calculation, not necessarily a safe contractual return; success-state repayment can exceed the lender’s required expected return because failure pays zero.

Worked case 2: Derive the minimum internal contribution

Let I=100,Y=180,pH=0.8,pL=0.4,B=20,R=1.1. The minimum success reward is 50, leaving expected pledgeable revenue 104. Therefore n must be at least 60/11, approximately5.45454545. High-effort total surplus is0.8×180−1.1×100=34.

3. Solve a collateral-constrained investment problem completely

Use a separate divisible project to isolate limited pledgeability. A firm has date0 cash n≥0, invests I≥0, and produces F(I)=A√I at date1, with A>0. The gross safe return is one. It can save unused cash or borrow B≥0. Only a fraction θ∈[0,1) of project output is enforceably pledgeable; the loan must obey B≤θF(I). There is no old debt. Loan proceeds plus initial cash finance investment, with unused cash carried at return one. A feasible I therefore satisfies I≤n+θA√I; for I≤n the firm self-finances and saves n−I. Its total terminal wealth in either case is n+F(I)−I.

Set z=√I≥0. The financing inequality is z²−θAz−n≤0, so the entire feasible set is the compact interval [0,Icap], where

Icap=[(θA+√(θ²A²+4n))/2]²,   I* = min{A²/4,Icap}.

To prove the optimum, F(I)−I rises until F′=A/(2√I)=1, then falls strictly. Continuity handles I=0; strict concavity on positive I and the monotone derivative give the unique maximum except the trivial zero feasible set. The output-based loan restriction is not an assumption that the firm can borrow against its whole going-concern value. It permits only the specified enforceable fraction of realized output in this deterministic economy.

Unconstrained investment is feasible when n≥ncrit=A²(1/4−θ/2). If θ≥1/2 this threshold is nonpositive and all n≥0 can finance I=A²/4. For θ<1/2 and a binding positive cap, implicit differentiation gives dI/dn=1/[1−θF′(I)]>0. The positive root has 1−θF′(I)>0 whenever n>0; the zero-cash/zero-pledge case must be treated directly. Since F′>1 in a genuinely constrained optimum, the marginal value of extra cash exceeds its direct saved value one.

For the objective F(I)−I and constraint n+θF(I)−I≥0, the multiplier is μ=(F′−1)/(1−θF′)≥0 when the constraint binds. It equals the marginal gain in project surplus from extra n. Total terminal wealth has derivative1+μ, because the new cash itself also belongs to the firm. Counting μ as the entire value of a transfer would omit that direct unit; counting1+μ as pure social surplus would count the transferred resource as free.

Worked case 3: Cash relaxes a real restriction

Set A=4,θ=0.2,n=0.2. The positive square-root solution is z=(0.8+1.2)/2=1, so constrained investment is 1, output4 and borrowing 0.8. Pledgeable output0.2×4=0.8 exactly repays the loan. Terminal entrepreneurial wealth is 3.2. The unconstrained investment would be4, with project surplus4 rather than3.

At this point F′=2, dI/dn=5/3 and μ=5/3. A marginal increase in internal funds raises investment by more than one because the extra investment expands pledgeable output. It does not create output without resources: lenders supply the additional loan and receive its repayment. Increasing n to1.28 gives I=2.56, output6.4, loan1.28 and terminal entrepreneurial wealth5.12. The constraint finally ceases to bind at n=2.4.

Holding A and θ fixed, extra n changes investment only while constrained. If n≥2.4, the optimal I remains4 and additional cash is saved or distributed. A positive empirical cash-flow/investment correlation need not identify this effect because cash flow may also reveal a changed A or investment opportunity.

4. Existing debt changes who receives a new project’s benefit

Let old assets deliver X at date1, old senior debt have face D, and a new project cost J at date0 and add deterministic payoff Z at date1. Use gross return one, no bankruptcy costs and no other assets. If existing equity must supply J and cannot renegotiate debt, its incremental payoff is

ΔE=max{X+Z−D,0}−max{X−D,0}−J.

Total project surplus is Z−J. Their difference is the old creditor’s incremental recovery, min{D,X+Z}−min{D,X}, which is nonnegative for Z≥0. Therefore a positive-surplus project can have negative incremental value to the party required to fund it. This is debt overhang: the preexisting claim captures some of the new project’s benefits. It is not the same mechanism as a technological installation cost or a hidden-effort rent.

Priority, debt renegotiation or a transfer can change the division. A proposal must still give creditors their reservation recovery, induce the investor to participate and respect total resources. A debt write-down is a change in claims; it does not physically manufacture the capital needed for J. If dispersed creditors cannot coordinate, legal priority prevents the proposed contract or new effort is needed, a seemingly feasible aggregate bargain may not be implementable.

Worked case 4: Find the bargaining interval

Take X=80,D=100,J=15,Z=25. Without investment, creditors receive80 and equity receives0. With equity-financed investment under the old face amount, terminal resources105 pay creditors100 and equity5; equity’s net incremental payoff is -10. It rejects a project with total surplus 10.

If creditors agree to a new face D′=85 before equity invests, they receive85, five above their no-project recovery. Equity receives20 and pays15, also gaining5. Both participate strictly. The entire weakly acceptable interval is (80,90), with endpoints included: creditors require D′≥80 and equity requires105−D′−15≥0. Strict improvement for both requires80<D′<90. The original face100 lies outside that interval.

If instead a new senior lender advances15 and is repaid15, the remaining90 all goes to old creditors; equity still receives zero. Senior finance can fund the positive-surplus project but does not create a strict residual incentive for an owner required to supply noncontractible effort. The absence of such effort in this deterministic example is an assumption, not a general solution to agency problems.

5. An exact finite balance-sheet propagation economy

To make persistence explicit, consider three consecutive production rounds, t=0,1,2. There is one entrepreneurial sector with initial cash n0>0. In each round, cash n plus a loan B≥0 finance I=n+B. A contract restriction caps advances at B≤λI, with0≤λ<1. Productive investment fully depreciates after producing AtI next date. Safe lenders require gross R>0. Assume productivity has a finite positive support and every possible At>R, so repayment is feasible and entrepreneurial net output is positive. This is a quantity constraint on contracts, not an endogenous external-finance risk premium.

After production the entrepreneur pays RB to lenders and has E=AtI−RB. At nonterminal dates a stipulated fraction s∈(0,1) of E is retained as next cash and the remaining fraction1−s is paid to the owner. At the last date all E is paid out. The retention rule is an explicit institutional primitive; it is not claimed to solve an unconstrained consumption-saving problem. The risk-neutral owner maximizes expected discounted dividends subject to this rule, and all discount weights are positive.

Optimality by finite backward induction. At the final production round the owner’s cash payoff is E=Rn+(A−R)I, increasing in I because A>R. The financing restriction gives I≤n/(1−λ), so its optimal investment is the cap. At an earlier date, the current dividend plus a positive linear continuation value of retained cash is a positive coefficient times E. The same monotonicity therefore selects the cap. Induction proves optimality at every history, including uncertain productivity so long as the maintained positive-return condition holds. The value is linear in n under this fixed contract/retention regime.

It=nt/(1−λ),   Bt=λnt/(1−λ),   Et+1=[(At−Rλ)/(1−λ)]nt,   nt+1=sEt+1.

The last cash equation applies only before liquidation. A productivity surprise at fixed chosen investment changes next cash by sIΔA. Leverage raises exposure relative to the same initial cash with λ=0. Even if productivity returns immediately to normal, the changed cash changes the next cap. Under constant later A the cash difference is multiplied each subsequent retained round by g=s(A−Rλ)/(1−λ). It decays when0<g<1, stays constant atg=1 and grows when g>1. This finite identity does not assert an infinite stationary distribution when g≥1.

Where do the loans and goods come from? Give outside lenders initial wealth W large enough that Wt≥Bt at every date and history in the specified finite productivity tree. They can invest unloaned funds in a linear outside technology returning R. Their next wealth is R(W−B)+RB=RW. Total productive goods are AI+R(W−B). Entrepreneurial cash after debt E plus lender wealth RW equals exactly that total. Entrepreneurial dividends and retained cash split E; neither is added again to output. Choose a finite horizon and sufficiently large W to satisfy all loan demands. This closes funding and resource accounts for the example. It does not clear an endogenous economy-wide interest rate or labor market.

When many borrowers face such restrictions, their net-worth losses can reduce aggregate investment; endogenous asset prices, wages, lender capital and policy can reinforce or offset the effect. A full general-equilibrium financial accelerator must solve those prices and resource constraints together. Our exact small economy establishes propagation from the financing cap and retained cash while holding the outside return fixed. Bernanke’s 2007 research overview explains the related external-finance-premium channel, where borrower balance sheets change financing costs. A quantity cap and a varying premium are distinct specifications of financial frictions.

6. Identify the mechanism before designing a policy

A firm’s investment and cash flow can co-move because cash relaxes a financing constraint, because both respond to productivity, or because accounting cash flow proxies expected demand. An investment regression with average Q does not automatically control for marginal investment opportunities, especially with measurement error or intangible capital. A credible research design must isolate a financing change whose direct effects on product demand, productivity and owner wealth are understood.

Specify the timing of the proposed shock, exposed firms and comparison group before inspecting outcomes. Study loans, prices, investment and payouts jointly: an internal-cash gain might repay old debt or increase dividends instead of installing capital. Check anticipation, borrower selection, changing lender composition and competing demand shocks. A bank-level funding event may also affect local demand; a collateral-price change may reflect expected firm profits. Instrument validity needs an economic exclusion argument, not just a large first-stage statistic.

Policy evaluation requires the counterpart’s accounts. A guarantee can relax lenders’ loss exposure while transferring risk to its guarantor; a grant transfers resources from taxpayers or other spending; debt restructuring redistributes recovery and may change future incentives. Compute the counterfactual allocation and financing on both sides. Higher measured investment is not automatically a welfare gain if it funds negative-surplus activity, shifts losses or reduces more valuable alternative uses of scarce resources.

Complete solutions and evaluation criteria

1. Dated costates

An extra terminal unit contributes βS′ to qT−1. At an earlier capital date t it supplies π′t now and1−δ units of the subsequent installed-capital claim; discounting to t−1 gives qt−1=β[π′t+(1−δ)qt]. The objective derivative with respect to It is βt[qt−1−Φ′]. An interior derivative is zero, a lower-bound derivative is nonpositive and an upper-bound derivative nonnegative. Concavity bounds the full gain by the tangent. Replace every ΔKt+1 by(1−δ)ΔKt+ΔIt; the stated recurrences cancel terminal and interior capital terms, leaving only those signed investment products, each at most zero. With ΔK0=0 there is no inherited-capital term. Omitting salvage instead sets its marginal value to zero and discards a real payoff; a finite horizon does not justify that omission.

2. Changed profitability and a cap

The derivative equation is1+2I=(2−0.9−I)/1.05, giving I=1/62, K=142/155 and q=32/31. Concavity proves global optimality. With Ī=0.01, the optimum is0.01 because the unconstrained optimum is larger. At K=0.91, date0 marginal q=(2−0.91)/1.05=109/105, exceeding1+2(0.01)=1.02. The positive derivative is consistent with the upper bound; it is not a first-order-condition failure.

3. Average versus marginal

The average discounted terminal-capital value is(a−bK/2)/R; the marginal value is(a−bK)/R. Their difference is bK/(2R)>0. A measured equity ratio subtracts debt and may include cash, rents, intangible assets and measurement differences absent from either numerator. A differentiable degree-one homogeneous capital-value function with a single scalable capital argument obeys V(K)=V′(K)K, so V/K=V′. Price-taking, constant-returns operating technology, appropriately homogeneous adjustment costs and a consistently defined scalable value claim can supply that structure. The quadratic terminal function in case1 is not homogeneous of degree one, so the equality cannot be invoked there.

The success reward floor is24/0.4=60. Pledgeable expected revenue is0.8(180−60)=96, so n≥100−96/1.1=140/11, about12.72727273. Reducing repayment enough to leave60 gives lenders at most96, violating their99 requirement. No contract in the specified class satisfies both constraints at n=10.

6. A different pledge fraction

Writing z=√I turns feasibility into z²−θAz−n≤0. The negative root is nonpositive and the positive root bounds z, giving[0,Icap]. The derivative A/(2√I)−1 crosses zero once at A²/4; clipping it to this interval proves the optimum. Here θA=1 and n=3/4, so z=(1+√4)/2=3/2 and I=9/4. Borrowing is I−n=3/2; output6 and wealth6−1.5=9/2. The cap binds since pledgeable output is1.5 and unconstrained I=4. The threshold is4−(1/4)×4×2=2. 7. Cash and resources

At I=1, F′=2 and θF′=0.4. Differentiating I=n+θF(I) gives In=1/(1−0.4)=5/3. Project-surplus derivative is(F′−1)In=5/3, equal to μ. Total terminal wealth is n+F(I)−I and has derivative8/3. Any tax distortion, implementation cost, displaced opportunity or other affected agent must also be included in a broader welfare account. Once unconstrained, In=0, μ=0 and extra cash raises private terminal wealth by exactly one; it creates no additional project surplus.

9. Finite dynamic optimality

At the final round E=Rn+(A−R)I increases strictly in I. Earlier, future value is a positive linear function of retained sE, so the sum of current dividends and discounted continuation is again a positive multiple of E. This induction establishes I=n/(1−λ) for every history with the stated primitives. At s=1/2 the cash multiplier is(1/2)(1.2−0.5)/0.5=0.7. Starting from1, the next two retained cash values are(0.7,0.49). A cash gap shrinks by0.7 each nonterminal retained round. The original s=3/4 gives multiplier1.05, so its gap grows in levels. Neither result describes cash after the final liquidation, when the retention rule stops.

At λ=3/4, I=1/(1−3/4)=4 and B=3, below outside wealth10. Productive output is4.8, repayment3 and entrepreneurial cash1.8; next retained cash is1.35. Repayment is safe because the maintained A>R implies AI>RλI for every allowed state. Near λ=1 the formal investment cap diverges and eventually exceeds available lender funding, violating W≥B; technological scale limits, price changes or different default risk can also invalidate the maintained model. The finite example is conditional on those restrictions, not a theorem permitting limitless safe leverage.

12. A ten-point referee protocol

Investment opportunities,2points: distinguish marginal q from a noisy average-Q proxy and use a stated demand/profitability measurement strategy; do not call cash flow itself an excluded financing shock. Identification,2points: specify an externally timed financing exposure and comparison group, test anticipation and explain why direct demand/productivity channels do not drive the result; identify a plausible violation. Mechanism,2points: examine loan quantities or terms plus payouts/debt repayment, alongside investment, so the claim can distinguish constraint relief from cash redistribution. Resource and risk account,2points: value expected guarantee payments, funding and distortions, creditor recoveries and displaced lending or public uses; a guarantee is a contingent claim supplied by someone. Refutation,2points: predeclare that no financing response among exposed firms, gains only in payouts rather than constrained investment, adverse selection, or fiscal losses exceeding supported project benefits would count against the proposed mechanism or welfare claim. Equivalent coherent designs earn credit; a correlation plus a presumption that all investment is beneficial does not.

8. Teach-back and delayed retention

Teach-back. Explain why a high marginal q, positive project NPV and sufficient total future output are three different statements. A complete answer dates q as an installed-capital marginal value, defines total surplus at the proper discount rate and separates pledgeable revenue from revenue that must remain with an entrepreneur for incentives. Then identify who loses or gains when old debt is restructured.
